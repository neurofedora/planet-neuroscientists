    Another Sydney investigation of astrophysicist leads to retractions

    The University of Sydney has once again investigated the work of a former research fellow in its physics department, this time resulting in the retraction of two papers over concerns that data in several figures were likely fabricated. 

    In September, the Journal of Geophysical Research: Space Physics retracted two articles by Joachim Schmidt, who was at the University of Sydney from 2010 to 2021. 

    As Retraction Watch reported in 2023, the university previously investigated work from Schmidt and his supervising professor, Iver Cairns. Researchers at the University of Michigan had raised concerns about a figure in a draft manuscript supposedly created using a simulation model developed by the Michigan team. Sydney’s investigation found Schmidt had used Adobe Photoshop to create or alter the images, which could not be reproduced by digital forensic experts or by Schimdt himself. 

    That investigation determined Schmidt alone was responsible for the fabrication and falsification. 

    The university’s latest inquiry came about after JGR: Space Physics told Cairns, Schmidt and Vasili Lobzin, a space scientist at Australia’s Bureau of Meteorology, an “anonymous concern” had been brought to the journal in 2024, according to the retraction notices. 

    A news article about Schmidt and Cairns’ work in the Australian magazine The Monthly reported that it had alerted the American Geophysical Union, which publishes JGR: Space Physics, in June 2024 to concerns about two papers the pair had written.

    A spokesperson for AGU declined to comment on whether The Monthly was the source of the anonymous complaint. After the journal notified the article’s authors about the concerns, Cairns notified the university in early 2025.

    “Last year Professor Cairns informed our Research Integrity team of his concerns related to a number of publications he had co-authored with a former senior postdoctoral fellow, and we commenced a new investigation,” a University of Sydney spokesperson told Retraction Watch.

    The papers, published in 2013 and 2014, examined solar radio bursts using simulations of coronal mass ejections. At issue was the authors’ use of a computer program called BATS-R-US to generate images from papers they published between 2013 and 2016 — the same software in question in the 2023 inquiry. 

    To investigate, the university granted Cairns access to the computer files Schmidt left behind. Cairns found evidence of experimental work, but also found some images were incorrect. The notices identified several figures showing theoretical radio emissions from solar events, but “were not constructed using outputs from the simulations but instead were likely fabricated,” both retraction notices state.

    Another paper by Schmidt and Cairns published in 2016 was retracted in April for the same reason.

    The articles have been cited 22 times each, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science.

    Cairns declined to comment, citing the ongoing investigation. Schmidt did not respond to a request for comments. 

    Neither investigation found evidence of wrongdoing on the part of Cairns, according to the University of Sydney spokesperson.

    Journal updates retraction notice as Princeton student author stays in the spotlight

    A Wiley journal has corrected a retraction notice to clarify the author, a Princeton student, never conducted the study in the first place, as the student has gained publicity for visits to the offices of Texas Congressional delegation.

    The paper, by Rishika Porandla, purportedly described a new way to break down polyvinyl chloride plastic by mixing it with gold nanoparticles and blasting it with an electron beam. Macromolecular Materials and Engineering retracted the study this May after Porandla, an astrophysics student at Princeton who completed the study while in high school, notified editors of methodological errors in the work. 

    But following an Aug.10 Retraction Watch story highlighting Porandla’s decision to retract her own paper after she contacted us about her initiative to “correct the scientific record,” commenters on our site and on PubPeer raised doubts about whether the experiments described in the study were genuine. Porandla later told us she never conducted the experiments. 

    On Oct. 19, the journal added a correction to the retraction, clarifying that “most of the physical experimentation described in the original article did not take place” and that “conditions, data, and conclusions reported in the article were not tested or obtained experimentally.” 

    Porandla, who is currently on leave from Princeton’s department of astrophysical sciences, declined to comment for this story. She previously told us she contacted Wiley in August to explain the experiments did not occur.   

    “The beam experiment described in the paper was a proposed experimental workflow with associated calculated results,” she told us. “Public confusion led me to recognize and regret that this is not clear in the retracted publication, and I have included detailed clarification in an updated retraction statement to Wiley.”

    The correction states the journal’s editor, publisher and author agreed to the erratum “to correct the retraction rationale in the May 2026 version of the retraction, which was based on errors that took place during a physical lab experiment.” 

    Wiley did not respond to an inquiry about the correction by our deadline. David Huesmann, editor-in-chief of Macromolecular Materials and Engineering, previously told us Porandla’s efforts to identify and correct her mistakes showed “an honest commitment to scientific integrity.”  

    Since her leave from Princeton, Porandla has remained in the spotlight, recently meeting with Republican Congresswoman Beth Van Duyne from Texas’ 24th District to discuss American leadership in scientific research, according to a post on X by Van Duyne that has since been taken down. Van Duyne wrote that Porandla “shared her work advocating for the continuation of American leadership in scientific research and innovation” and “how policymakers can support STEM workforce development.” 

    The post drew many racist comments about Porandla and Van Duyne’s interaction with the student, the Times of India reported. 

    Van Duyne’s office did not return a message seeking clarification about the post. 

    In an Oct. 9 LinkedIn post, Porandla noted she has become “more involved in science policy and advocacy” from “meeting with congressional offices on Capitol Hill to organizing nationwide advocacy initiatives.” This includes being selected as a “research-to-policy implementation intern with Penn State’s EIC Collaborative” and traveling to Washington to “discuss federal science funding” and proposed Office of Management and Budgement changes to research grant regulations, according to her post.

    Thousands of research studies violate journal policies on collaborations between editors and authors

    Over 10,000 peer-reviewed papers from two publishers breach journal policies about past collaborations between handling editors and authors, a new analysis has found. 

    The work — presented October 15 at the 26th ACM/IEEE Joint Conference on Digital Libraries in Frisco, Texas  — examined how often research studies published by journals run by the Public Library of Science and MPDI violate the publishers’ policies covering conflicts of interest. 

    PLOS requires that a handling editor of a manuscript and its authors haven’t coauthored papers together in the previous five years, while MDPI’s limit is three years. 

    To mine those relationships, Jules Di Scala, a computer scientist at the University of Toulouse in France, and his colleagues developed a tool called EditorCheck. It scans metadata files of published papers to collect the names of handling editors of each manuscript. It then searches the database Dimensions for any papers on which the handling editor and author were coauthors. 

    They used the tool to examine more than 340,000 papers published in PLOS journals and more than 172,000 published by MDPI journals. More than 8,000 papers published by PLOS journals violate the publisher’s policy on author-editor relationships, as well as around 2,500 papers published by MDPI titles, the researchers found. 

    EditorCheck identifies articles for which the policy has been breached, but journals should look further into cases that are the most problematic, Di Scala told Retraction Watch. “In the end, we don’t know if the review process was honest or not or not,” he said. 

    For example, for large-scale collaborations with thousands of coauthors, Di Scala said, it’s not easy to decipher what the past working relationship between an author and handling editor might have been. 

    The ethics team at PLOS helped with benchmarking for EditorCheck by annotating some of the collaborations flagged in the results, the study authors wrote. Di Scala said when he and his colleagues reached out to PLOS, they had already taken action by reassigning some of the articles they flagged to different handling editors, or adding another editor to supervise the editing process. As a result of the study’s findings, PLOS’ publication ethics team took a closer look at around 100 papers that the analysis flagged, Di Scala said. 

    PLOS did not reply to our request for comment on the study by press time. 

    Jamie Barclay, head of executive affairs at MPDI in Basel, Switzerland, told Retraction Watch: “We would be very interested in reviewing the database behind the study. If we then identify any cases in which MDPI’s policies have been breached, we will investigate, as is our standard policy.” 

    Di Scala said he and his colleagues plan to further refine EditorCheck to flag papers with handling editors and authors who are based at the same institution. 

    EditorCheck is “very simple” yet “super useful,” said Florian Naudet, a psychiatrist and metascience researcher at the University of Rennes in France, who was not involved with the new study. He suggested publishers should implement the tool in their pre-publication checks and workflows. Conflicts of interest have traditionally been considered when money is involved, he said, but tracking past collaborations is important because publications are “the currency for academics and editors are in charge of allocating this currency.”

    Naudet is involved with the Open Editors Plus, a database that documents the positions of nearly 750,000 editors in positions at scholarly journals. That dataset is used largely to track editors publishing excessive numbers of papers in their own journals, Naudet said. 

    He said journals should at least label studies with disclaimers documenting past collaborations and let readers decide if those amount to conflicts of interest. In some cases that are more serious, publishers may choose to retract studies instead, he said. Last month, for instance, an Elsevier journal pulled 165 papers, dozens of which were handled by associate editors with significant past records of collaborations with authors. 

    Vitali Mindel, a business scholar at Virginia Tech University in Blacksburg, who has reported on conflicts of interest in academic publishing, told us the study authors do a good job of exposing potential conflicts of interests between handling editors and authors. 

    But the study should have also explored reciprocal handling, where two editors handle one another’s papers, either at the same time or in turns, submitted to the same or different journals, Mindel said. “Their data does allow for that sort of examination,” he said. 

    The researchers plan to study datasets from other publishers who release the names of handling editors in their metadata files, Di Scala said. While many publishers do not release such data, they should do so for transparency, he said. 

    Mindel agreed, arguing that sharing handling editors’ identities should be mandated across all publishers. “When we do audits for those journals where they do disclose editors, we realize the shenanigans happen behind the scenes,” he said. “We can only imagine what happens in journals which do not disclose editors, which are protected by this anonymity.”
