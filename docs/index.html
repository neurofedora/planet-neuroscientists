    ‘Springer’ agency website offering authorship for sale goes down following Retraction Watch story

    The website for Springer Journals Online, an agency offering authorship slots in articles in exchange for payment, has gone offline, four days after Retraction Watch published its investigation into its activities.

    As we reported on Monday, the agency was soliciting researchers offering services that included securing authorship in research articles intended for journals published by Elsevier, Springer Nature, Taylor & Francis, Wiley and SAGE, among others. 

    Springer Journals Online did not immediately respond to a request for comment by email asking why the website was unavailable, if it had halted operations, or if it wished to address our previous story. No one answered calls made via WhatsApp.

    The agency uses an obviously similar name to Springer Nature, one of the world’s largest academic publishers. A spokesperson for Springer Nature told Retraction Watch that the publisher had no connection to the agency and said they were “working with the relevant authorities to address this issue.”

    There was an update to the “springerjournalsonline.net” domain dated October 16, but it’s not clear from the domain record what that update was or if it’s responsible for the website outage. Retraction Watch could not determine if the agency has completely ceased operating or if it is continuing operations through other channels.

    In August, Richard Fateman, a retired computer scientist from the University of California, Berkeley, received an email offering authorship in an article in exchange for a share of the publication costs. The offer prompted an investigation into the agency’s operations and their use of established publishers’ and researchers’ names to solicit researchers.

    Journal retracts, republishes paper on Long COVID treatment trial

    Annals of Internal Medicine has retracted a paper on treatments for Long COVID because the authors did not report dosing information for metformin, one of the drugs tested in the study. The editor-in-chief of the journal called the exclusion “sloppy” and “inaccurate.” 

    The same day the journal retracted the paper, it published a new version addressing the exclusion. But one researcher told Retraction Watch the replacement still leaves unanswered questions. 

    Published in March, the clinical trial found the antidepressant fluvoxamine – marketed as Luvox – reduced fatigue and metformin showed no significant benefit for patients with long-term fatigue and other chronic symptoms following COVID illness. Other studies have found fluvoxamine helped reduce depression in patients with Long COVID, and researchers have found starting metformin within three days of being diagnosed with COVID-19 can help prevent long-term effects of the disease. 

    During the trial, doctors reduced the twice-a-day metformin dose from 750 mg to 500 mg because patients were experiencing side effects including gastrointestinal problems, and 9% of patients in that group had dropped out. In their analysis, the authors pooled the two groups because they considered the doses to be “equivalent,” senior author Edward Mills told Retraction Watch. 

    “Someone didn’t like that and wrote to the editor demanding we keep them separate, so we did the new analysis that has them as two different groups,” Mills said. “It makes no difference to the results or interpretation.”

    Carolyn Bramante, a metabolic health and COVID-19 researcher at the University of Minnesota, told us both the retracted and replacement papers had different inclusion and exclusion criteria for the fluvoxamine, placebo and metformin arms. “Having the same inclusion/exclusion criteria is a fundamental, basic requirement of randomization,” she said.

    Bramante has studied the use of metformin in people with acute COVID-19 and has advocated for patients with the condition to take the drug to protect against Long COVID.

    Bramante said she also was concerned with the high number of adverse events reported in the paper. She pointed to another article published in March in Annals of Internal Medicine led by So Yun Lim, a researcher at the University of Ulsan College of Medicine in Seoul, which found few adverse events when testing the efficacy of 14 days of metformin in Long COVID patients. Conversely, nearly half of patients in Mills’ study who took metformin reported adverse events.

    Lim’s study had “values you would expect” for adverse events, she said. “You typically don’t expect trials to have such different findings.”

    Christine Laine, the journal’s editor-in-chief, told us Annals of Internal Medicine decided to retract the paper “because the authors did not report the changes in metformin dosing that occurred during the conduct of the trial.” 

    In the new version of the paper, the authors included the reduction in the dose of metformin and an explanation of why that arm of the study was stopped for futility, both of which were recommended by the trial’s data and safety monitoring committee. 

    The retracted article concluded metformin showed no benefit to patients with Long COVID, but the new version states the metformin findings were inconclusive. 

    Several of the same authors, including Mills, a part-time professor of health research methods at McMaster University in Hamilton, Ontario, lost a similar paper on acute COVID-19 from The Lancet for concerns with the metformin arm of an earlier version of the REVIVE-TOGETHER trial. Both the retracted paper and replacement in Annals cite the retracted Lancet paper. 

    “This isn’t really a retraction but rather a republication as I think formatting the manuscript would have been difficult or impossible,” Mills told us in an email. 

    Laine told us, “The editors do not know whether the reason for the inaccurate reporting was intentional,” she said. “We do know that it was sloppy, inaccurate, and the scientific record needed to be corrected.”

    When we asked why Annals chose to republish the paper, Laine said: “Not making these results available in a republished article would have been disrespectful to the 399 patients who altruistically participated in the study.” She also said the article underwent further peer review prior to publication in October. 

    Corresponding author Jamie Forrest, a health informatics researcher at Northeastern University’s Vancouver campus who was also on the Lancet article, told us Mills’ comments “represent those of the authorship.”

    Before its retraction, the paper was cited four times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science. The paper joins our list of almost 800 retracted COVID-19 articles, which includes a 2020 retraction from the journal for a paper claiming masks were ineffective in preventing the spread of COVID-19.

    PLOS and Janeway collaborate to develop open-source publishing infrastructure

    Note: PLOS issued the following press release October, 2026

    Janeway is pleased to announce a new collaboration with PLOS to develop and extend the Janeway codebase for use in parts of PLOS’s article submission process. As part of the collaboration, PLOS is contributing engineering effort, code and ideas upstream, helping to develop shared open-source infrastructure for the benefit of both PLOS and the wider scholarly publishing community.

    This is part of PLOS’s efforts to develop and contribute to open, shared infrastructure as part of its role in the open science ecosystem. It also reflects the core values on which Janeway is developed, based on the principles of open source and collaborative approaches to shared infrastructure. This means developing code and shared components that can be used and built upon by the wider Janeway community. By contributing to open tools and open shared infrastructure, PLOS aims to help strengthen the knowledge commons while reducing duplication and fragmentation across the system.

    Andy Byers, Director of Publishing Technology commented: “We’re delighted to be working with PLOS. Janeway has always been built on the belief that shared, open infrastructure is better for everyone, and having an organisation of PLOS’s standing contribute engineering effort and ideas back into the codebase is a real endorsement of that approach. The work we do together will benefit not just PLOS but every press and journal that relies on Janeway.”

    PLOS Chief Digital Officer, Tom Scott, said, ‘Open, community-developed infrastructure is essential to the future of scholarly publishing, and Janeway is an important part of that future. We’re excited to see what we can achieve together and to help Janeway continue to grow as an open, community-driven solution for scholarly publishing.”

    About PLOS

    PLOS is a non-profit organization on a mission to drive open science forward with measurable, meaningful change in research publishing, policy, and practice. We believe in a better future where science is open to all, for all.

    Building on a strong legacy of pioneering innovation, PLOS continues to be a catalyst in open science, reimagining models to meet open science principles, removing barriers and promoting inclusion in knowledge creation and sharing, and publishing research outputs that enable everyone to learn from, reuse, and build upon scientific knowledge.

    About Janeway

    Janeway is an open-source digital publishing platform developed in 2017 by the Open Library of Humanities at Birkbeck, University of London. Designed to support the full scholarly publishing workflow, from submission and peer review to production and publication, Janeway offers modern, standards-compliant features and integrates with services like Crossref and ORCID. Licensed under the AGPL, it is freely available for self-hosting or as a hosted service, and is used by many journals and institutions worldwide seeking a flexible, transparent, and sustainable publishing solution.

    Janeway currently provides the publishing infrastructure for all OLH journals and is also used by a growing number of university presses and other scholarly publishing organisations, including California Digital Library, MIT, University of Michigan Press, UCL Press, New York University, TU Darmstadt, the Society for Clinical Data Management, Iowa State University, Dublin City University, Ghent University, and University of Westminster Press.

    Paper touted by MD Anderson gets expression of concern for data ‘concerns’

    Science Translational Medicine has issued an expression of concern over “a series” of data concerns in a paper lauded by MD Anderson Cancer Center last year for identifying “a new driver” of bone metastasis.

    The article, published in May 2025, has been the subject of a long and growing thread on PubPeer since April, where critics have been raising concerns about numerous images that appear to have been duplicated. The majority of the paper’s 22 authors are affiliated with MD Anderson in Houston, with at least two authors from Shanghai Jiao Tong University in China.

    In June, the journal issued a correction to the paper, stating the authors “identified errors” during cross-checking of the original data, including incorrect and misplaced images.  

    On Oct. 14, journal editors announced they were placing an expression of concern on the article because of “accumulated concerns” that compromised their confidence in the findings. The journal wanted to alert readers about the issues while they “work with the authors to determine whether they can be addressed through an Erratum,” according to the notice.  

    According to a statement from Science Translational Medicine, the correction addressed concerns raised on PubPeer in April. At the time, “the editors deemed it more efficient to work with the authors to publish a correction outright, without the intermediate step of an EEoC,” according to the statement. 

    “Since the publication of the correction in June 2026, however, further issues were raised by readers on PubPeer, in August and September of 2026,” the statement read. “The editors deemed it appropriate to alert readers to the additional concerns by issuing an EEoC, in part to allow more time to assess and potentially resolve the issues.”

    When asked if a retraction is being considered, the editors stated they are “working with the authors to determine whether the issues can be resolved with another correction/erratum.”

    The work was supported in part by a grant from the U.S. Department of Defense and nine grants from the U.S. National Institutes of Health.

    Jiao Tong University, where at least two of the authors are based, is among the Chinese institutions on the Department of Defense’s list of restricted entities. The agency has warned researchers that “collaborating with an individual affiliated with an entity on the [list] constitutes a compliance risk and may negatively impact their eligibility for federal funding.” The National Science Foundation announced a new policy banning its grantees from partnering with institutions on the government’s restricted party lists. NIH hasn’t adopted a similar policy but has recently scrutinized papers by its grantees that include coauthors from China. 

    None of the authors we attempted to contact returned messages seeking comment. 

    MD Anderson highlighted the paper on its website shortly after it was published, showcasing the study for identifying “a new driver and therapeutic vulnerability of bone metastasis.” The article examined the role of lipid metabolism – the breakdown and creation of fats – in lab models of bone metastasis. Researchers also reported finding two therapeutic strategies that inhibit bone metastasis and which merited further study. 

    “Bone metastasis remains a major clinical challenge,” author Li Ma said in the post about the study. “By identifying [acyl-CoA binding protein] as a metabolic driver of this process and demonstrating the efficacy of targeting lipid metabolism in preclinical models, our work opens new therapeutic avenues for halting bone metastasis.”

    But in April, PubPeer commenter Yong‐Chang Zhou pointed out numerous instances of duplicate mouse images in several figures, followed by observations of identical numerical values by another PubPeer user in August. In September, yet another PubPeer commenter raised further concerns with the paper, writing “the reported coincidence” between some of the figures appears “statistically extraordinary.”

    In response to the April comments, Ma wrote that authors “revisited the source data for the live-mouse bioluminescence imaging experiments and identified several errors in the Supplementary Materials file.” 

    “These errors arose during figure assembly,” Ma wrote on PubPeer. “We have verified the original data and replaced the affected panels with the correct images. We have contacted the journal and are in the process of making a formal correction.”

    In response to the August comments, Ma explained some of the problems stemmed from “copy-and-paste errors introduced during assembly of the Source Data Excel file.” The authors have since corrected the source data for the affected figures, she wrote. In reply to the September comments, Ma defended the work, writing the authors “do not believe the probability calculation proposed in the comment provides an appropriate estimate for these longitudinal tumor-growth data.”

    Former Crick Ph.D. student loses COVID vaccine paper for fabrication

    Science Translational Medicine has retracted a paper on COVID-19 vaccines after an institutional investigation revealed a former Ph.D. student fabricated data and falsified images in the article. The retraction is the former student’s second for fabrication this year. 

    The July 2022 article looked at whether a vaccine targeting a particular part of the SARS-CoV-2 virus could help defend against a wider range of COVID-19 variants in mice. It has been cited 83 times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science. 

    According to the October 14 retraction notice, lead author Kevin Ng, formerly a Ph.D. student at the Francis Crick Institute in London, was responsible for fabricated data in one of the figures and falsifying images in another. Ng admitted responsibility for the fabrication during the editorial investigation, the notice states, which “casts doubt on some other experiments presented in the paper, for which primary data and samples are unavailable, precluding post hoc verification.” 

    The retraction follows another earlier this year in Nature on which Ng was also the first author. Ng’s supervisor, George Kassiotis, and two other Crick researchers are coauthors on both papers. The Crick investigation into the Nature paper — which investigated the sensitivity of lung cancers to immunotherapy —  similarly found Ng solely responsible for the fabrication. 

    That investigation prompted the institute to look into other works by Ng, according to a statement Kathryn Ingham, the public affairs lead at the Crick Institute, provided from the Crick Institute. Beyond the Science Translational Medicine paper, “the investigation found no evidence of data fabrication or inappropriate manipulation.”

    “There was no evidence of malpractice by any other authors of the paper,” the statement says. 

    Ingham did not respond to our request for the full investigation report. Kassiotis forwarded Retraction Watch’s request for comment to Ingham. 

    Nature put an editor’s note on another of Ng’s papers on September 29 after PubPeer commenters flagged “abnormal” data and apparent “numerical reuse.” 

    “We are actively investigating concerns that have been raised with us and will take action as appropriate once this process is complete,” Francesca Cesari, chief biological, clinical and social sciences editor for the journal, told us.

    After graduating from the Crick, Ng was a postdoctoral researcher at Rockefeller University in New York. Alicia Samuels, associate vice president for communications and public affairs at the university, told us he was no longer employed there but declined to elaborate on when and why he left. 

    A spokesperson for Science Translational Medicine told us the Crick sent them a request to retract the paper on September 8. “No other papers were named in this communication, and to date, no concerns have reached our attention regarding other papers co-authored by Ng,” they said, noting the publisher was not currently investigating any of Ng’s other papers published in Science journals.

    The Science Translational Medicine article joins our list of over 700 retracted COVID-19 papers. It’s the second retraction for the journal this year, and the seventh in the journal’s 17-year history.

    Ng has pivoted away from science and now covers classical music, according to his website, an archived version of which lists his research and affiliations with Rockefeller and the Crick. Ng wrote an article on Giacomo Puccini’s opera “Turandot” for The New York Times earlier this year.

    We were unable to find current contact information for Ng, and messages sent to his Instagram account went unanswered.

    Authors respond with ‘a bit like a threat’ after journal reveals postdoc’s identity

    In March, Thomas Harvey sat in on a talk at the American Physical Society’s summit in Denver on a new model of the universe that claimed to eliminate the need for dark matter and dark energy. William George, a fluid dynamicist who spent his career studying turbulence and an emeritus professor at Chalmers University of Technology in Sweden, gave the presentation. 

    “It was very rambling,” said Harvey, a postdoc working in high energy theory at MIT, “and it was very clearly wrong.” 

    After the talk, Harvey found George’s paper on the topic in Physics of Fluids. He decided to flag the problems he saw on PubPeer under a pseudonym, including errors in the math and misapplied principles of general relativity, and then sent a “polite email” to the editors. 

    “That’s where it went very wrong,” Harvey said. 

    Harvey’s email about the paper went to Savvas Hatzikiriakos, one of the journal’s two co-editors in chief, expecting it would remain confidential. “I just kind of assumed that surely any sensible journal would operate that way,” he told Retraction Watch. AIP Publishing, which publishes the journal, has guidelines for editors that make it their responsibility to “protect the anonymity of individuals raising concerns.” 

    Instead, the editors shared Harvey’s concerns with the authors — as well as his name, his institution and that he was a postdoc. In their reply, George and coauthor Thomas Johansson, also retired from Chalmers, acknowledged the paper contained a math error but insisted the conclusions were correct. The authors also suggested Harvey be “encouraged to withdraw” his criticisms, and wrote if the criticisms were published, “we will surely do our best to make them public and take our chances in the public arena.” 

    The authors’ response also recounted a “similarly aggressive young post-doc” who had criticized an earlier paper, resulting in it not being published in Physics of Fluids. They made it a point to present the postdoc’s comments “in every forum for several years,” and the postdoc “never managed to live down the stupidity and arrogance of his stupid remarks.” 

    George told Retraction Watch he wrote this example to again make the point that what goes in print stays there. “Harvey was correct in recognizing we had made an error. And that was useful. But he handled it stupidly,” George told us in an email. “If our theory proves to be correct, he (and many others) will look rather stupid.” 

    Harvey said he isn’t worried, since the authors are retired and work in a different field. But added “it felt a bit like a threat” to his career. 

    George rejected that interpretation in an email to Retraction Watch. “Certainly not a threat. But an attempt to make him aware that things you say in print last forever,” he wrote. “And when you say dumb things and especially say them maliciously (which he did in the original comment), they tend to live for a very long time.”

    Harvey learned his identity had been shared when Hatzikiriakos sent him the authors’ reply. Harvey wrote back to him and his co-editor Gerald Fuller, concerned that his identity had been shared without his consent. “A researcher should be able to flag a mathematical error to a journal without having their identity disclosed and without receiving threats in return,” he wrote. In a call with Retraction Watch, he described the situation as “basically editorial malpractice.” 

    Hatzikiriakos apologized to Harvey for disclosing his identity, calling it an “oversight,” and said Harvey’s original email didn’t indicate his comment was submitted in confidence. Hatzikiriakos suggested he submit a formal comment to the journal, which would be reviewed alongside a response from the authors. 

    “This is the only formal process available to address and potentially correct fundamentally flawed published work, thereby preventing the dissemination of incorrect conclusions,” Hatzikiriakos wrote. 

    “I didn’t really want to do this,” Harvey told Retraction Watch, “but as they had already leaked my identity, I felt I had little to lose.”

    AIP Publishing said it is aware of concerns about the paper and about how the criticisms were handled, and is reviewing the case. “We are unable to comment further while the review is underway,” said media relations specialist Hannah Daniel.

    There are “many things wrong” with the paper itself, according to Harvey. One is an algebraic error in one of the paper’s central equations. “They’re trying to do cosmology, and they’re left with the universe that is empty,” Harvey said. With their equation, “there’s nothing in the universe.” He called this error, and others he pointed out, “elementary.” 

    George acknowledged the math error. “Obviously we screwed up,” he wrote on PubPeer, and promised to issue an erratum, but maintains the rest of the analysis is still valid. In the original response to Harvey’s criticisms, the authors argued that physicists are “hopelessly biased against our findings,” calling their critics “apostles of the status quo” which is “precisely WHY our results are published in a fluids journal.” 

    According to George, arXiv told him and his coauthor they were not qualified to submit a paper on cosmology or astrophysics. (Disclosure: Retraction Watch cofounder Ivan Oransky is chair of arXiv’s board of directors but is not involved in day-to-day management or editorial decisions.) He said he pitched the paper to the Physics of Fluids editor-in-chief instead, and supplied a list of potential referees. Physics of Fluids has been his go-to journal for controversial ideas since his first paper there in 1973, George said. 

    “We are surely not withdrawing the original paper,” he told Retraction Watch. 

    Harvey is now waiting on the journal to publish his comment, as well as the authors’ response. Eight referees reviewed his comment, and none of them defended the original paper, according to the documents seen by Retraction Watch. Several recommended the article be retracted, and some stated that the authors’ response did not resolve the issues. 

    Hatzikiriakos, the journal’s editor, told Harvey that publishing a comment and response would be of greater benefit to the scientific community, per the referees’ suggestions — though this suggestion did not appear in the referee comments seen by Retraction Watch. Hatzikiriakos did not respond to our requests for comment. 

    In George and Johansson’s draft formal response, shared with Retraction Watch, they stand by their results, with the “single exception” of correcting the equation, “however upsetting they may be to the [General Relativity] and Astronomy communities.”

    Another paper rejected from arXiv is now in edits at Physical Review Fluids, an APS journal, according to George. “It will probably cause similar outrage, since it too shows Dark Matter is not necessary,” he said. 

    After Retraction Watch wrote to AIP, its research integrity and ethics manager emailed Harvey promising a “thorough review.” 

    “If I see something that is clearly incorrect, it’s published in a prestigious, apparently well-established journal … and is out of scope,” Harvey said, “probably, I have some kind of moral obligation to try and point this out.” 

    The next time he finds a problem with a paper, he will make it clear he expects anonymity, Harvey told Retraction Watch. “I thought I was doing them a favor,” he said, “but it didn’t seem to be viewed that way.”
