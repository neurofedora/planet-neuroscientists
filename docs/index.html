    PLOS becomes the first publisher to join the Research Software Alliance (ReSA)

    PLOS is delighted to join the Research Software Alliance (ReSA) as its latest Organisational Member and the first publisher to join ReSA’s growing network. ReSA Organisational Members support ReSA financially to bring research software communities together to collaborate on the advancement of the research software ecosystem.

    PLOS is a non-profit organization on a mission to drive open science forward with measurable, meaningful change in research publishing, policy, and practice. PLOS believes in a better future where science is open to all, for all.

    Building on a strong legacy of pioneering innovation, PLOS continues to be a catalyst in open science, reimagining models to meet open science principles, removing barriers and promoting inclusion in knowledge creation and sharing, and publishing research outputs that enable everyone to learn from, reuse, and build upon scientific knowledge. 

    As part of this commitment, PLOS promotes the sharing of research code to support reproducibility, transparency, and reuse, and encourages all authors to make code underlying their research findings publicly available. Since 2021, and in line with our long-standing policy on data availability dating back to 2014, a growing number of PLOS journals have strengthened commitments to code and software sharing, and it is required as a condition of publication in PLOS Computational Biology, PLOS Biology, and PLOS Medicine.

    Véronique Kiermer, Chief Scientific Officer at PLOS, said, ‘PLOS is committed to driving change in open science. We believe that credit and recognition for open science practices beyond the article are major barriers to wider adoption of open science and through our ‘Redefining Publishing’ initiative, we have worked to determine how code and software can be better recognised in scholarly publishing. We are currently working on the first stage of implementation of a “knowledge stack” to increase credit and recognition for non-article outputs, starting with increasing visibility and credit for code, software, and data shared with publications in PLOS journals. We are delighted to join ReSA and look forward to collaborating with the membership to make progress on our shared goals to increase value and recognition of research software.’

    PLOS has already been actively engaged in ReSA’s work to improve the recognition, sharing, and publication of research software, including through its involvement in the Publishing Research Software (PubSoft) Forum and the inaugural International Research Software Conference (IRSC26).

    At IRSC26, Lauren Cadwallader, Senior Open Research Manager at PLOS, presented Beyond Computational Biology: Making Code Sharing Policies Work Across Research Fields, exploring how code-sharing policies can be applied across research disciplines. Lauren also co-facilitated a Birds of a Feather (BoF) session introducing a new PubSoft Working Group. Co-led by PLOS and Springer Nature, the Working Group aims to improve publisher policies for sharing research software and code and recognising software as a distinct research output.

    We’re pleased to build on this collaboration through our membership with ReSA and look forward to continuing our work together.

    Planning a submission to arXiv in the next few weeks? Make sure to check arXiv’s holiday schedule to understand upcoming changes and delays to announcements.

    The 2026 end-of-the-year holiday stretch will be temporarily impacting arXiv’s mailings, help desk, and announcement schedule.     While the arXiv team logs in from around the globe, our staff primarily takes time off according to the holiday schedule of the United States, which at the end of the year includes many holidays in quick succession, leading to several announcement delays.

    Submissions to arXiv are typically made public on arXiv.org and announced by email on a regular schedule. As our team rests, celebrates, and takes time to be with family, there will be some disruption to arXiv’s regular announcements, mailings, and user support.

    • Friday, December 25th: No announcement or help desk support. Articles received and accepted at or after 14:00 ET Thursday, 24 December 2026 and before 14:00 ET Monday, 28 December 2026 will be announced at 20:00 ET Monday, 28 December 2026.

    Third retraction for former marine ecologist could ‘reopen wounds’ in decade-long scientific dispute

    Sandra Binning feels like she’s “being dragged back 10 years to a time in our careers that was honestly very, very difficult.”

    A fish ecologist at the University of Montreal, Binning was part of a group of scientists who attempted to replicate findings that flipped the field of marine behavior on its axis: Increased carbon dioxide in seawater, or ocean acidification, can debilitate the ability of fish to detect predators. Papers supporting the conclusion primarily came from marine ecologist Danielle Dixson.  

    In 2021, a Science news story reported on more than 20 studies that had come under scrutiny, including Dixson’s research. A subsequent investigation by the University of Delaware, where Dixson was once an associate professor of marine ecology, found she had committed research misconduct, which led to her dismissal.  

    Now, four years after that investigation, Ecology Letters has retracted Dixson’s 2009 paper on the effects of ocean acidification on clownfish behavior. The paper, published while she was a Ph.D. student at James Cook University in Townsville, Australia, is Dixson’s third retraction. The paper has been cited 412 times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science, including 86 times since it was flagged in 2020.

    The retraction came just as Binning and colleagues published data collected a decade ago while trying to replicate Dixson’s work. “Quite frankly, I would have preferred not to have to spend time justifying our research and have it being linked so intrinsically with this retraction and this fraud,” Binning said. “It’s painful.” 

    According to the August retraction notice, “a third-party complainant” alerted the publishers to “statistically implausible variance in the data.” 

    Timothy Clark, a fish physiologist and professor at Deakin University in Victoria, Australia, along with Binning and others, alerted Wiley, which publishes Ecology Letters, to concerns about Dixson’s research after attempting to replicate her work. Over three years, Clark and colleagues tested whether ocean acidification could impair the behavior of fish in coral reefs, and found no effect, they reported in Nature in 2020. Their critique opened the floodgates to a field-wide dispute. Many accused the researchers of being climate deniers who dismissed the impacts of ocean acidification on marine life, while few stood by their choice to challenge Dixson’s widely publicized conclusions.  

    “There were big fallouts with friends and colleagues,” Binning said. 

    When the researchers tried to inform Dixson’s team of their findings, they were met with a “brick wall and skepticism” about their methods, said Dominique Roche of Carleton University in Ottawa, another fish ecologist on the replication experiments team. 

    Dixson did not respond to Retraction Watch’s request for comment by email and phone. 

    While the Ecology Letters paper stemmed from Dixson’s doctoral research, her two previously retracted papers came from later stages of her career during her postdoc at Georgia Tech and later at her own lab at the University of Delaware. The earlier retraction statements echo the same concerns raised in the latest notice about her experimental timeline and a lack of confidence in the data’s validity.

    A spokesperson for Wiley did not respond to a question about why the retraction happened six years after the paper was flagged. The publisher offered no further comment beyond its notice. 

    Holden Thorp, the editor-in-chief of Science who signed the journal’s 2022 retraction of one of Dixson’s papers, explained that the time between publication and retraction can depend in part on the journal’s size. “Science only publishes 750 papers a year, so our capacity to put a lot of effort into these retractions is very different from much bigger publishers,” Thorp said.

    For graduate students, the delayed retraction poses a risk for those who “wasted their entire Ph.D. on a question based on bogus data,” Binning said. 

    Roche and Binning continued trying to replicate Dixson’s conclusions after their original attempt, leading to new findings on behavior of individual damselfish. In a paper published Oct. 8 in the Journal of Animal Ecology, the researchers reported their results from experiments conducted at Lizard Island in 2016. This research occurred in tandem with their investigation into Dixson’s work.

    “This was 10 years in the making,” Roche said. “We wanted to look a bit deeper into individual-level differences. We thought maybe something was happening that we didn’t see across a population.” 

    Once again, the researchers found little to no effect of increased CO2 on fish behavior.  

    “Even though it took us 10 years to publish the paper, it’s still novel,” Binning said. 

    The authors of the new study wrote a recent blog post about the role of the now-retracted paper. “The journal Ecology Letters recently retracted one of the key early studies that helped establish the expectation of severe behavioral impairment from ocean acidification in reef fishes,” the researchers wrote. “Retractions are an uncomfortable part of how science corrects itself, but they matter here because the affected work is heavily cited and continues to shape reviews, models, and policy framing.”

    Guest post: 20 years later, reflections on the retracted Lancet case report about codeine use and breastfeeding

    In 2005, Parvaz Madadi was a graduate student in pharmacology and toxicology at Western University in London, Ontario, studying under Gideon Koren, a once-renowned pediatrician and pharmacologist who would later resign and lose his medical license amid misconduct allegations. Madadi started working with Koren just before the publication of the now-retracted 2006 case report in The Lancet on an infant’s death from opioid poisoning, reported in the paper as the result of morphine exposure through breast milk. Madadi would go on to coauthor several papers with Koren on the topic. 

    Following interviews with journalist Ben Taub for an article in the New Yorker, Madadi revisited her emails from that era and contacted The Lancet on issues related to the case and requesting the journal retract the article. After an institutional investigation, The Lancet retracted the case report on Nov. 19. In this guest post, Madadi, a forensic scientist and writer, reflects on her role in that retraction, and events from 20 years ago.

    This moment brings reflection on my evolving understanding of this case report over these past 20 years: from a graduate student who defended this publication, to a forensic toxicologist who worked in death investigations, to a mother who can more deeply appreciate the relational frameworks that inform this story.

    Today, the retraction of this publication is a win for advocates of transparency in science. Very simply put, this case report was missing important toxicology findings; these details were relevant to interpreting the case and would have informed the scientific discourse that followed.

    However, this was by no means a simple case report. It was a complex interplay of forensic casework, maternal-neonatal pharmacology, and, at the time, cutting edge pharmacogenetic research. At its core was the tragic death of an infant and a family who had experienced a traumatic loss.

    The Lancet published this case report as a bench-side finding with direct significance to bedside practice, yet the report did not provide caveats or limitations inherent with this extrapolation. Following publication, the case report formed the basis of public health advisories in North America. Subsequently, an anonymous research participant became publicly identified and took on a role as a scientific advocate. When Nicholas Bateman and others began to question the forensic details underpinning the study’s conclusions, a death investigation that was closed also became the subject of scientific scrutiny and speculation.

    As the lead and corresponding author of The Lancet paper, Gideon Koren was faced with both ethical and scientific challenges with his interpretation of this case study. As his master’s student, I became drawn into the controversy by being given lead authorship in columns he had written, based on his original Lancet submission, for publication in Canadian Family Physician and Canadian Pharmacists Journal. 

    As a junior researcher just starting my academic journey, I perceived a gifted first authorship from my supervisor as a benevolent offering. In retrospect, Koren had departed from standard practice on authorship order based on contributions and also brought me into the forefront of a forensic case study, with all the implications that come with first authorship. More subtly, it was from that point forward that Koren began to refer to The Lancet case report as “ours,” and over time, I assumed responsibility for defending a case report I had neither formulated or written. 

    Years later, I conducted a large research study with the coroner’s office, and trained and worked as a forensic toxicologist myself. I came to appreciate the intricacies of death investigations and the precision that is needed when drawing conclusions. One of the main pillars of training for forensic scientists is to recognize your positionality: not only to clearly state what your area of expertise is, but to be aware of the limits to your scope and knowledge.  More intrinsically, forensic scientists must be open to any new evidence that is presented and constantly self-reflect on potential rigidity in their scientific positions.

    When I revisited this case report last year, in conversation with Ben Taub of the New Yorker, I found myself in a place of greater clarity in regards to the layers of this case. As I considered the information Taub presented, the case history and codeine gastric toxicology information he shared caused me pause. I subsequently went back to old emails to try and understand how this information had been missed. During this process, I identified additional ethical, technical and authorship concerns and raised these issues to The Lancet. The SickKids Research Integrity Office subsequently conducted an investigation and concluded that the omission of material toxicology data constituted falsification. Ultimately, this finding became the central basis for Lancet’s decision to retract the paper. 

    Twenty years later, I have come to appreciate the power of scientific discourse. Inquiring scientific minds had questions about this case, and through the contributions of Bateman, David Juurlink, Taub, and others, the dialogue was sustained with great perseverance. At the same time, this case inspired new research and insight in the field of pediatric pharmacology. A substantial body of literature has emerged from this case report, including epidemiological, observational, and modelling studies examining the safety of maternal opioid use during breastfeeding. The totality of the evidence from these studies now needs to be critically synthesized to inform best practice guidelines for breastfeeding mothers.     

    From my perspective, the core of this journey has been all about transparency.  Meaningful scientific discourse requires the disclosure of all relevant evidence from which we draw our scientific conclusions. The retraction of this case report has confirmed and addressed the foundational importance of this issue.  

    Yet there is another more subtle thread of transparency, and that is to recognize how relational frameworks can influence the way we interpret, present, and defend scientific data. Authorship, mentorship, scientific prestige, areas of expertise, and even advocacy; for breastfeeding mothers, for a research participant; for a deceased infant — these are the subtle threads of positionality which have woven the tapestry of this case study for 20 years.

    Two publishers investigating studies claiming to predict blood type from fingerprints

    The shape of your fingerprint can predict your blood type — at least, it can according to more than a dozen conference papers and journal articles published since 2023. But those papers seem to rely on fingerprint images that never included any information on the print owner’s blood type, researchers in Australia say. 

    The papers report training artificial intelligence models to sort fingerprints into blood groups with high accuracy using several collections on the data sharing platform Kaggle. One problem: The fingerprints in those datasets appear to be taken from a single original collection, the Sokoto Coventry Fingerprint Dataset (SOCOFing), which contains no blood type information. 

    “The papers are produced under false pretence with fake data and should therefore be retracted,” said Lars Kooijman, an adjunct engineering research fellow at Monash University in Melbourne, and Stephanie Summersby, an adjunct associate lecturer with school of psychology at the University of New South Wales, in an email to Retraction Watch. 

    Two publishers are now looking into the articles. 

    One paper published in Franklin Open, an Elsevier journal, didn’t name the specific dataset it used, but Kooijman found the fingerprints matched datasets on Kaggle that appeared to have clustered the images into blood type groups. 

    In an email to Retraction Watch, Sanivarapu Prasanth Vaidya, a computer scientist and lead author of the work, said the team had used the “Fingerprint Blood Group Classification Dataset” on Kaggle. “We did not independently collect fingerprint samples or conduct laboratory blood-group tests on the individuals represented in the dataset,” he wrote. He said the team had not known the images might come from SOCOFing or that the labels were generated by clustering, and had chosen the data “in good faith.” 

    The dataset he said he used appears to have been uploaded this July, while his paper was published in December 2025. Vaidya said the date on the Kaggle page may reflect a “subsequent update to the Kaggle repository rather than necessarily its original upload date.” 

    Elsevier is aware of the concerns, a spokesperson for the publisher said. “An investigation is underway, and therefore no further information can be shared at this point.” 

    Most of the other articles appeared as conference proceedings in IEEE Xplore, which is “currently evaluating the papers in question,” said a spokesperson in their publishing ethics team. The lead author of two of the papers, Krisha R., said in an email that the team had run no blood tests of their own, and it was too early to say whether a correction or retraction was needed. 

    Kaggle removed seven of the datasets the papers cited after Kooijman reported them for plagiarism. But at least two versions remain public on Kaggle, including the one Vaidya named. 

    Kooijman and Summersby came across the papers while reviewing research on fingerprint image quality. They said they grow suspicious whenever a paper reports more than 90% accuracy in biology, “as there is simply too much noise.” There is also little evidence that fingerprints are related to blood type, said Kooijman and Summersby, who did the analysis independently of their work at Monash and UNSW. At best, some studies have found an association between fingerprints and blood type, though say the results can’t be used at an individual level, and others have found no link. 

    The Kaggle datasets the papers cited included around 6,000 fingerprint images sorted into eight blood group folders. The pair ran the files through a hashing algorithm, which generates a unique signature from a file’s contents, and found more than 6,000 exact matches with SOCOFing — meaning the images had come from that dataset. But the file naming system was also a giveaway that the images had been clustered, they said. “The people who created this dataset were so slack they even left the name ‘Cluster’ in the fingerprint image names.” 

    “We have not collected or used blood type in our research (a PhD thesis) or predicted blood type from the scanned fingerprints,” Vasile Palade, one of SOCOFing’s creators, said in an email to Retraction Watch. 

    One uploader of a derivative dataset even acknowledged in the description that the images had come from SOCOFing and had been clustered into blood groups, according to Kooijman and Summersby.

    They found no papers from before 2023, and suspect AI-generated code played a role. More likely exist, they said. 

    “The fact that fake information is being circulated as scientific fact worries us,” Kooijman and Summersby wrote in an email to Retraction Watch, “especially the implications this may have in the medical system if a detection system such as the ones proposed in the articles is ever implemented.”

    Will AI solve biology?

    Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, has repeatedly claimed that AI will solve most biological problems, not just in the long run, but within the next five years. My instinct is that this is just commercial propaganda, but Amodei seems to genuinely believe it. Besides, Amodei’s academic background is actually similar to mine (computational neuroscience and biophysics), so I was a bit curious about his arguments. So, I went to his 2024 essay, “Machines of loving grace”, to understand what he means more precisely.

    Sadly, the argumentation is mostly a variation of these two fallacies: 1) AI has been getting smarter; extrapolate and it will soon exceed human intelligence everywhere; 2) AI has solved problems that everyone thought it could not solve, therefore it will eventually solve all problems.

    He articulates the first argument very explicitly at the beginning of his essay: “In terms of pure intelligence, it [will be] smarter than a Nobel Prize winner across most relevant fields. This means it can prove unsolved mathematical theorems, write extremely good novels, write difficult codebases from scratch, etc.”. The problem here is that “intelligence” is a catch-all buzzword that refers to very different capacities. Amodei does not seem to see the difference, for example, between math and biology. The big breakthroughs of AI have been in formal domains, that is, domains in which the whole world is defined by rules: games, coding, maths. But the laws of physics, or biology, are not the rules of the real world, they are models of it, tentative abstract constructions made by humans to understand an uncertain and undefined world. Conflating the two is basically conflating model and reality, it’s a basic epistemological mistake. Doing science and solving games are two different things. This being said, solving games is certainly a part of scientific activity, and so it seems plausible that AI could have a contribution, but it’s just a contribution.

    This is something that can be difficult to fully grasp for someone who has been trained in a formal domain. I have been, and it took me many years of working with experimental biologists and then doing experimental biology myself to realize that the epistemology of formal domains is not the same at all as the epistemology of natural sciences. A model in biology is really a gross idealization of reality, and quite often, what we describe as biological laws are more a bunch of heuristics and metaphors. But it’s not a big deal if you’re doing math, because you are studying the model, not the world. So, the second argument is based on an assumed equivalence between all “problems” (math, biology, coding, etc.), which is clearly false.

    I will now comment more specifically on Amodei’s essay. Many of the specific arguments are of the kind: technology (not specifically AI) has driven progress in the past, therefore the next technology (AI) will produce much more progress. That is, extrapolation with few arguments about what AI would add specifically and why. The core section is about biology and health.

    Biology

    “Intelligence might be used to develop a new experimental paradigm that allows us to learn in vitro what used to require live animal experiments”, and later: “developing better animal and cell experimental models (or even simulations) that are more accurate in predicting what will happen in humans.”

    How specifically AI would help us doing that, it is not explained. Apparently just because it is “intelligent”. But the mention of simulations is telling: maybe one idea is using AI to simulate living systems? Surely, once you have a very accurate model of a living system, that could help. But we don’t have such models, and we are not close at all to getting one. Importantly, making a model is not a formal activity; it starts in real life, not in a symbolic world. It’s not the first time we hear such claims. Henry Markram defended the Human Brain Project (a 10 year mega-project with 1 billion euro of public funding) in exactly those terms: we will measure everything about the brain, and then make a model from it, and we will then be able to simulate it and predict the effect of drugs with just “in silico” experiments. Needless to say, it did not work. Measurements were made, models were built, but none could be used to understand either neurological diseases or cognition. In my book (The Brain, In Theory, chapter 3), I explain in detail why “bottom-up” approaches like this never work in biology; in brief, because it adopts the epistemology of engineering to address biology, which has its own epistemology (such as the fact that a living organism does not have parts in the same sense as a machine, among other differences). The fact is that if you want to learn something new about life, you need to study life, not a model of life, and so, formal tools like AI will necessarily have limited scope.

    Then a few clichés about science, such as: “There’s perhaps ~1 of these major discoveries per year and collectively they arguably drive >50% of progress in biology.”. What does this even mean? Here “discoveries” actually refers to new techniques rather than scientific discoveries, and “progress” actually refers to scientific discoveries allowed by those techniques. Then in a way, it’s a trivial statement. When a new tool is developed, scientists quickly adopt it, and that implies that there is much scientific progress in the direction allowed by that tool. But that basically tells us that science is a human activity, and that what humans try to do depends on what they can do. So, for example, thanks to calcium imaging and optogenetics, neuroscientists have been able to measure the activity of many neurons, and stimulate many neurons. So, they have used the tools, and every other paper uses those tools. Were these revolutionary techniques? Maybe we can say that, as they improved enormously on earlier techniques. But has this been a revolution in biological understanding? I don’t think so.

    Another cliché on science: “[Breakthroughs] also are often delayed many years by lack of support from the scientific community for promising directions” and “This suggests that it’s not just massive resource concentration that drives discoveries, but ingenuity.” Amodei mentions CRISPR, which is based on discoveries about cellular immunity. They were hints about such findings already in the 1980s, he writes. So here, he is suggesting that AI will help spot these hints very early. But this is a fallacy: of course, retrospectively, you can find hints about all the major discoveries in literature a century old or more. You can find the concept of atom in Greek philosophy, about 2500 years ago. The point is that you don’t know in advance what is right, what is actually a good lead, and what is just a bad idea, and that is precisely the reason why there is “lack of support from the scientific community”, because a “promising direction” is something very subjective; not all promises are actually fulfilled. AI is not going to help with that.

    Even in AI, the basic algorithms that drive neural network learning date from the 1980s at least, but they were abandoned in the 1990s in favor of other machine learning approaches (such as support vector machines). Why? Well, simply because back then, neural networks didn’t work that well, and scientists thought that they had a major problem, namely that gradient descent would necessarily be stuck in local minima (think of letting a ball from the top of the mountain, it might not arrive at the sea). This was a reasonable argument. It turned out that in very high dimension, it is not so much of a problem, but this was a surprise to most people. At some point, some people tried anyway and it worked. But it certainly wasn’t a “promising direction”. Now in an empirical domain like biology, there is just no way to know whether something is a “promising direction” in any objective sense. Some people think it is promising, other don’t, and you cannot know in advance who will be right. So, when Amodei writes that “there are hundreds of these discoveries waiting to be made if scientists were smarter and better at making connections between the vast amount of biological knowledge humanity possesses”, we can see that he vastly misunderstands the problem of science, in particular the famous problem of “unknown unknowns”.

    Then he mentions the progress in mRNA technology and cancer, which is not based on AI. How this is an argument for AI, not so clear. It seems to be along the line: technology has allowed progress, therefore AI will provide more. But on the face of it, this to me is rather an argument against AI: we should perhaps divert some of the trillions of dollars for data centers to the funding of those new techniques that have actually proven their merits.

    Alzheimer’s: “There is a good chance it can eventually be prevented with relatively simple interventions, once we actually understand what is going on.” Argument: AI is so smart. And finally: “Improved treatment of most other ailments. […] Most of these seem “easier” to solve than cancer and Alzheimer’s”. AI will solve Alzheimer; other diseases are simpler; therefore, AI will solve all diseases. QED.

    There is also the promise of doubling human lifespan. The argument? “Life expectancy increased almost 2x in the 20th century, so it’s “on trend” that the compressed 21st would double it again to 150.”. First of all, the argument is not about AI. Second, it’s a really messed up argument. Most of the recent increase in life expectancy is due to lower death rates in childhood, while the increase in the modal age at death (peak of the distribution of age at death) has been much more modest over the last century (about 10 years), and the maximum human lifespan has not changed at all. We cannot reduce childhood death rate below zero, so there is not that much space for improvement. Then follows an argument on rats who can be made to live longer with a certain diet. What he omits is that this basically means that rats are constantly starved, so their metabolism runs low and that’s why they live longer. It’s scientifically interesting, but it doesn’t sound like such a promising cure for aging. And again, what would AI bring specifically to aging research? I guess “intelligence”. I don’t see any more precise argument, but that doesn’t prevent him from writing things like this: “Once human lifespan is 150, we may be able to reach “escape velocity”, buying enough time that most of those currently alive today will be able to live as long as they want”. Here, an error on what life expectancy means: when life expectancy at birth increases by one year, it does not mean that the currently 83 years old will live one year more, because the gain is distributed over all ages.

    To be clear, I am not saying that it is impossible in principle for humans to live longer than 130. Maybe it is possible, and it’s certainly worth working on aging research. But so far, clinical progress has been rather limited (i.e., there has been more progress on mitigating the consequences of aging than on the prevention of aging), and there is little indication that AI could help in any way.

    Then he writes about neuroscience and mental health.

    Neuroscience

    This section starts with some technique fetichism, marvelling about new microscopy techniques, which I suppose is an argument about how other techniques based on AI would be marvellous too.

    Then there is NeuroAI hype, the idea that studying AI can help you understand the brain, since they must be doing basically the same thing: “some of the things we’ve learned (or are learning) about AI itself in the last few years are likely to help advance neuroscience”. This is the general idea that the brain “computes with neurons”, which I take down in my book (The Brain, In Theory). Specifically, he suggests that we can study an artificial neural network as a proxy for the human brain: “It is much easier to do experiments on artificial neural networks than on real ones (the latter often requires cutting into animal brains), so interpretability may well become a tool for improving our understanding of neuroscience.”. Sure, it is certainly easier to run a model than to study reality. But is it even a model, though? Will studying the inner structure a LLM help much in understanding a prion-like neurodegenerative disease like Alzheimer’s or Parkinson? Or is he suggesting that if you train an LLM on sad songs, you get a model of depression?

    I see one valid remark: AI can help us invent new drugs. Specifically, one area where AI is certainly promising is computational biology. For example, algorithms can be used to predict what molecules are likely to interact with various proteins. But we should not overstate what “help” means here. You may predict that a drug will interact with a protein, but that does not immediately translate in knowledge about the effect on the organism, whether it cures anything, whether it has side effects, and this is obviously a major bottleneck in drug development.

    The paragraph on behavioral interventions is pure speculation: “it stands to reason that AI could accelerate these as well”. Why? We’re not told. And then “the idea of an “AI coach” who always helps you to be the best version of yourself”. Then on mental illness, at least he admits: “I’m not an expert in psychiatric disease”. But he has some ideas: “The answer is likely to be some combination of “something went wrong biochemically” (although it could be very complex) and “something went wrong with the neural network, at a high level”. Therefore, AI will cure mental illness? Here, Amodei’s main argument is simple: optimism. Writing about psychopathy: “my instinct is to be optimistic about what AI can invent here.”

    Then follow some old-fashioned clichés about genetics: “Most mental illness is partially heritable, and genome-wide association studies are starting to gain traction on identifying the relevant factors, which are often many in number. It will probably be possible to prevent most of these diseases via embryo screening, similar to the story with physical disease.”. He clearly misunderstands what these “associations” mean. Here I might need to explain a little. There are diseases that are genetic, for example a mutation in a specific protein can produce a specific disease, say a genetic type of epilepsy due to a defective ion channel. But this is not the case of most diseases, including neurological and psychiatric diseases. Nonetheless, if there is some level of heredity in the disease, whether carried by genes or not, it will show up in correlations with a large number of genes, simply because genes are hereditary too. Let me give a simple example. We know that someone born in the USA from Chinese parents will speak perfect English, so speaking Chinese is not due to specific genes. Yet, it is clear also that you will find correlations between many genes and the ability to speak Chinese, simply because there are genes that are more frequent in China than in the USA. These are just “associations” indeed. From his writing, I don’t think Amodei understands this point: “Oddly however, in recent years GWAS studies seem to suggest that these correlations might have been overstated. In any case, AI-accelerated neuroscience may help us to figure these things out”. In other words: I don’t get it ("oddly"), but surely AI will.

    The rest is too vague is be commented on (such as the promise to improve “human baseline experience”).

    Economics and politics

    The next two sections deal with social aspects and are even less well argued. Here we clearly drift from what AI can do, but it’s an interesting read as it shows the dangers of AI hype.

    So far, Amodei has been arguing that AI will be so super-intelligent that it will solve every problem that humanity has faced. But in the economics section, he draws a red line: one thing that AI cannot solve is economic planning. Solving biology, no problem. But beating capitalism, that’s really impossible! This is quite hilarious.

    Then the usual tech optimism that shows a complete misunderstanding of climate change and other threats due to human activity. For example: “We can expect that AI will lead to improvements in technologies that slow or prevent climate change” Why? Because “there’s good reason to think that AI-enhanced research will give us the means to make mitigating climate change far less costly and disruptive”. He doesn’t seem to be aware that we perfectly know what to do technically to prevent climate change, and that the problem is mainly political, not technological.

    On agriculture: “Advances in crop technology like better fertilizers and pesticides, more automation, and more efficient land use drastically increased crop yields across the 20th century”, so he proposes “finding even more ways to do this”. Again, he doesn’t seem to be aware that this industrial approach to agriculture, which treats fields as food factories rather than nutritive ecosystems, is destroying its very conditions of existence (such as destroying soils).

    What is tragic here is that what he argues for is essentially to invest huge amounts of capital to build data centers, when it could have been used to address climate change. Data centers will not only fail to address these issues, but they will worsen them.

    When he finally talks about politics, it becomes a little ridiculous. He recognizes that, in bad hands, AI could be used in very dangerous ways. His solution then: to make sure the “good” countries own the technology, while the bad don’t: “a global environment in which democracies control the most powerful AI”. And what if a democracy falls into fascism? (rings a bell maybe?)

    His propositions in the social domain drift into dystopia. For example, automating law. He notes that “trying to make the law fully mechanical hasn’t worked because the real world is messy and can’t always be captured in mathematical formulas.” But then “AI might be smart enough for this: it is the first technology capable of making broad, fuzzy judgements in a repeatable and mechanical way.”. Problem solved! He doesn’t seem to understand that applying the law is not an end but means. The law is invented by humans so as to shape society in certain directions, which is also why ultimately there are actual humans who judge and argue about real situations. What seems more likely, unfortunately, is that AI will be used instead to exploit loopholes in the law; i.e., to amplify what big companies already do to boost their private interests against the general interest (see my former post on profit robots).

    Finally, about democracy: “AI could be used to both aggregate opinions and drive consensus among citizens, resolving conflict, finding common ground, and seeking compromise”. Democracy reduced to data analysis of “opinions” existing out of nowhere, neglecting that opinions are shaped by actual human interactions where you try to understand each other, and where you might change opinions, not just find an intersection between individual positions. He doesn’t seem to realize either that “conflict” may arise from power inequalities, which you solve by addressing those inequalities, not just “seeking compromise”.

    Can AI solve biology? No. Can it solve economics, society and democracy? Also no. Can it yield some progress in biology? Probably. But then we must also consider the opportunity cost: what good will investing a trillion dollars in AI do, vs. investing a trillion dollars in, say, preventing climate change? I’m not sure the answer should be AI. And of course, the side effects: the ecological cost of data centers, the destruction of education, the degradation of the research ecosystem (e.g. the peer review system). Hype has a cost too.
