    Sage issues dozens of retractions from two journals it acquired

    In recent weeks Sage has retracted batches of articles published in two journals it acquired from IOS Press in 2023. In each case, the retractions were for manipulation of, and concerns about, peer review.

    The first tranche of papers was retracted July 15 from Journal of Intelligent and Fuzzy Systems (JIFS) and constituted 43 papers, some of which had been presented at a conference in Türkiye. According to the retraction notice, after an internal investigation, Sage “became aware that the submissions contain indicators of third-party involvement and attempts to manipulate the peer-review process” in the special issue.

    The second batch of retractions applied to 34 papers in the International Journal of Knowledge-Based and Intelligent Engineering Systems from 2017 to 2025. The August 5 notice cites suspicion of third party involvement, peer review issues, citation manipulation and non-standard language, including tortured phrases, as reasons for retracting the articles. Tortured phrases can indicate plagiarism.

    Since acquiring IOS Press in November 2023, Sage set the record for most retractions for a single journal when it retracted over 1,500 papers from JIFS. The move came after Clarivate put the journal’s indexing on hold for concerns about article quality. Its entry on the Clarivate website still shows the “on hold” flag. Chris Burnage, public affairs manager at Sage, told us the latest retractions were a result of a separate investigation from the one in 2024-2025.

    Sage has taken similar action for other former IOS Press journals, including 45 papers in  Clinical Hemorheology and Microcirculation retracted in November. 

    Sage defines third-party involvement as referring to paper mill activity, peer review manipulation, or “any other evidence suggesting the wholesale generation of research submissions.” 

    Among the authors of the retracted papers from JIFS were organizers and session chairs for the 2020 International Conference on Intelligent and Fuzzy Systems, run out of Istanbul Technical University in July of that year. Of the retracted papers, eight were coauthored by at least one of  the six editors of the published proceedings. 

    One of those editors, Istanbul Technical University engineering economics professor Cengiz Kahraman, is listed as author on six of those articles. He told us the authors had sent “numerous emails” to Sage raising objections to the retractions and called the publisher’s actions “legally and editorially unacceptable.”

    “Retraction decisions are complex and lengthy processes that should be concluded by taking into account the editorial and peer-review system under which the articles were originally published,” Kahraman said. 

    Burnage told us the publisher has “limited information about the original publication process.” 

    Selcuk Cebi, an engineer at Yildiz Technical University in Istanbul, was also an editor of the conference proceedings. He told us he has “never been presented with any article-specific evidence indicating unauthorized third-party involvement or manipulation of the peer-review process in relation to my paper.”

    Cebi and Kahraman lost another paper last year as part of another bulk retraction in JIFS. Cebi told us he sent Sage “the original submitted manuscript, revised versions, reviewer response documents, and the final accepted manuscript” in his appeal of the retraction, but has not received a “substantive” response. 

    The mass retraction at International Journal of Knowledge-Based and Intelligent Engineering Systems applied to papers from six volumes at the journal. Authors on all papers except one were affiliated with institutions in China. Gui-Wu (Guiwu) Wei,  a professor of business administration at Sichuan Normal University in Chengdu, was author on seven of the retracted papers. He did not respond to our request for comment. 

    Sage isn’t alone in its attempts to clean up journals it acquired. Wiley bought Hindawi in 2021 and stopped using the brand name in 2024 after retracting thousands of papers and closing journals overrun by paper mills.   

    “Since acquiring IOS Press, we have worked to integrate the portfolio into Sage’s editorial, technology and research integrity processes,” Laura West, communications and public affairs manager at Sage, told us. “As part of that effort, we have applied the same rigorous publication ethics and research integrity standards that guide all of our journals publishing.”

    Princeton student says she did not conduct the experiments described in her now-retracted paper

    Earlier this week, Retraction Watch reported on a Princeton student’s decision to retract her own paper from an engineering journal due to “significant methodological errors.” The student, Rishika Porandla, now tells us she never conducted the experiments described in the study.

    “The beam experiment described in the paper was a proposed experimental workflow with associated calculated results,” she wrote in an email. “Public confusion led me to recognize and regret that this is not clear in the retracted publication, and I have included detailed clarification in an updated retraction statement to Wiley.”

    Porandla’s original article, published in 2024 in Macromolecular Materials and Engineering based on work she did while in high school, claimed to show it was possible to enhance the biodegradation of polyvinyl chloride, or PVC, by mixing it with gold nanoparticles and blasting it with an electron beam.

    On May 22, Porandla reached out to Retraction Watch by email to share the news of her retraction because she felt it “highlights a broader issue involving student researchers entering the publication space before fully understanding experimental rigor and reproducibility standards.” 

    She also noted she had faced “significant pressure from mentors to move quickly toward publication before the work had been sufficiently validated.”

    The retraction notice published by the journal indicated she had decided to retract the paper after conducting a follow-up study and discovering problems with “the calibration of the electron beam exposure time and the distribution of gold nanoparticles.”

    While the original decision to retract the paper brought praise from one of her professors and the journal’s editor-in-chief, it also led to deeper scrutiny of the high-achieving student’s publishing record and a “research-driven college counseling” business she runs to help students gain admission to Ivy League colleges.

    Shortly after our original story was published on August 10, commenters on PubPeer began raising doubts about whether the electron beam experiment occurred and pointing out problems with citations and other issues in a handful of other preprints and student publications she had posted online. (Our co-founder Ivan Oransky is a member of the PubPeer Foundation’s board of directors, but has no role in moderation or any other day-to-day operation of the site.)

    “What concerns me more is the apparent failure of the ‘adults in the room’ and of the safeguards that are supposed to surround student research: teachers and advisors, mentors, and, where applicable, peer reviewers and editors,” Kevin Patrick, a sleuth who goes by “Actinopolyspora biskrensis” on PubPeer, told us. “The extraordinary pressure surrounding college admissions has created incentives that are worth examining here.”

    There’s no question Porandla is an exceptional student. In 2024, when she was a senior in high school in Coppell, Texas, her school posted a video on YouTube of teachers and students gushing about her drive, generosity and intelligence.

    The video ends with a statement from Mark Clampin, then the head of the astrophysics division at NASA headquarters, where she participated in five internships during high school. “Rishika is my hope for the future generation of science,” he wrote. “I cannot begin to imagine what discoveries and achievements lay in her future.”

    By then, Porandla was hard at work on the Spacetime Archives, a website and outreach program she founded in partnership with the Junior Texas Astronomical Society. She conducted interviews for a video podcast and recruited other former NASA interns and other space nerds to create educational materials, blog posts, and even a student journal, The Physical Scientific Archives, where she served as the editor.

    “AI-usage for paper writing, papers without references, or unethical experimental procedures are strictly prohibited,” the journal’s submission guidelines read.

    Shruti Joshi, now an engineering student at the University of Texas at Austin, learned about the program in late 2023 as a junior in high school. One day at the Cozby Library and Community Community Commons in Coppell, she stumbled upon Porandla giving an astronomical presentation and sent her a message afterwards.

    “She had a program to mentor students to write papers about certain topics,” Joshi said. Porandla matched Joshi with two other students and helped the group conduct a study on black holes using open-source data. 

    In February 2024, their study appeared in Volume 1, Issue 3 of Porandla’s journal, and it was later uploaded to the SSRN preprint server.

    “This is just a notice that your Spacetime Archives paper has been published on SSRN,” Porandla wrote in an email to her coauthors on July 13, 2024. “Two of you have ‘no affiliation’ next to your names and that is because your schools are not registered with SSRN. Over the next week, I’ll be working on that issue!”

    That year, Porandla founded Counseling by Rishika which features nine consultants — several are also coauthors — to provide one-on-one college advising for $950 over five weeks. 

    These consultants, her website said until recently, have “conducted and published original research in fields spanning medicine, astrophysics, quantum mechanics, and computer science.” 

    Porandla and her mentees and consultants have shared their wide-ranging work in student journals, conference proceedings, preprint servers, and at least one open access journal published by OMICS International, which has been previously fined for deceptive tactics. 

    How many paid for the help is unclear, but the website for her firm says it has advised “more than 100 students.” Joshi said she didn’t pay to work with Porandla. 

    “My consulting work aims to help students conceptualize and develop projects for middle and high school science fairs and competitions,” Porandla told us.

    Her single-author paper in Macromolecular Materials and Engineering, published during her freshman year at Princeton, appears to be her only publication in a widely recognized, peer-reviewed journal. 

    She previously told us that it represented “countless hours in the lab” and “was part of my identity as a researcher.”

    She originally said she decided to withdraw the paper after discovering methodological errors during a class with Princeton professor Andrea DiGiorgio. 

    DiGiorgio told us she remained confident in Porandla’s integrity but declined to comment on the new concerns beyond a brief statement.

    “The beauty of science and scholarship is that they are self-correcting, they have to be, and negative discourse around self-correction only impedes this process and sows fear into future researchers who are faced with this difficult decision,” she wrote in an email.

    JAMA journal won’t retract oxycodone paper despite author request, citing no ‘falsification’

    JAMA Internal Medicine has rejected a petition by a group of clinicians calling for the journal to retract an article on oxycodone therapy for osteoarthritis. The request — which initially included two of the paper’s original authors — stems from 19-year-old court records revealing the sponsor of the study withheld evidence about opioid withdrawal that contradicted the paper’s conclusions.  

    Published in 2000 under the journal’s previous name, Archives of Internal Medicine, the study of 133 patients concluded that around-the-clock, controlled-release oxycodone was safe and effective for managing osteoarthritis pain and that withdrawal symptoms were rare. In 2007, documents made public during a federal criminal case against Purdue Frederick Company, the maker of OxyContin, showed the company concealed how many patients became dependent on the drug during the trial and omitted withdrawal data. Purdue pleaded guilty in 2007 to fraudulently marketing OxyContin with the intent to mislead, agreeing to pay the government more than $600 million in penalties. 

    Despite the withdrawal data being made public nearly 20 years ago, the study has remained in the scientific literature without changes, Andrew Kolodny told Retraction Watch. Kolodny is medical director for the Opioid Policy Research Collaborative at Brandeis University and president of Physicians for Responsible Opioid Prescribing. Kolodny has also testified before Congress about the opioid epidemic, assisted federal authorities with investigating the opioid industry and pain organizations, and acted as an expert witness for plaintiffs in litigation against opioid makers. 

    In late July, Kolodny and seven others, including two of the paper’s authors, called on JAMA Internal Medicine to retract the paper. The withheld data directly contradict the article’s central conclusion that controlled-release oxycodone can be abruptly discontinued without causing withdrawal, according to a July 29 letter sent by the group to Sharon K. Inouye, the editor in chief of the journal. 

    Kolodny had previously sent solo retraction requests in the fall and winter of 2025, which the journal declined.  

    The letter states “prompt action by the journal is especially important” because the article continues to influence the medical literature and clinical practice. 

    “It has been cited hundreds of times and, notably, appears in at least two clinical practice guidelines,” the letter reads. “The continued citation of a publication containing unreliable conclusions—including in contemporary documents intended to guide patient care—underscores the importance of correcting the scientific record.”

    The paper has been cited 247 times, according to Clarivate Web of Science. 

    Jen Zeis, a spokesperson for JAMA Internal Medicine, told us the journal does not plan to retract the article. 

    “We have received a petition to retract this article and reviewed the request multiple times,” she said in an email. “We saw no evidence of fabrication or falsification in the published article. We have also discussed with two of the coauthors, including one who had cosigned the petition, who do not agree with this request for retraction.”

    The article about the study disclosed funding from Purdue and identified coauthor Peter G. Lacouture as a company employee. The authors included two reports of withdrawal symptoms by patients who abruptly stopped taking the medication, but noted that “withdrawal syndrome was not reported as an adverse event for any patient during scheduled respites.” 

    Kolodny came across the 2007 court proceedings during his research for a federally funded project  to identify articles in the literature with misinformation about opioids. According to the settlement documents, Purdue knew eight participants in the study had possible opioid withdrawal symptoms a year before the paper was published.

    “Many in the medical community who didn’t pay attention to the criminal case against Purdue have no way of knowing that there is anything wrong with this paper,” Kolodny said. “Clinicians could very well wind up using this paper during their care of patients on oxycodone.” 

    Kolodny first wrote to JAMA Internal Medicine in October 2025 to request the paper be retracted. In November, an editor replied that the journal does not plan to retract the piece, according to correspondence we’ve seen. Kolodny wrote again in December with additional supporting information and legal documents, but the journal reiterated its decision. 

    “There is no evidence of fabrication or falsification in this article that warrants retraction, and we do not intend to take further action,” Annette Flanagin, executive managing editor for JAMA and the JAMA Network, wrote in a Dec. 19, 2025 email to Kolodny. 

    The signatories on the July letter originally included two authors of the paper, Roy Fleischmann, a rheumatologist and professor at University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, and Barry Bockow, a rheumatologist and associate professor at the University of Washington. Bockow did not return messages seeking comment. 

    According to correspondence we’ve seen, Fleischmann told Kolodny that during the Purdue investigation, he testified before a grand jury that he was “not provided with all the relevant information” needed to “write the publication correctly.” 

    Fleischmann expressed his support for retraction in a July 14 email to Kolodny. 

    But Fleischmann has now changed his mind, telling us he “signed the letter in haste without looking at all the facts.” He has since reviewed the article in depth and said he agrees with the journal that “the manuscript was 100% accurate with no falsified data.”

    “The possibility of dependence is discussed in the manuscript,” he told us. “For this reason,  I changed my position and do not believe that this article should be retracted.”

    The lead author of the study, rheumatologist Sanford H. Roth, died in 2024. 

    In an August 4 email to Kolodny, Flanagin wrote that “without agreement of all of the surviving coauthors to retract the article, we cannot move forward.” 

    The paper should have been retracted years ago, but “unfortunately no one has set the record straight,” said Caleb Alexander, an internist and epidemiologist who codirects the University of California, San Francisco-Johns Hopkins University Opioid Industry Documents Archive and a signatory of Kolodny’s letter to JAMA Internal Medicine. “These types of misleading articles have helped to fuel the opioid epidemic.” 

    The paper illustrates the broader pattern of the opioid industry using scientific evidence to support unsubstantiated claims to inflate the benefits of opioids and minimize the dangers, said Ravi Gupta, an internist and assistant professor at Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine who signed the letter. Gupta and several other signatories authored a 2024 paper about that pattern. 

    “It is imperative to retract papers that advanced unsubstantiated claims to maintain scientific validity, as well as for accountability and to minimize patient harm,” Gupta told us.  

    David Juurlink, professor of medicine and pediatrics at the University of Toronto, also signed the letter. He has been central in the investigation into a 20-year-old case report of a baby’s death from opioid poisoning, brought back into the spotlight earlier this year by an article in the New Yorker. He also coauthored an analysis finding a five-sentence letter in the New England Journal of Medicine was heavily cited for stating opioid addiction was rare. The letter prompted the journal to add an editor’s note to the original letter. 

    Kolodny said he has been surprised and disappointed by the journal’s repeated refusals to retract the paper. When he first encountered the misrepresented findings in the court record, it seemed like “very-low hanging fruit” to get the article retracted, compared with more complex retraction pursuits.

    “If a journal is presented with credible information that a paper warrants retraction, it should retract the paper,” he told us. “The burden shouldn’t be on the person identifying the problem to reach out to other experts to get a group letter or to reach out to the co-authors. It should stand on its merit.”

    Biology journal pulls 100+ articles and counting from special issues for peer review manipulation

    Elsevier’s International Journal of Biological Macromolecules has issued more than 100 retractions and counting for manipulated peer review in guest-edited special issues. The publisher has said one more retraction is coming. 

    Since June the publisher has retracted 120 papers. According to the retraction notices, the journal identified “[s]ystematic, coordinated, and widespread manipulation of the peer-review process.”

    A spokesperson for Elsevier told Retraction Watch the publisher “became aware of possible systematic manipulation and other issues such as plagiarism, image duplication, and authorship issues in a number of articles published in Special Issues” in 2025, which led them to conduct a wider investigation into more recent special issues. 

    Nearly all of the retracted papers had authors with affiliations in China and were published in 2024 and 2025. The articles covered cancer cell mutations and molecular causes of chronic conditions like sleep apnea and pain, among other topics. 

    Guest-edited special issues appear to be particularly vulnerable to manipulation. Amid its retraction of more than 10,000 papers between late 2022 and early 2024, Hindawi temporarily suspended publishing special issues in 2023 because of manipulated peer review. In 2024, a Springer Nature journal retracted 34 papers from special issues for “compromised editorial handling and peer review process.” The same year, Annals of Operations Research retracted an entire special issue over concerns about “compromised” peer review.

    The author who lost the most papers (five) in the latest spate of retractions was Junkai Qin, who listed affiliations with Minzu Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region and Affiliated Hospital of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities. The five retracted articles from the International Journal of Biological Macromolecules are the only ones listed on Qin’s ResearchGate profile. We could not find a corresponding ORCID or institutional profile page for the researcher, and none of the corresponding authors of the five papers responded to our emails. 

    Li Jin, a researcher who listed affiliations at Macau University of Science and Technology and University of Electronic Science and Technology, lost three papers in the bulk retraction. Jin told us he was in the process of contacting the journal “to inquire about the specific details and official rationale for these retractions, including the peer review concerns.” 

    The Elsevier spokesperson told us the retractions “pertaining to this peer-review manipulation scheme are now almost complete, and Guest Editor checks were further tightened to enable detection of issues as soon as possible.”

    Fabian Wittmers, a postdoctoral researcher at Oregon State University, told us he has been sending batches of concerning papers to the journal since April 2025. He has flagged over 300 articles so far, both to the journal and on PubPeer, he told us, and wrote about the journal in a blog post last September. Only one of the papers he reported was among those retracted for peer-review manipulation; 11 of the 352 he has reported have been retracted for other reasons. 

    The journal roughly doubled its publication volume from nearly 5,000 articles in 2023 to over 9,600 in 2024, and published over 10,300 articles in 2025. 

    The publisher did not respond to our question regarding this spike, but said: “Given the rise in submissions across the research publishing ecosystem and other changes seen in recent years, the investment made by the academic publishing industry to ensure research integrity is higher than ever.” So far in 2026, the International Journal of Biological Macromolecules has published over 4,000 articles.

    BMJ Group retracts paper on COVID-19 vaccines and mortality featured in Senate hearing

    BMJ Public Health has retracted a paper some — including a witness at a recent U.S. Senate subcommittee hearing — have used to link COVID-19 vaccines to deaths. The action comes more than two years after a researcher cited in the work flagged issues with the study. 

    Published May 6, 2024, the paper used the “Our World in Data” database and the World Mortality Dataset to look at COVID-19 related deaths during the height of the pandemic. News outlets covered it, from The Telegraph — “vaccines may have helped fuel rise in excess deaths” — to the New York Post, which quoted the paper’s statements about reported “serious injuries and deaths following vaccination,” a move it later corrected. 

    An expression of concern, placed on the paper about a month after it was published, attempted to quell these claims, noting the study “does not establish any such link.” The notice also announced an institutional investigation by the Princess Máxima Center in the Netherlands, where three of the four authors worked, including corresponding author Saskia Mostert.

    Critics of the paper said it contained misquotes and omissions on vaccines and was “full of inappropriate insinuation.” Ariel Karlinsky, an economist at Hebrew University in Jerusalem, Israel, and cocreator of the World Mortality Dataset, contacted the journal starting in June 2024, requesting a retraction multiple times. Karlinsky felt the analysis misused his data and “copied whole blocks of text and mathematical notation” from his article. 

    According to the August 11 retraction notice, BMJ Public Health retracted the paper for “misinformation in the discussion regarding the possible causes of excess mortality, and because the limited nature of the original work by the authors was not sufficiently described.”

    In 2024, the Princess Máxima Center investigated the involvement of two authors, Minke Huibers and Gertjan Kaspers, but did not look at Mostert’s work on the study because she was “not able to cooperate with the investigation,” the report reads. 

    The anonymized version of the report summary dated January 2025 was published alongside the retraction and concluded that while the authors’ actions did not constitute misconduct, Kaspers and Huibers exhibited “carelessness, ignorance and negligence.”

    The report also found the article “pays disproportionate attention to the possibility that introduction of corona measures and vaccination might have caused excess mortality, whilst largely ignoring alternative explanations” and “misleadingly and unilaterally cites literature that supports its own arguments.”

    According to the detailed retraction notice, which is over 700 words long, Kaspers and Huibers requested the retraction alongside their institution “due to concerns about misinterpretation of the paper by others and subsequent public health risk,” but did not agree with the journal’s determination the paper contained misinformation. Emails to both researchers returned out-of-office notifications. 

    In a June 2024 announcement that has been removed but is archived here, the Princess Máxima Center said it was “emphatically” distancing itself from the publication and said they “strongly support vaccination.“

    Jan Hoeijmakers, chair of the scientific integrity complaints committee at Princess Máxima Center, did not respond to our questions about specifics of the investigation or our request for a copy of the full report. 

    Lead author Mostert resigned in 2024 after the Princess Máxima Center announced it would be investigating the paper, she said at a hearing on June 3 before the U.S. Senate Homeland Security & Governmental Affairs Committee subcommittee on investigations on “Plausible Mechanisms of COVID-19 Injections Causing Cancer and Attacks on Scientific Publications and Research.” In her testimony, which was largely focused on the BMJ Public Health paper, she said the authors “did not claim certainty regarding the causes of excess mortality. Rather, we asked attention [sic] for three major events that were non-existent before the pandemic: COVID-infection, containment measures, and COVID-vaccines.” 

    The press office for U.S. Sen. Ron Johnson, chair of the subcommittee on investigations, did not respond to our request for comment regarding the retraction of the paper.

    Mostert argued governments and media implemented fear and propaganda tactics to reduce “tolerance for reason and dissenting voices” during the pandemic and claimed the investigation into her paper violated academic freedom and constituted censorship. 

    She also said institutional responses like that from the Princess Máxima Center “are increasingly used as ‘official channel’-tactics to discredit, intimidate, isolate and silence those raising unwelcome scientific questions,” leading to her resignation. 

    Also in her testimony, Mostert said she proposed a revision to the published paper that “included a section on vaccine effectiveness in reducing mortality,” but the suggestion was “ignored by the journal.” She also claimed the original section on containment measures and vaccines was “much shorter in the original version of the paper,” but reviewers “asked us to either delete the text or provide more evidence. As there is evidence available, we included it.”

    The BMJ Public Health retraction notice acknowledged the submission, but said the change would be “insufficient.” 

    Mostert wrote to the journal on behalf of herself and the study’s fourth author, Marcel Hoogland, that they disagreed with the decision to retract. Hoogland was listed as an independent researcher based in Amsterdam on the now-retracted paper. Mostert’s most recent article, published in February in JCO Global Oncology, lists her as an unaffiliated researcher. Requests for comment sent to two email addresses bounced back as undeliverable.

    The article has been cited 21 times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science. Altmetrics for the article put it in the top 5% of all research outputs scored by the site. It joins our list of nearly 700 retracted COVID-19 papers. 

    The retraction is hardly the first of a paper linking COVID-19 vaccines to excess deaths. For example, earlier this year, a lawsuit failed to block the retraction of a paper linking heart-related deaths to the COVID-19 vaccine. In 2024, Cureus retracted a paper on the “extensive” harms of the vaccine. 

    Karlinsky, whose estimate model was cited in the now-retracted paper, said he respected the Princess Máxima Center’s “swift action and internal investigation and request to retract on the matter, but disagreed with their finding that no plagiarism or malice occurred.”

    He told us he suspected the retracted paper plagiarized his work, using a tortured phrase as an example: There was “no other way to explain how ‘compromise between flexibility and simplicity’” in his paper “was tortured to ‘a trade-off between suppleness and chasteness,’” he said. Tortured phrases can indicate plagiarism.

    Karlinsky also called the BMJ Group’s response “quite bad.” The journal was “not touching on the fundamental issues and making themselves look good in this, where they really weren’t,” he told us. 

    He was particularly critical of how long the retraction took. Karlinsky submitted a commentary in June 2024 criticizing the work, which was rejected without review a month later, he told us. 

    The Princess Máxima Center shared the investigation report with the journal at the end of 2024, according to the retraction notice. “The time to investigate this paper was lengthy because the journal first awaited the formal official outcome of the institutional report,” which was shared in May 2025, the notice reads. After receiving the report, the journal attempted to contact Mostert again “for her perspective on key issues,” it states, before making its final decision. 

    “The fact that [the journal] prides itself on having waited until they hear back from Mostert is, in my opinion, ludicrous,” Karlinsky said, noting the move prolonged the investigation. “This is like letting a murder suspect get away with it because he’s refusing to come to the police station.”

    A college student had to retract her paper. The move was ‘the starting point for a better study’

    Note, August 14, 2026: See an update to this story here.

    As a sophomore at Princeton University, Rishika Porandla took a course called “The Curious Scientist,” where students practice disciplinary research skills and act as a “PI” on a project of their choosing.   

    Porandla didn’t have to think twice about what subject to dive into. In high school, she had studied the biodegradability of polyvinyl chloride and discovered a new process to break down the plastic. The finding led to one of her proudest high school achievements — a published journal article. She was eager to use the Princeton class to advance her discovery. 

    From the outset, Porandla’s project for the class seemed like “a slam dunk,” Andrea DiGiorgio, the course’s instructor, told Retraction Watch.   

    As Porandla dug deeper into her prior work at the Princeton Imaging and Analysis Center, however, she unearthed a troubling find: Two methodological errors. The mistakes affected the study’s conclusions, rendering the results inaccurate.  

    At first she struggled to accept the discovery.  

    “I found myself hoping the next experiments would explain away the inconsistencies,” she recalled. “I thought it might have been a calibration error in the Princeton experiment, not the one I did back in high school. I thought, ‘OK, this is a different facility, maybe I overlooked something when I replicated this procedure.” 

    But the more she examined the study, the clearer the reality became.  

    Porandla contacted DiGiorgio for guidance and started researching what to do, she told us. She read about authors submitting corrections or retractions after finding mistakes in published papers. Crestfallen but determined to do the right thing, Porandla contacted Wiley about the concerns and asked for guidance pursuing either a correction or retraction of the work. 

    “[It] was really hard because I had to let go of something that beyond just being a publication, beyond just being on my CV, was a full project that I really thought was part of my identity as a researcher,” she told us. “The paper represented years of work in high school [and] countless hours in the lab. It was one of the experiences that convinced me that I wanted to be a researcher.”

    After a review by Wiley and the editors of Macromolecular Materials and Engineering, the journal retracted the paper in May. 

    Those familiar with Porandla’s case praised her candor to come forward, citing the rarity of self-retractions. A 2013 study of 1,423 retractions indexed by the Web of Science database found about 22% of retractions were self-reported, although that can be difficult to discern based on the content of retraction notices. The Center for Scientific Integrity, the parent nonprofit of Retraction Watch, recently announced the inaugural winners of a new effort to recognize scientists who self-retract. The annual Ctrl-Z Award honors researchers who discover errors in their published work and take meaningful steps to correct the record, recognizing that doing so can come with professional risk. 

    “Instances of authors proactively raising concerns about their own work are not the most common scenario,” David Huesmann, editor-in-chief of Macromolecular Materials and Engineering, told us. “This author, and others who do reach out to identify and correct mistakes in their work, show an honest commitment to scientific integrity.”    

    Huesmann confirmed Porandla reached out to the journal directly, and an evaluation of the concerns raised “ultimately led to the decision to retract.” The journal has since encouraged Porandla to resubmit an updated paper, Huesmann said. 

    Porandla described herself as a “very high-achieving student” in high school, conducting internship projects ranging from radio galaxy classification and supermassive black hole studies to climate and orbital dynamics modeling at the NASA Johnson Space Center in Houston.  Her interest in electron beam research developed after a science competition where judges selected her proposal on beam therapy for osteosarcoma as a finalist.  

    Porandla later pitched an idea to her chemistry teacher about using high-energy electron beams as a degradation mechanism. She wanted to study whether infusing PVC with gold nanoparticles and then using electron-beam irradiation and near-infrared photothermal heating would improve the plastic’s biodegradability and make it less toxic. 

    Over the next two years, she studied how varying irradiation conditions affected key material properties of the plastic to maximize degradation. Teachers praised the work, and she received “pretty positive” feedback from the reviewers who conducted her International Baccalaureate assessment, she recalled.  

    “I actually got the referral to Wiley from my chemistry teacher,” she told us, who had found a paper on a similar topic in a different Wiley journal. “It was my own decision, but everyone was very supportive of me publishing.”

    Looking back, Porandla said she doesn’t believe her teachers adequately advised her on the importance of rigorous experimental validation, replication, and independent verification before publication. At the time, she didn’t question feedback from teachers that her study was publication-ready. 

    Porandla was the sole author of the paper, which went through Wiley’s standard peer review process, she said. Based on her correspondence with editors, the journal had difficulty securing reviewers and the manuscript ultimately proceeded with a single reviewer. Her manuscript went through one round of revisions that primarily centered on presentation, figures and expanding the discussion in several sections, she told us. 

    “I did not receive any reviewer comments critiquing the study’s methodology or questioning the scientific content itself,” she said. “I feel that I would have felt more comfortable with publishing the work if there were more external reviewers or if more rounds of review were conducted.” 

    Porandla’s former chemistry teacher declined to comment for this story. 

    The problems Porandla later found in her paper related to the relationship between the programmed electron-beam exposure time and the actual delivered dose not being independently verified, she explained. Imaging and elemental mapping also revealed the gold nanoparticles in the study were not uniformly dispersed throughout the PVC matrix as originally assumed. Both “fundamental flaws” affected the reliability of the conclusions, Porandla said. 

    “I no longer felt comfortable having the paper remain in the scientific literature because I knew that other researchers could potentially cite it, build upon it, or use it to inform future work,” she told us. 

    DiGiorgio, the Princeton instructor, was impressed by Porandla’s decision to self-retract and the bravery it took to share her findings with the class. DiGiorgio herself once submitted a small correction to a published paper and called it “a terrifying process,” and one in which “you worry about your credibility, your reputation,” and “your future.”  

    “This is what makes Rishika’s self-retraction so important and such a show of integrity and respect for science,” DiGiorgio told us. “She could’ve been quiet about this and moved on to another project. But she made the right decision. In her reflections on the process, she discussed that getting the real results out there was more important than the personal stakes of a self-retraction.” 

    Porandla is now a rising junior at Princeton studying astrophysics with intended minors in materials science and engineering and engineering physics. She plans to pursue a career developing space plasma detector instrumentation for heliophysics projects.

    Since the retraction, she has been conducting follow-up work to address the weaknesses in the original study and is working toward a corrected version of the article for the journal. The revised manuscript will incorporate a redesigned methodology, stronger validation procedures, and a more comprehensive understanding of the physical mechanisms involved, according to Porandla.  

    “I think this is an important part of my journey with this research because I see that retractions are often viewed as the end of a project,” she said. “In my case, the retraction became the starting point for a better study.”

    She does worry that when applying to fellowships or graduate school, people might wrongly assume the retraction was linked to misconduct or carelessness, she said. But Porandla makes  a point of bringing up the retraction when asked about her research and readily shares her story. She hopes such transparency will set the record straight. At least one researcher, Nathan Georgette, reported no harm to future job prospects after self-retracting. Ctrl-Z Award winner Kate Laskowski recounted the “overwhelmingly supportive” response she received in her field following her self-retraction.     

    “In my conversations with people, I try to make it known that this was a learning experience for me,” Porandla said. “I think that there’s a bit of flexibility that’s given if you have integrity and correct the record yourself.”

    Porandla responded that she hand-drew the figure in her Macromolecular Materials and Engineering manuscript and that it was not AI-generated. She noted the peer reviewer suggested some of the questioned references during the editorial process. (We have seen correspondence between Porandla and the editor confirming this.)     In a separate PubPeer post, commenters raised questions about citations issues and possible data problems in a 2024 preprint by Porandla and two others about autonomous navigation methods for hypersonic aircraft.     Porandla responded that Dasari performed the research and drafted the manuscript and that her involvement was “limited to overseeing the broader research program” in which Dasari was participating.     Since the Retraction Watch story was published, Porandla told us she has continued reviewing her high school research and responding publicly to substantive concerns.

    “The aftermath has been difficult, particularly seeing work I did at a young age in informal environments like science fairs and research competitions scrutinized publicly while I am actively trying to address its shortcomings,” she told us. “However, I understand why these questions are being asked and do not want that discomfort to prevent me from engaging with legitimate criticism. I would rather acknowledge and correct problems now than allow unreliable work to remain unaddressed.”

    Zimbabwe strengthens national commitment to open science at the 6th ZULC Biennial Conference

    The following post is a joint announcement with TCC Africa

    Harare, Zimbabwe | 7 July 2026

    The Zimbabwe Open Science Stakeholder Convening, held as a side event during the 6th Biennial Zimbabwe University Libraries Consortium (ZULC) Conference, successfully brought together policymakers, university leaders, librarians, researchers, research infrastructure organizations, and international Open Science partners to chart a coordinated path toward advancing Open Science in Zimbabwe.

    Hosted at Manna Resorts, Harare, the convening was jointly organized by the Zimbabwe University Libraries Consortium (ZULC), the Training Centre in Communication (TCC Africa), and PLOS, with support from Zimbabwe’s Ministry of Higher and Tertiary Education, Innovation, Science and Technology Development (MHTEISTD).

    The hybrid event attracted both in-person and virtual participants, demonstrating growing national interest in strengthening Zimbabwe’s research ecosystem through Open Science.

    Building Momentum for Open Science in Zimbabwe

    The convening was organized against the backdrop of increasing global and continental efforts to make research more transparent, accessible, collaborative, and impactful. Across Africa, governments, higher education institutions, and research organizations have been strengthening policies and infrastructure that promote Open Science as a driver of research excellence and innovation.

    While Zimbabwe has made important strides in research governance, scholarly communication, and digital research infrastructure, participants acknowledged that many Open Science initiatives had remained fragmented. The convening therefore provided an important platform for stakeholders to align priorities, strengthen collaboration, and begin identifying practical pathways toward a coordinated national Open Science strategy.

    Setting the Regional and National Context

    The convening was moderated by Joy Owango, Executive Director of TCC Africa, who opened the discussions by highlighting the rapid evolution of Open Science across Africa. She emphasized the importance of regional collaboration, policy alignment, and sustainable digital infrastructure in supporting national research systems.

    The official welcome remarks were delivered by Blessing Chiparausha, Chairperson of ZULC, who outlined the objectives of the convening and reaffirmed the consortium’s commitment to strengthening research support services and scholarly communication across Zimbabwean universities.

    Learning from Regional and Continental Experiences

    One of the strengths of the convening was the diversity of perspectives shared by regional and international experts.

    Dr. Sylvance Okoth, Executive Secretary of the East African Science and Technology Commission (EASTECO), shared lessons from East Africa’s Open Science journey, illustrating how regional collaboration, policy harmonization, and institutional commitment had accelerated Open Science implementation across several countries.

    Higher education leadership was further explored by Professor Kuzvinetsa P. Dzvimbo, Chief Executive Officer of the Zimbabwe Council for Higher Education (ZIMCHE), who discussed the strategic role universities play in embedding Open Science into institutional governance, research quality assurance, and national development agendas.

    From a scholarly publishing perspective, Roheena Anand, Executive Director for Global Publishing Development and Sales at PLOS, highlighted practical approaches to increasing equitable participation in Open Science and scholarly communication.

    Institutional readiness was addressed by Dr. Felicia Nkrumah Kuagbedzi, Acting Coordinator for ICT, Communications and Knowledge Management at the Association of African Universities (AAU). She presented AAU’s experiences in supporting African universities through governance frameworks, policy development, capacity strengthening, and regional collaboration initiatives that enable sustainable Open Science implementation.

    Understanding Zimbabwe’s Current Landscape

    A major highlight of the convening was the presentation of findings from Zimbabwe’s national Open Science stakeholder survey.

    Presented by Blessing Chiparausha, the survey offered valuable insights into the country’s current Open Science landscape, identifying both existing strengths and areas requiring further investment. The findings served as the evidence base for the subsequent policy dialogue and will inform ongoing discussions on national priorities.

    Infrastructure readiness was subsequently explored by Mrs. Miriam Chahuruva, Chief Executive Officer of the Zimbabwe Research and Education Network (ZIMREN), who outlined the critical role of national research and education networks in enabling data sharing, collaboration, digital repositories, and broader Open Science infrastructure.

    The discussion was further enriched by Professor Innocent Chirisa, Vice Chancellor of Zimbabwe Ezekiel Guti University (ZEGU), who reflected on the role of universities in fostering institutional cultures that support openness, collaboration, and research excellence.

    Participants also heard from Maria Stella Namyalo, Programme Officer for Science, Technology and Innovation at AUDA-NEPAD, who highlighted continental initiatives supporting Open Science and the African Union’s broader vision for strengthening research and innovation ecosystems across Africa.

    From Dialogue to Action

    The stakeholders convening brought together participants to discuss practical implementation pathways for Open Science in Zimbabwe.

    Guided by findings from the national stakeholder survey, discussions focused on identifying national priorities for Open Science policy development, strengthening research infrastructure, creating incentives for researchers, and integrating Open Science principles into institutional and national governance frameworks.

    Participants emphasized the importance of coordinated leadership across government, universities, research institutions, libraries, publishers, and research support organizations. The dialogue also underscored the need for sustained investment in digital infrastructure, research data management, policy development, and capacity building to ensure long-term success.

    The session concluded with a broad consensus that Zimbabwe has a significant opportunity to build an Open Science ecosystem that aligns with continental initiatives while responding to national priorities.

    Key Outcomes

    The convening achieved several important outcomes, including:

    • A shared understanding of Zimbabwe’s Open Science landscape, including existing strengths and key implementation challenges. 
    • Consensus on priority areas for advancing Open Science, including policy development, sustainable funding, digital infrastructure, research data management, and capacity building.
    • Recognition of policy entry points for embedding Open Science within existing national and institutional frameworks.
    • Strengthened collaboration among government agencies, universities, libraries, publishers, and regional organizations (particularly MHTEISTD, ZULC, ZIMREN) to advance Zimbabwe’s Open Science agenda.
    • Consensus on the need for a national Open Science strategy led by the Ministry of Higher and Tertiary Education, Innovation, Science and Technology Development (MHTEISTD).

    More on the convening discussion can be found here.

    Looking Ahead

    The Zimbabwe Open Science Stakeholder Convening marked an important milestone in the country’s journey toward more open, collaborative, and impactful research.

    By bringing together national decision-makers alongside regional and international partners, the convening established a strong foundation for future collaboration and demonstrated Zimbabwe’s growing commitment to aligning with Africa’s broader Open Science agenda.

    As conversations continue beyond the conference, the partnerships strengthened during the convening are expected to contribute to the development of policies both at the national and institutional levels, including the inclusion of open science principles in national higher education policies or acts, and infrastructure, with continued efforts to raise awareness about the benefits of shared infrastructure for open science.

    Clarivate removes hold on mega-journal Heliyon after internal audit, 650+ retractions

    Clarivate has lifted its hold on Heliyon after the journal’s internal audit led to over 1,000 retractions, expressions of concern and corrections. As a result, the journal will receive an impact factor in the coming cycle, a Clarivate spokesperson said. 

    Clarivate decided in September 2024 new content from Heliyon would not be indexed in its Web of Science due to concerns about quality. The journal told us at the time it was “investigating,” and since then, it has retracted over 650 articles.

    Clarivate lifted the hold on May 14, and Heliyon released a statement shortly after, noting all content published while the journal was on hold will now be indexed. The statement says the audit “revealed behaviors that did not align with our policies.” As we wrote in February, the journal has previously said the concerning practices included compromised peer review, authorship irregularities, tortured phrases and citation manipulation. 

    Rebecca Krahenbuhl, communications manager at Clarivate, told us since all Heliyon’s missing content was backfilled by the August 1 cutoff, it will receive a new impact factor and updated metrics in the October Journal Citation Reports update.

    Being indexed in major databases is seen as a marker of credibility for journals, and some universities and governments create lists of approved journals based on indexes. Authors are less likely to submit to delisted journals, given universities rely on such metrics to judge researchers’ work for promotion and tenure decisions. 

    The journal issued roughly 1,100 “corrections to the record” which include article corrections, expressions of concern and retractions, which the statement said “affect approximately 3% of content published in Heliyon across 12 years of publication.” 

    Queen Muse, head of media and communications at Cell Press, told us the 1,197 updates to the scientific record consisted of 687 retractions, 80 expressions of concern, two withdrawals, three removals, 317 corrigenda and 108 editor’s notes. 

    Heliyon “considers research from all areas of the physical, applied, life, social and medical sciences,” according to its website. The journal published over 11,000 and 17,000 articles in 2023 and 2024, respectively. That number dropped to around 3,000 articles last year. So far in 2026, the journal has published over 500 research papers and case reports. 

    Amy Bourke-Waite, senior director of communications at Clarivate, told us the company would not comment on specific journals, but referred us to an April blog post by Nandita Quaderi, editor-in-chief of Web of Science. In it, Quaderi noted Clarivate expects publishers to “proactively correct the scholarly record when unreliable content is identified, and to continue these efforts even after a hold is removed.”

    Moving forward, Heliyon will “continue to make improvements to the journal’s workflow, including the use of our Check Integrity screening tool with human oversight to help prevent ethical issues in submitted papers, and throughout the publication process,” the May statement reads. “The outcomes of the investigation reflect a systematic effort to correct the scientific record and reinforce trust in the journal’s content.”

    Decade-long fraud by market survey firm a ‘gut punch’ for researchers

    In April 2025, David Victorson, a psychologist at Northwestern University in Chicago, was polishing several manuscripts for submission when a colleague sent him a link to a press release from the U.S. Department of Justice. “Eight Defendants Indicted in International Conspiracy to Bill $10 Million for Fraudulent Market Survey Data,” the headline read.

    Victorson couldn’t believe it. Op4G, a market research company he first worked with more than a decade earlier, was being indicted for selling bogus data between 2014 and 2023. In fact, Victorson had just used them to run an online survey related to meditation as part of a U.S. National Institutes of Health project to create a standardized mindfulness metric. He didn’t yet know whether his own data were compromised, but he knew there was no way he’d be submitting his manuscripts with those data.

    “Because they were within the period of the indictment, we scrapped them entirely,” Victorson said. “It took years off our lives.”

    In the aftermath of the revelations about the fraud, researchers at a half dozen institutions inside the United States have been scrambling to review and correct or update their published studies and unpublished manuscripts.

    In June, The Journal of Adolescent and Young Adult Oncology retracted a 2023 study on the role of social media in supporting young adults with cancer, after the authors notified it of their concerns.

    “While the authors cannot conclusively infer that fraudulent data was provided to them, they have lost confidence in the conclusions drawn and requested retraction of the article,” the editors wrote in the retraction notice. The lead author, Allison Lazard, a professor of media studies at the University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, did not respond to a request for comment.

    Other studies have not been updated swiftly. Stacey Tessler Lindau, a physician-scientist at the University of Chicago, was part of a team that used Op4G to collect data from 3,200 respondents for two studies they conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Lindau did not respond directly to Retraction Watch about whether she remained confident in her data, but she passed our message on to Jeremy Manier, associate vice president for communications and public affairs, who wrote that “If any retractions or corrections associated with the use of that data are appropriate, those steps will be taken.”

    Op4G, which stands for “Opinions for Good” was founded in 2010 with what sounded like an admirable mission: Survey participants would receive cash payment split between them and their favorite non-profit organizations.  

    Because recruiting subjects to meet the requirements for certain projects could be challenging, the company’s founders soon came up with a scheme to generate fabricated data for the clients, according to prosecutors. Employees and their friends and family members would sometimes take the surveys themselves for payment, or they would recruit bogus survey participants from the United States, Serbia, India and Brazil, who they dubbed “ants.” Court records indicate that legitimate survey data were often mixed with their “ant farm” data — a process they dubbed “scrambling the eggs.” 

    In 2018, several Op4G employees and founders decided to move their fake-data business to a new company, Slice MR. The scheme finally unraveled thanks to an internal whistleblower, Kristen Tripphahn, who joined Op4G as chief operating officer in February 2022. 

    “I, like many others, believed Op4G was all about doing some good in this world, but I discovered 6 months in that nothing could have been further from the truth,” she wrote in a Substack post. (Tripphahn declined our request to comment.) 

    Although the majority of the two firms’ clients were companies conducting traditional market research, the list of victims filed in court by prosecutors includes a number of hospitals and universities, including Northwestern, University of Maryland, Wake Forest, University of North Carolina and Northwell Health in New York.

    Victorson, whose work focuses on patient-reported outcome measures, first used Op4G to conduct an online survey related to prostate cancer in 2013 and was pleased with the quality of the data. Because those data were acquired prior to the period outlined in the indictment and there are no obvious red flags, he has no reason to question their validity.

    However, in 2020, he hired the firm for his recent NIH study on mindfulness. He declined to disclose the exact cost of running the survey. When Victorson learned of the fraud, he immediately called the FBI agent involved in the case, hoping to learn more. While not all of the data were fake, the agent told him, there was no way to know for sure how badly his own data was tainted.

    “We were just about to publish our primary paper based on those data when we found out about this, and we stopped everything,” he said. “We have since re-collected all the data and re-analyzed all the data.” Remarkably, he said, the main findings still stand even though the numbers have changed slightly. 

    For Victorson, the worst part of the fraud was that he knew the perpetrators personally. Frank Hayden, the chief operating officer for the survey firm, lived in his town, Evanston, Illinois. The vice president for sales, Ryan Stoudt, stopped in to grab burgers and talk about football when he was visiting. Both men have pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud. (Stoudt did not respond to a request for comment, and Hayden could not be reached.)

    “It’s such a gut punch,” Victorson said. “There are a lot of things that suck about this, but one of the harder things is that it’s not just like someone took my credit card number and bought gas in Minnesota.”
