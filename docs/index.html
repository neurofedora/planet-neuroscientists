    Tennessee researcher heads to court amid claims of theft, forgery and revenge

    In May 2025, Tennessee authorities were preparing to arrest a university math professor for allegedly soliciting university donations from his students and pocketing the money. 

    Investigative records paint the picture of a professor who took financial and personal advantage of his students for years – from manipulating them into paying him rent to making them clean up after his dog – and grew vengeful when they spoke out about his alleged crimes.

    As state investigators closed in, the professor, Don Hong, tried to get revenge against a student who reported him by accusing her of research misconduct and telling a journal to retract a study she recently authored, according to an investigative report from the Tennessee Comptroller of the Treasury. When a grand jury indicted Hong on May 5, 2025, for theft and forgery, it tacked on a retaliation charge for his actions against the student.  

    But later that month, the International Journal of Computer Mathematics retracted a paper about mortgage rates by Ph.D. student Danlei Zhu, an advisee of Hong’s, and four others in the department of mathematical science at Middle Tennessee State University (MTSU) in Murfreesboro. According to the retraction notice, “significant concerns” were raised about the study’s methodology following publication, and an independent external review determined the methodology and statistical analysis “were incorrect” and the conclusions “unreliable.”

    Whether the IJCM paper is the same article referenced in the state’s investigative report is unclear. Taylor & Francis, which publishes the title, would not tell us what prompted the journal’s investigation. Jennifer McMillan, vice president for external communications for Taylor & Francis, told us the company did not have “anything further to add” to the retraction notice. 

    However, the details may soon be revealed in an upcoming court trial. Hong has a status hearing in his theft case scheduled for Sept. 25. He has not yet entered a plea, according to court officials. A jury trial is set for March 1, 2027. 

    Hong was among the faculty who taught and supervised research in the university’s Computational & Data Science Ph.D. program, home to all authors on the retracted International Journal of Computer Mathematics at the time of publication. 

    Hong did not return messages seeking comment. 

    Zhu and her coauthors did not return multiple messages seeking comment. Zhu was vice president of graduate affairs for the university’s Actuarial Math Student Association between 2022 and 2023. Hong, coordinator of MTSU’s Actuarial Science Program, previously served as faculty advisor for the student group. 

    Hong has worked at MTSU since 2005 and remains an active professor of mathematical sciences at the institution, according to a university spokesperson. As part of his role, Hong travels to China to recruit students and create partnerships with other universities, according to the Comptroller’s report. 

    State authorities started investigating Hong after an MTSU internal audit revealed questionable financial transactions between the professor and students. Investigators learned Hong encouraged students to donate to the Actuarial Science Program, but the donations were transferred to Hong’s personal bank account rather than to the university. Between January 2020 and May 2024, Hong stole at least $8,225.48 in university donations, the state alleges. 

    Hong gave conflicting and inconsistent explanations about why he held onto the donations, including that he planned to “gradually” pay the money back, that he planned to invest the funds, and that he planned to give the money to the university at the end of the semester, according to investigators. 

    During their investigation, authorities interviewed various students from China who reported that before coming to the United States, Hong told them of available housing near the university.

    However, Hong concealed that he or his spouse owned the housing, and he used an alias to create fictitious lease agreements with students, according to the Comptroller’s report. The professor acted as a middleman between the property arrangements, but the students were unaware to whom they were paying rent. Some students also told authorities Hong required them to take care of his dog during spring break without payment. Hong also took cash from students for “airport transportation,” which he claimed as a reimbursable expense from the university, according to investigators.  

    A Ph.D. student and research graduate assistant, identified as “Student 4” in the state’s report, was among the students who lived in a rental property operated by Hong. The student went to Hong’s office “multiple times” to report “an act of violence” that occurred at one of Hong’s properties between the student and her roommate and to ask for help, according to the Comptroller’s report. But “Hong advised her not to contact the police, saying it would bring shame because they are Chinese,” according to the report. Hong told the student if she moved out, “she would be responsible for the rent for the whole year.” 

    The student moved out of the property around February 2022, but continued paying Hong until her lease agreement ended, according to the state’s report. She told authorities that in China, “students are taught to listen to their professor(s), and she believed Hong.”

    Shortly after “Student 4” and another student reported Hong’s actions to investigators, a person who called himself “Peter Panelist” began levying research misconduct accusations against her, according to the state. Hong emailed a journal under the alias to report a paper the student wrote contained plagiarism. The email, obtained by investigators, stated:

    “Given the lack of methodological rigor and the apparent duplication of previous work, the paper…should be retracted. It is concerning that such a manuscript was accepted, especially in a reputable journal..”

    Authorities obtained subscriber information for the email account used to send the message and traced it back to Hong. The registration IP address for “Peter Panelist” was an IP address connected to Hong’s university Microsoft account, according to state investigators. 

    “The investigation revealed that the accusations against Student 4 appear to be retaliatory, based on the fact that they were submitted after she reported Hong’s potential misconduct to state investigators,” the report states. “The investigation further showed that the email account for “Peter Panelist” was created approximately four days before an accusation was submitted against Student 4, indicating this account was designed merely to transmit anonymous allegations against her.” 

    The report does not identify which journal investigators contacted or the authors involved in the paper. 

    MTSU would not tell us if the research misconduct allegations against the student and her coauthors  were found to be credible or if it opened an investigation into the concerns. In a statement, the university said the information we requested is “confidential under the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act” and under the university’s students records policy. 

    “Our research misconduct policy also requires that such matters be treated confidentially by the university,” the statement said.

    Wiley investigating book chapter on biodiversity for fake references

    While reading a chapter from a 2024 book on restoring biodiversity in post-industrial land earlier this month, an ecologist came across a reference to a study on rewilding she wanted to look up. 

    But the ecologist, who has asked to remain anonymous for fear of retaliation from the chapter authors, couldn’t find a record of the 2020 paper, purportedly published in Ambio by “Knijn et al.” She decided to check the other references. She told Retraction Watch she found at least eight that didn’t exist. 

    The ecologist flagged the issue to the book’s publisher, Wiley, on September 7 but told us she never heard back, aside from an automated response.

    A Wiley spokesperson told us the publisher “takes integrity seriously and investigates all concerns when they are flagged to us.” They said Wiley would “take appropriate measures once the investigation has concluded.”

    The publisher owned Edifix, a reference-checking service, from 2019 until it was acquired by another company earlier this year. The spokesperson did not answer our question asking whether books, including this one, were run through the program, but said Wiley “has published AI use guidelines for book authors and safeguards the integrity and quality of the books we publish through established checks at three stages: book proposal, contracting, and manuscript review and development. Edifix is not one of the tools that we currently use.”

    The Wiley representative did not respond when we asked if the publisher was aware of issues with the book chapter before we contacted them last week. 

    Bhanu Pandey, one of the four authors of the chapter in the book, Restoring Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services on Post-Industrial Land, told us he wasn’t aware of the fake citations before we reached out. All four authors were researchers at CSIR-Central Institute of Mining and Fuel Research in Jharkhand, India, at the time of the study. 

    Bhanu Pandey told us first author, Sameer Shekhar, “took the lead in compiling the chapter materials and coordinating the submission.” He said he is looking into the book chapter and planning to submit a request for a correction to Wiley: “Where a citation is inaccurate or the associated statement is unsupported, I will propose the appropriate correction, revision, or removal and submit it to the chapter editor and publisher through their formal editorial process.”

    None of the other authors responded to our request for comment, including Shekhar, who we reached out to multiple times on both his professional and personal email addresses.

    Vimal Chandra Pandey (no relation to Bhanu), a researcher at CSIR’s National Botanical Research Institute, edited the book. He told us he would “check in this regard” but did not answer questions asking how the fake references may have been overlooked. Wiley’s page on potential conflicts for editors says they “must refrain from handling manuscripts if they have a COI, like a professional or personal relationship with the authors.”

    Other publishers have dealt with similar cases: After retracting a book on machine learning containing non-existent references following our coverage of the case, Springer Nature said in June they would begin issuing expressions of concern for books. In October, research integrity specialist Michał Wójcik wrote a Python script to identify several books with fake citations, which can be a sign of misuse of large language models.

    Elsevier journal retracts 100+ papers for authorship issues, editor conflicts of interest

    Science of the Total Environment (STOTEN) has retracted 165 papers this year, most for “suspicious” changes in authorship or editorial conflicts of interest. 

    The retractions have come after STOTEN was delisted from Clarivate’s Web of Science in November after the index put it on hold due to concerns about article quality. The journal published over 10,000 articles in 2024; so far this year it has published fewer than 1,000. 

    The retractions, most of which were issued in August, applied to articles published from 2018 to 2025. At least 50 of the papers were handled by associate editors who had “an ongoing collaboration on a co-publication” or “an extensive record of collaboration” with the authors of the articles, according to the notices. 

    Other notices cite changes in authorship between submission and revised or published versions of the paper without approval from editors, a move which can indicate paper mill activity. Other reasons for retraction included plagiarism and tortured phrases and data concerns. 

    Jörg Rinklebe, a soil and groundwater management researcher at the University of Wuppertal in Germany, lost 10 papers, the most for any author. Eight of them were retracted because the paper’s handling editor had previously co-published with him or with other authors, the retraction notices state. In a statement to Retraction Watch, Rinklebe said he did not “appoint, request, or control the handling editors” for his papers. 

    “At the time, I did not understand my previous professional collaborations with the assigned editors to constitute a disqualifying conflict under the policies and practices then applicable,” he said. “I complied with the disclosure requirements presented to me through the applicable submission procedures.” 

    According to Elsevier’s guidance on duties for editors, editors “must not be involved in decisions about papers which they have written themselves or have been written by family members or colleagues.” The page doesn’t define “colleague” but its guidance for reviewers gives an example of a conflict of interest as “collaborations between the reviewer and author(s) in the previous three years.”

    Several of the editors named in the retraction notices have been removed from STOTEN’s website. One of them, former associate editor Daniel Tsang, was named in six retraction notices to Rinklebe’s papers, once as a coauthor and five as an editor. He did not respond to our request for comment. 

    Five of the papers will join our list of nearly 700 retracted COVID-19 articles, three for changes in authorship. One such article, on the environmental life cycle of medical waste during the pandemic, has been cited 100 times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science. 

    One of Rinklebe’s papers was also retracted because two authors were added during revisions, although one was later removed. He did not respond to our question regarding how the changes came about. 

    “What I object to is the retrospective application of responsibility to me for editorial decisions made within Elsevier’s own system, particularly where the system itself raised no objection at the time and where no evidence of interference with the scientific review has been presented,” Rinklebe said. 

    While STOTEN retracted several papers for authorship changes, it corrected at least four others to remove researchers who were “added to the paper without authorisation or validation by the editor at the time of review,” which the notices state was against journal policy. 

    One of the corrections, issued this April for a paper on plant pollutants, removed authors Randeep Rakwal of the University of Tsukuba in Japan and Ganesh Kumar Agrawal of Research Laboratory for Agricultural Biotechnology and Biochemistry Nepal in Kathmandu. The duo lost a paper from another Elsevier journal in 2019 for plagiarism. 

    When we asked why some authorship changes warranted correction and others retraction, a representative for Elsevier told us each case was assessed individually. The representative also said “the contributions of the authors concerned didn’t warrant authorship status, but their contributions were valid, hence the research itself wasn’t retracted but the authorship was corrected.”

    Clarivate recently lifted a hold on Heliyon, another Elsevier journal placed on hold in 2024. In a recent post about the publisher’s investigation, Elsevier said it was strengthening its focus on pre-publication checks and would “redeploy” their post-publication review “for other investigations.”

    Misconduct case dropped against AI expert after claims of AI use in recent book

    Sweden’s national committee on research misconduct has dismissed a case against an expert in the social ramifications of artificial intelligence following allegations her recent book contained hallucinated references and other signs of AI use. 

    The author, Virginia Dignum, a professor in responsible AI at Umeå University, denied references in her book The AI Paradox were fabricated and said she made “ordinary editing mistakes.”

    Frank Drewes, head of the computing science department at Umeå University, told Retraction Watch the institution received a report containing the allegations from an anonymous individual on July 6 and referred the case to the National Board for Assessment of Research Misconduct (NPOF) in Uppsala, in accordance with Swedish regulation.

    NPOF ruled on Sept. 4 that the allegations fall outside their remit and will not be investigated. “The referral is consequently closed, and the complaint is dismissed,” an English translation of the report states.

    In its report, NPOF said Dignum “does not regard the book as research, but rather as a popular science work… No research claims are made in the book. She wrote the book in her spare time, not within the scope of any research funding.”

    Umeå and the misconduct board did not find any evidence to contradict Dignum’s claims. “Based on the description of the book, its content, and its target audience—and in light of the known circumstances of its creation—the Board concludes that it does not constitute research,” the board concluded. 

    Drewes told us the university “will not take any specific measures in response to the current complaint” now that NPOF has closed the case. 

    Dignum has not responded to multiple requests for comment. But in a Sept. 5 post on LinkedIn noting the dismissal, she wrote she was “relieved,” adding the case “points to something the academic community hasn’t resolved: what ‘acceptable AI use’ in writing actually means.”

    In an earlier LinkedIn post published on August 27, she said someone made an anonymous complaint of research misconduct against her to Umeå with two claims: AI-detection tools flagged parts of her book as generated by AI, and the book contained referencing errors.

    Dignum wrote: “All six sources exist; none were invented, and all have since been corrected in the ebook and future print runs.” A few of the erroneous references came from using AI to convert citations into a format known as BibTeX, she added.

    Princeton University Press, which published the book in February, did not respond to requests for comment. The publisher did release an updated version of the ebook with corrections to several references. 

    “These were errors of carelessness, not fabrication. In a sense, is ironic (sic) to see AI being credited, with my own, human, errors,” she wrote.

    Dignum admitted she used AI tools to “check phrasing and suggest alternative wording” and that this is “accepted academic practice.”  

    “The text itself is entirely my own and I take all accountability for it,” she wrote in the Aug. 27 post. “More broadly: it is impossible to prove a text is not AI-generated, since models can produce text resembling almost anything. Such accusations are easy to make and nearly impossible to refute, which is exactly why they should be treated with caution, not as evidence in themselves.”

    What level of AI use in academic writing is acceptable is unclear, said David Resnik, a bioethicist at the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences. The issue in general is in “ethically murky ground,” he added.

    Retraction Watch received similar claims by email in March and then in July and August this year. The person who sent the latter claims used an alias and declined to provide their real name. They denied contacting the university or Dignum but said they contacted the book’s publisher, some academic journals and other data sleuths about the issues. 

    “Whistleblowers are a mixed blessing,” Resnik said. “They can do a lot of good when they reveal illegal or unethical research practices. But they can also do a lot of bad when they are going after things that are maybe minor concerns.”  Institutions receiving reports from whistleblowers must follow up, establish committees and investigate, he added. “Lots of hours of time can be spent following up a complaint that may turn out to be fairly inconsequential,” he said.

    Human brain is NOT two separate organs

     

    A terrible headline on a press release made its way through social media the other day, misleading thousands of people.

     

    Human brain is two separate organs, Stanford Medicine-led research finds

    By Krista Conger

    September 18, 2026

    A new study led by Stanford Medicine found the brain is two separate organs adjacent to one another. The finding could aid research into devastating neurological diseases.

    For centuries, scientists have thought of the brain as a single, unified organ. But new research led by Stanford Medicine reveals that what we call the brain is two distinct organs that evolved independently over hundreds of millions of years.

     

    Except it's not true! 

     

    The senior author Dr. Kyle Loh came to the opposite conclusion, as he noted in a Nature News story:

    “The brain is one organ,” says study co-author Kyle Loh, a development biologist at Stanford... “But it’s built in two different parts that connect and work together.”

     

    There are two types of neural progenitor cells, which is not the same as saying “two separate organs”: 

    The research suggests that one type of precursor cell forms the segment called the hindbrain, which is crucial for basic functions such as heartbeat and breathing. A second type of precursor cell, meanwhile, generates two structures: the forebrain, a central player in higher-order functions such as reasoning and planning, and the midbrain.

     

    And some researchers even say “it's hard to rule out the existence of a single brain progenitor that appears only briefly and gives rise to the dual precursor...”

     

    But it's too late! All the so-called news outlets that faithfully reproduce press releases have parroted the same terrible headline (e.g., The Independent, The Telegraph, Neuroscience News, New York Post, Discover Magazine, etc.). It's on reddit and slashdot [that still exists?] and LinkedIn and Bluesky. 

    And now everyone believes the “two separate organs” story.

    We already live in a post-truth society. why am I upset?
