    Editors of economics journal resign after association renews Taylor & Francis contract

    Forty-one editors, associate editors and advisory board members resigned from their roles at the Review of Social Economy after the Association for Social Economics, which owns the journal, renewed its contract with publisher Taylor & Francis. 

    The resigning members, who cited concerns the publisher would eventually transition the journal to a model based wholly on author publishing fees as a reason for their departure, say they plan to launch their own publication. ASE, however, denies the journal plans to fully rely on author publishing charges. 

    The four coeditors of the journal resigned on July 4, according to an email to the ASE we have seen. Former editor Roberto Veneziani, economics professor at Queen Mary University of London, said that happened two days after the association decided to renew its contract with Taylor & Francis. On July 10, 37 associate editors and advisory board members also resigned, noting they shared the values of the coeditors. 

    The exodus will join our Mass Resignation List at number 56, the seventh we’ve covered this year. Earlier this month, editors of a statistics journal also resigned over article processing charges. 

    Review of Social Economy is a hybrid open access journal, giving authors the option to publish open access for a fee or having their work accessible to subscribers only. Veneziani told Retraction Watch he believed the journal would remain hybrid for at least the next three years. However, he claimed Taylor & Francis “have clarified that it is only a matter of time” before it becomes fully open access and relies on article processing fees paid by authors. Veneziani did not respond to our question asking for more information on how or when Taylor & Francis stated this.

    When we asked the publisher if Veneziani’s claims were true, a spokesperson told us the journal would remain hybrid “for the immediate future.” 

    “The structure of ASE, with its financial dependence on the journal income, has created an unhealthy financial dependence on royalties from the journals,” the former editors wrote in their resignation letter. 

    In an announcement posted last night, ASE said the former editors’ request to move to a fully open access model “would have major implications for ASE’s revenues and operations.”

    “While the editors of RoSE insist that the journal is moving towards a model with author publishing fees (APFs), not subscriptions, this is not the case. The journal will continue to publish on a hybrid model,” the announcement says. 

    In their resignation letter, the former editors said they have been in discussions about the possibility of moving to an open-access model for years. “We have used a considerable part of the editorial budget for ‘buying out’ articles from behind the pay wall, especially those of young scholars or scholars from non-OECD countries,” they wrote.

    Veneziani said “the only time we’ve asked for a discount and/or waiver for authors coming from Africa (for the special issue on African Politics Philosophy and Economics published earlier this year) T&F have declined the request.”

    Taylor & Francis offers waivers for article processing charges for authors in low-income countries, but only for those publishing in fully open-access journals, the publisher told us. 

    Four associate editors remain at the journal, including economist Léonce Ndikumana of the University of Massachusetts Amherst, who told us he was “waiting to hear about the editorial strategy going forward before I decide whether I stay on or move on.” The other remaining editors didn’t respond to our email asking whether they plan on staying. 

    A representative from Taylor & Francis told us the journal “took the decision to maintain the existing hybrid model for the immediate future, ensuring Review of Social Economy remains a publishing option for all researchers, whether or not they have OA funding available.”

    “We respect that the editorial team had a different vision which informed their decision to move on,” the spokesperson said. “The Association for Social Economics and Taylor & Francis thank the outgoing editors and board of the Review of Social Economy for their contributions to the journal.”

    Veneziani said the editors are planning to launch a diamond Open Access journal, the Journal of Social Economy and Philosophy, under Stanford University Press. The journal would be more affordable for authors and readers, he said. Alan Harvey, director of Stanford University Press, confirmed the press is in discussions with the editors about a new proposed journal under the publisher’s Subscribe 2 Open program, which was “specifically established to address the growing concerns around commercial scholarly publishing practices.”

    In its announcement, ASE said the editors of Review of Social Economy wanted to move to “another press” for complete open access. Veneziani told us the other publisher was Stanford University Press.

    Prior to ASE publishing the statement on its website, Rojhat Avsar, association director for the organization, declined our request for comment. “This information was not supposed to be public and is strictly the internal affairs of the Association,” he told us. “Whoever has leaked it is no longer part of the Association (if it is the ex-editor of the journal) and does not have any right to share information that was meant to be circulated only within the ASE board.”

    The coeditors maintained in their resignation letter that a complete open access model “would, with high likelihood, have allowed generating a stable income, while preserving the quality and integrity of the journal. But ASE’s leadership – with its short-term cycles, which make longer-term planning maybe more difficult than it would otherwise be – has decided that it does not want to take this step.” 

    Veneziani told us the short-term cycle referenced the ASE president one-year term: “we’ve now had to deal with a string of Presidents whose only objective was to finish their mandate without much hassle.”

    Exclusive: A couple paid more than $800,000 for a gene-editing therapy for their daughter. She died, and it wasn’t made public

    An investigation by Science and Retraction Watch has uncovered details about a clinical trial that resulted in the death of its sole patient: a 6-year-old girl with a rare genetic mutation affecting her cognitive development. Her death has never been reported publicly, even though the medical team published its preclinical work in Nature earlier this year. Also omitted from the paper and trial records: The family paid more than $800,000 to fund the therapy’s development. The family has asked the authors to withdraw the paper, and experts we spoke to raised issues they say may warrant a retraction. The beginning of the story, by Retraction Watch contributing writer Brendan Borrell, is below, lightly edited for brevity. Read the full story at Science.  

    The 6-year-old girl tugged on her mother’s hand as they pressed through the doors of the hospital in Shanghai. Behind them, her father rolled a large suitcase with everything the child needed for the weeklong stay. She told her parents it felt like they were going on vacation. In fact, they brought her here for an experimental gene therapy. 

    The girl was slipping behind her peers in kindergarten. She still spoke in simple sentences and ate with training chopsticks. Underneath it all was a single mutated DNA base, a T that should have been a C.

    “Your ‘book’ has a small mistake, which has caused you to have a disease that affects your growth,” read the children’s version of the informed consent form from the hospital. “Over time, it can get more serious.” 

    Doctors hoped to repair that mistake while her brain was still building itself. It would be a clinical trial of one, funded in part by $860,000 the parents had scraped together from their own savings and from relatives.

    The parents felt they were in good hands. Xinhua Hospital, which is affiliated with the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, was acclaimed for its pediatrics department. It was the first Chinese institution to perform open heart surgery on infants, and the first in the country to separate conjoined twins. If all went well, the girl would be the first person in the world to receive a gene-editing therapy directed at the brain. It would rewrite the mutated gene in her neurons, restoring the needed DNA base so she could make a vital protein.

    Leading the effort was Zilong Qiu, a neuroscientist at the university’s brain center, the Songjiang Research Institute. At the time, in late March 2025, Qiu was one of several researchers around the world vying to push base editors — a more precise form of the powerful gene editor CRISPR — into custom treatments for children with rare diseases. One month earlier, KJ Muldoon, an infant with a life-threatening metabolic disorder, had quietly received his first intravenous infusion of one such treatment at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia.

    Although news that base editing saved “Baby KJ” would soon rocket around the world — Science named the feat one of the runners-up for its 2025 Breakthrough of the Year — the story of what happened at Xinhua Hospital has remained hidden. An entry for the study posted to ClinicalTrials.gov has not been updated for more than a year. And when Qiu and his colleagues published proof-of-concept animal studies related to the trial in Nature early this year, they stripped the paper of references to the family and its financial contributions, noting only that “bridging the gap between preclinical research and clinical translation remains a significant challenge.”

    That vague language glossed over tragedy: Seven days after the girl’s medical team infused trillions of viruses carrying the recipe for the base editor into her spinal fluid, she died of a severe immune reaction linked to the therapy, Science and Retraction Watch can now reveal.

    According to official documents and accounts provided by the girl’s parents, the hospital had allowed Qiu’s experimental treatment to proceed under a regulatory provision that does not require approval from national regulators. The lax oversight of this recent trial and the failure to publicly report the fatality “shows the gap between what is intended and what has been put in place,” says Joy Zhang, a sociologist at the University of Kent who has written about the pervasive culture of secrecy in Chinese scientific institutions.

    Seven experts in fields including genetics, virology, and bioethics who reviewed details of the Nature study and the clinical trial for Science and Retraction Watch expressed concern that Qiu and his team downplayed the trial’s risks in describing them to the parents, overlooked safety signals in animal studies, and proceeded even though success was unlikely. “This shouldn’t have gone to trial,” says Steven Gray of the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, who develops viruses for gene therapy.

    Gray and several of the other experts are calling for a full review of the images and other data in submitted and published versions of the Nature paper and full disclosure of the study’s funding. Some of the issues might warrant a retraction, they say. Neither Qiu nor his university or the hospital responded to multiple requests for comment for this story, and Nature says it was not aware of the issues surrounding the clinical trial before it published the group’s paper.

    The girl’s parents have decided to tell her story now because they are angry about what they feel is a lack of accountability by the researchers and the institutions. “Learning the reality of these missing safeguards has fundamentally changed how we now view the entire project,” says the father, a software engineer. “We did not realize how unusual and dangerous many of the arrangements were.”

    Journal retracts breast cancer paper 10 years after sleuth flagged issues

    A biomedical journal has retracted a paper by a cancer researcher who sued her former institution over misconduct allegations. The action comes a decade after a sleuth first raised concerns about the article.

    Former Ohio State researcher Flavia Pichiorri criticized how the publication, the Journal of Experimental Medicine, handled the dispute and said the journal suddenly pivoted away from its plan to republish the study with repeated experiments, a claim supported by correspondence seen by Retraction Watch.    

    In 2020, Ohio State University found Pichiorri, who worked in the lab of Carlo Croce, culpable for manipulating and reusing images in four publications, spanning from her time in Croce’s lab through establishing her own lab at Ohio State. Croce was cleared of direct misconduct, but OSU found serious management and oversight failures in his laboratory. Once a high-profile cancer researcher at Ohio State, Croce now has 18 retractions. He has filed several defamation lawsuits unsuccessfully, and reportedly owes his attorneys more than $1 million. 

    In its final report for Pichiorri’s case, the university recommended immediate retraction of two of her papers, including a 2013 article in JEM, and barred her from future employment at Ohio State. 

    On June 30, JEM retracted the paper for data falsification. According to the retraction notice, JEM was notified in 2021 that Ohio State had determined the “RNU6 data in the original Fig. 1, E and F, as well as in the corrected versions, were falsified.” Following a review, the editors decided to retract the article. The notice states JEM notified all authors for whom contact information could be found “but received no response.”

    David Sanders, a biologist at Purdue University in Indiana, said he first contacted the journal in 2016 to report duplication issues in the paper and has pressed journal leaders steadily over the last 10 years to retract the problematic article. A correction was made to the work in 2017, and the journal issued an expression of concern in 2022.   

    Sanders told us that after the initial correction to the JEM paper, he contacted the journal to report new image duplication concerns. After many emails over the last decade, he wrote to the journal again in January after finding additional problems in the paper, including more duplication and images that could not have accurately represented the experimental conditions, he told us. Sanders said he was pleased the journal finally took action, but added the notice did not address all of the errors.

    “The notice just says it was retracted for that original problem which occurred back in 2017,” Sanders told us. “It does not mention the new issues. This is typical. I’ve seen this in numerous cases, where if [the journal] feels they can say that it’s retracted for X, they don’t bother to mention issue Y.” 

    “Journals need to take these issues seriously in a timely fashion and address them correctly,” Sanders told us. “There was no need for this long a lapse before the article was retracted.” 

    Pichiorri, now an independent scientist, disagreed with the retraction and told us the journal overlooked evidence provided to editors that supported the paper’s data. All original manuscript data were independently verified by a forensic expert and submitted to the journal in 2017, Pichiorri told us. 

    At JEM’s request, the authors repeated experiments for figures 1E and F to demonstrate feasibility, including the RNAU6 element at the center of OSU’s concerns, Pichiorri said. In 2023, an executive editor told authors the paper would be retracted and replaced with a new article with the replacement figures and data from the repeat experiments, according to correspondence we’ve seen.  

    Pichiorri told us she was never contacted about the retraction, and the last correspondence she received from the journal was in 2023. 

    “Unfortunately, I have no insight into what occurred after that point,” Pichiorri told us. “The handling of this situation has not only been damaging to the authors but also to the research agencies that invested significant resources and validated the experiments associated with this publication.” 

    Pichiorri stands by the work, adding that “robust science is grounded in reproducibility,” and that to her knowledge, “the scientific conclusions of the manuscript have never been scientifically disputed.” She believes she was unfairly targeted by OSU, despite Croce’s senior authorship on two of the manuscripts investigated.  

    “OSU kept Dr. Croce working there and all the money associated with the grants they received because of his work and found two women that left the institution to use as scapegoats,” she told us.

    Rory Williams, a spokesperson for Rockefeller University Press, told us after revisiting the paper’s expression of concern and further review, JEM “editors no longer had confidence in the data and decided to retract the article.” He declined to speak further about the process and would not address questions about why the journal changed course in its resolution. 

    Croce did not return a message seeking comment. 

    The other paper the university recommended be retracted was a Cancer Cell article that was removed in 2022. A correction was made to a third paper involved in the case. Pichiorri has two other retractions on papers she wrote with Croce. 

    Pichiorri sued the Ohio State Board of Trustees in April 2023, alleging the release of its misconduct findings to journals and to her new employer violated her due process rights, defamed her and inflicted emotional distress. An appellate court dismissed her legal challenge in December.   

    Ohio State University declined to comment.

    Sanders, who has uncovered many research integrity issues over the years and was sued unsuccessfully by Croce, said the message to sleuths and whistleblowers is to keep pushing journals and publishers to correct faulty literature.   

    “It should not be the responsibility of the whistleblower to monitor the quality of the text of a correction,” Sanders said. ”But if you’ve requested one and want to have a thorough improvement to the scientific literature, then you really need to be following up on the corrections to make sure that the journal’s actions and statements address all the issues and accurately report the nature of the correction.”

    Exclusive: Medical student in Nepal behind busy research factory

    A second-year medical student in Nepal is at the center of an international research network churning out everything from systematic reviews and meta-analyses to case reports and database studies, Retraction Watch has learned.

    The student, Raghabendra Kumar Mahato, leverages this network, which includes nearly 1,000 members connected through the WhatsApp messaging platform, to produce large numbers of conference abstracts and journal papers at breakneck speed, according to posts and messages we have reviewed. The work has been presented or accepted for presentation at conferences organized by the American Heart Association (AHA), the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and many other medical societies and has appeared in journals from major publishers.

    But a review of dozens of WhatsApp messages we obtained and an interview with a disillusioned member of the community, called the “Global Clinical Research Consortium,” suggests the network produces fast-churn science, includes a known paper miller and treats authorship as a tradable commodity.

    “We have high-quality SRMA [systematic review and meta-analysis] and CDC [U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention] database studies ready for submission,” Mahato wrote in a recent message regarding the American Society of Clinical Oncology’s Quality Care Symposium in Boston this fall. “Seeking individuals interested in joining as co-authors and, preferably, attending/presenting the accepted abstracts.”

    Conferences often require abstracts to be presented in person, but registration and travel costs can be prohibitive for many in the global south, as can journal publication fees. The materials we obtained show Mahato bartered authorship to people willing to present the network’s research and shoulder the associated expenses.

    “Is it possible for you to present physically in US,” Mahato, who owns the WhatsApp community, asked a member of the network in a message earlier this year. After receiving an affirmative, Mahato replied that in return, “We can offer you authorship from ourside [sic].”

    Representatives from AHA and ESMO confirmed several abstracts coauthored by Mahato have been accepted for their upcoming conferences, and, following our inquiry, will be giving them a closer look.

    Other messages to the community included “urgent” calls for conference presenters and collaborators for submission-ready abstracts, and in one case for an “already accepted paper.” Mahato noted that researchers “wishing to present their own work are also welcome.”  In one message he promised “guaranteed authorship” to collaborators who had publication-fee waivers from the publishers MDPI or Frontiers.

    In an email to us, Mahato, who already has more than 100 publications to his name, denied “authorship is exchanged solely in return for conference presentation.”

    He said presenters were identified in advance for “many conference-based projects” and that presenters were “expected to make meaningful scholarly contributions” such as “reviewing conference submission guidelines,” “critically reviewing the scientific content prepared by collaborators” and “participating in data curation where appropriate.”

    There is no indication in the material we obtained that Mahato sells authorship for money. He rejected the characterization of his organization as a paper mill, adding, “Our objective is to facilitate collaborative academic research involving contributors with different areas of expertise.”

    The Global Clinical Research Consortium belongs to a growing cottage industry of businesses and organizations catering to medical students and junior physicians under pressure to publish, often in hopes of landing prestigious residencies in the U.S. Some of these outfits charge for their services, which may or may not involve some amount of research training, while others say they don’t. All of them aim at the same thing: Helping customers or members pad their resumés.

    Efforts like these can create a disconnect between authorship and publication and the underlying purpose of research, Martina Linnenluecke, director of the Centre for Climate Risk and Resilience at the University of Technology Sydney, told us. “The primary objective of research should not be to simply to secure an acceptance at a conference or journal for career purposes but to answer a meaningful research question through rigorous analysis,” said Linnenluecke, coauthor of the 2025 paper, “The junkification of research.”

    Mahato’s organization works across several medical specialties, including neurology, cardiology, pediatrics and oncology. One of its strengths, according to advertising materials, is “Turning Ideas Into High-Impact Publications.” Based on Mahato’s LinkedIn posts and WhatsApp messages, well over 70 of his conference abstracts appear to have been accepted for presentation, and he has submitted, or is planning to submit, dozens more. He also has more than a dozen full-length studies and case reports in journals such as Wiley’s Clinical Cardiology and Clinical Case Reports and Springer Nature’s Journal of Cardiothoracic Surgery and BMC Neurology.

    Like other organizations of its kind, the Global Clinical Research Consortium produces studies that can be done in front of a computer, such as analyses of open datasets. A recent surge in open science suggests the efforts are being felt at the journal level. In particular, a preprint from January found exponential growth in publications based on the CDC WONDER database, with an increasing proportion of authors from Pakistan and, to a lesser extent, India. According to the preprint, most of the papers bore signs of “being produced from a template, with formulaic titles and identical methods.”

    Mahato’s network could be one source of such publications. Most of his coauthors are in Pakistan. His WhatsApp group is also dominated by Pakistani numbers, although hundreds are from other countries, including India, Egypt, Nigeria, the United States, United Kingdom, Georgia, Peru and South Korea.

    So far, Mahato has published one CDC WONDER study with coauthors in Pakistan and has uploaded two more to preprint servers. More may follow. In a WhatsApp message from this summer, Mahato wrote he needed “5 people who can write CDC manuscripts…all work done ….just need write up and who can complete a cdc paper writing within 4 days!”

    On his LinkedIn page, Mahato wrote that “11+ of our research abstracts have been accepted for presentation” at the European Society for Medical Oncology Congress 2026 in October, and that five abstracts were accepted by the American Heart Association’s Basic Cardiovascular Sciences Scientific Sessions, which took place July 13 to July 16. 

    Vanessa Pavinato, head of communications for ESMO, told us the society was not aware of any questionable practices by Mahato or his network. ESMO officials were able to find seven accepted abstracts coauthored by Mahato, primarily ePosters, Pavinato told us. The society is now reviewing the submissions, she said.  

    “We have immediately started an internal investigation, based on the information you kindly shared,” she told us. “It will take us some time though to get to the bottom of it.”

    Likewise, a spokesperson from AHA confirmed Mahato had five abstracts accepted for their recent conference. “As part of our review when allegations are brought forward, we will work with our volunteer leaders to identify the best action for each case and/or abstract,” the spokesperson said. “This step is currently in progress.”

    At scale, Linnenluecke said, “practices aimed at securing places at conferences or getting articles into journals place significant pressures on already stretched review and conference systems and adds to the growing volume of poorly verified research that makes it much more difficult to identify credible findings.”

    The disillusioned network member we interviewed, Anit Sinha, provided more detail about the network’s inner workings. Sinha recently graduated from Gandaki Medical College in Pokhara, Nepal – the same school Mahato is now attending – and is preparing for his residency. He joined Mahato’s network to boost his CV, but says he became “suspicious” of its practices after he started working on a review paper in “one of the subgroups.”

    After seeing a bulleted outline of the manuscript, which looked like it was generated with AI, Sinha said, he and seven other network members each picked one section they would write “under the direction of an associate admin” whose WhatsApp username was “S.B.” 

    “Neither admin (including Raghabendra Kumar Mahato) wrote any section,” Sinha told us. “Their feedback consisted entirely of formatting instructions. When I asked for substantive content feedback, none was given.”

    S. B.’s number belongs to Shreya Singh Beniwal, an entrepreneurial physician and known paper miller in India. Beniwal was first author on a questionable paper advertised for sale by the erstwhile iTrilon, as we reported in 2024 in an investigation for Science. She also ran an organization on WhatsApp that sold author slots on research papers, we later found. 

    Beniwal, who continues to publish widely, told us she was “not aware of [Mahato’s] WhatsApp group or any of its subgroups” and did “not know any of the administrators or active members of this community.” She could have been “added to the group by someone else or may have joined through a shared link unintentionally.” 

    But screenshots we reviewed show Beniwal was an administrator of a subgroup called “New Cardio topic” along with Mahato and another person in India. She managed the group’s work on various sections of a manuscript on heart disease and often used an authoritative tone with other members, including Mahato. “Get it done by evening,” she told Mahato at one point. “Sure, give me few time [sic],” he replied. The owner of the manuscript in Google Docs was an email she has used on several recent papers.

    On June 4, Beniwal asked the subgroup if anyone was “interested in joining abstracts with us in AHA 6th is deadline so where we are submitting a few more topics [sic].” And on July 19, four days after we contacted her, she announced in a subgroup called “Ongoing Projects Community 6” that she would be starting “only 3-4 topics this month” due to time constraints, and that these collaborations would be “fast-track projects.” 

    After we contacted her, Beniwal’s WhatsApp handle changed from “S. B.” to an asterisk, but she remained in Mahato’s group. Another number in the group also belongs to Beniwal.

    Mahato, who appears as corresponding author on nearly all of his papers, told us his “role extends well beyond managing communication” between network members.

    “I participate in project planning, coordination of collaborators, manuscript drafting, scientific editing, integrating contributions from multiple authors, organizing revisions, ensuring consistency throughout the manuscript, managing submissions, corresponding with editors, responding to reviewer comments where applicable, and coordinating projects through completion,” he said.

    Mahato began publishing last year, shortly after enrolling in medical school in December 2024. Since then, his research career has been meteoric. According to his Google Scholar profile, he already has amassed nearly 120 publications – including an abstract in Neurology on which Beniwal is also an author. The abstract states the authors did a “retrospective comparative analysis of trial and open label extension data,” but it simply rehashes already–published data points, as Sinha pointed out. “They never even did any independent analysis,” he said.

    Posts on his LinkedIn profile show Mahato is a reviewer for several journals from major publishers, including the International Journal of Surgery, Annals of Medicine and Surgery and the International Journal of Surgery Case Reports, all of which belong to a troubled collection of Wolters Kluwer titles. And soon, he told Sinha in a WhatsApp exchange, he will be joining a journal editorial board; he did not specify which.

    Most of Mahato’s early publications are commentaries, but lately he has added several systematic reviews, database studies and case reports to his bibliography. His many conference abstracts have only just begun to appear in supplements of society journals.

    On several of his papers, Mahato appears with an “MBBS” appended to his name, suggesting he already earned his medical degree. Some articles also state he provided “senior academic oversight.” Mahato told us the suffix was supposed to indicate his “academic program of study” and “was never intended to misrepresent my academic status.”

    Sinha said unethical behavior is “quite common” among students and researchers in Nepal, “but not at the scale Raghabendra Mahato is operating at.”
