    Clarivate removes hold on mega-journal Heliyon after internal audit, 650+ retractions

    Clarivate has lifted its hold on Heliyon after the journal’s internal audit led to over 1,000 retractions, expressions of concern and corrections. As a result, the journal will receive an impact factor in the coming cycle, a Clarivate spokesperson said. 

    Clarivate decided in September 2024 new content from Heliyon would not be indexed in its Web of Science due to concerns about quality. The journal told us at the time it was “investigating,” and since then, it has retracted over 650 articles.

    Clarivate lifted the hold on May 14, and Heliyon released a statement shortly after, noting all content published while the journal was on hold will now be indexed. The statement says the audit “revealed behaviors that did not align with our policies.” As we wrote in February, the journal has previously said the concerning practices included compromised peer review, authorship irregularities, tortured phrases and citation manipulation. 

    Rebecca Krahenbuhl, communications manager at Clarivate, told us since all Heliyon’s missing content was backfilled by the August 1 cutoff, it will receive a new impact factor and updated metrics in the October Journal Citation Reports update.

    Being indexed in major databases is seen as a marker of credibility for journals, and some universities and governments create lists of approved journals based on indexes. Authors are less likely to submit to delisted journals, given universities rely on such metrics to judge researchers’ work for promotion and tenure decisions. 

    The journal issued roughly 1,100 “corrections to the record” which include article corrections, expressions of concern and retractions, which the statement said “affect approximately 3% of content published in Heliyon across 12 years of publication.” 

    Queen Muse, head of media and communications at Cell Press, told us the 1,197 updates to the scientific record consisted of 687 retractions, 80 expressions of concern, two withdrawals, three removals, 317 corrigenda and 108 editor’s notes. 

    Heliyon “considers research from all areas of the physical, applied, life, social and medical sciences,” according to its website. The journal published over 11,000 and 17,000 articles in 2023 and 2024, respectively. That number dropped to around 3,000 articles last year. So far in 2026, the journal has published over 500 research papers and case reports. 

    Amy Bourke-Waite, senior director of communications at Clarivate, told us the company would not comment on specific journals, but referred us to an April blog post by Nandita Quaderi, editor-in-chief of Web of Science. In it, Quaderi noted Clarivate expects publishers to “proactively correct the scholarly record when unreliable content is identified, and to continue these efforts even after a hold is removed.”

    Moving forward, Heliyon will “continue to make improvements to the journal’s workflow, including the use of our Check Integrity screening tool with human oversight to help prevent ethical issues in submitted papers, and throughout the publication process,” the May statement reads. “The outcomes of the investigation reflect a systematic effort to correct the scientific record and reinforce trust in the journal’s content.”

    Decade-long fraud by market survey firm a ‘gut punch’ for researchers

    In April 2025, David Victorson, a psychologist at Northwestern University in Chicago, was polishing several manuscripts for submission when a colleague sent him a link to a press release from the U.S. Department of Justice. “Eight Defendants Indicted in International Conspiracy to Bill $10 Million for Fraudulent Market Survey Data,” the headline read.

    Victorson couldn’t believe it. Op4G, a market research company he first worked with more than a decade earlier, was being indicted for selling bogus data between 2014 and 2023. In fact, Victorson had just used them to run an online survey related to meditation as part of a U.S. National Institutes of Health project to create a standardized mindfulness metric. He didn’t yet know whether his own data were compromised, but he knew there was no way he’d be submitting his manuscripts with those data.

    “Because they were within the period of the indictment, we scrapped them entirely,” Victorson said. “It took years off our lives.”

    In the aftermath of the revelations about the fraud, researchers at a half dozen institutions inside the United States have been scrambling to review and correct or update their published studies and unpublished manuscripts.

    In June, The Journal of Adolescent and Young Adult Oncology retracted a 2023 study on the role of social media in supporting young adults with cancer, after the authors notified it of their concerns.

    “While the authors cannot conclusively infer that fraudulent data was provided to them, they have lost confidence in the conclusions drawn and requested retraction of the article,” the editors wrote in the retraction notice. The lead author, Allison Lazard, a professor of media studies at the University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, did not respond to a request for comment.

    Other studies have not been updated swiftly. Stacey Tessler Lindau, a physician-scientist at the University of Chicago, was part of a team that used Op4G to collect data from 3,200 respondents for two studies they conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Lindau did not respond directly to Retraction Watch about whether she remained confident in her data, but she passed our message on to Jeremy Manier, associate vice president for communications and public affairs, who wrote that “If any retractions or corrections associated with the use of that data are appropriate, those steps will be taken.”

    Op4G, which stands for “Opinions for Good” was founded in 2010 with what sounded like an admirable mission: Survey participants would receive cash payment split between them and their favorite non-profit organizations.  

    Because recruiting subjects to meet the requirements for certain projects could be challenging, the company’s founders soon came up with a scheme to generate fabricated data for the clients, according to prosecutors. Employees and their friends and family members would sometimes take the surveys themselves for payment, or they would recruit bogus survey participants from the United States, Serbia, India and Brazil, who they dubbed “ants.” Court records indicate that legitimate survey data were often mixed with their “ant farm” data — a process they dubbed “scrambling the eggs.” 

    In 2018, several Op4G employees and founders decided to move their fake-data business to a new company, Slice MR. The scheme finally unraveled thanks to an internal whistleblower, Kristen Tripphahn, who joined Op4G as chief operating officer in February 2022. 

    “I, like many others, believed Op4G was all about doing some good in this world, but I discovered 6 months in that nothing could have been further from the truth,” she wrote in a Substack post. (Tripphahn declined our request to comment.) 

    Although the majority of the two firms’ clients were companies conducting traditional market research, the list of victims filed in court by prosecutors includes a number of hospitals and universities, including Northwestern, University of Maryland, Wake Forest, University of North Carolina and Northwell Health in New York.

    Victorson, whose work focuses on patient-reported outcome measures, first used Op4G to conduct an online survey related to prostate cancer in 2013 and was pleased with the quality of the data. Because those data were acquired prior to the period outlined in the indictment and there are no obvious red flags, he has no reason to question their validity.

    However, in 2020, he hired the firm for his recent NIH study on mindfulness. He declined to disclose the exact cost of running the survey. When Victorson learned of the fraud, he immediately called the FBI agent involved in the case, hoping to learn more. While not all of the data were fake, the agent told him, there was no way to know for sure how badly his own data was tainted.

    “We were just about to publish our primary paper based on those data when we found out about this, and we stopped everything,” he said. “We have since re-collected all the data and re-analyzed all the data.” Remarkably, he said, the main findings still stand even though the numbers have changed slightly. 

    For Victorson, the worst part of the fraud was that he knew the perpetrators personally. Frank Hayden, the chief operating officer for the survey firm, lived in his town, Evanston, Illinois. The vice president for sales, Ryan Stoudt, stopped in to grab burgers and talk about football when he was visiting. Both men have pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud. (Stoudt did not respond to a request for comment, and Hayden could not be reached.)

    “It’s such a gut punch,” Victorson said. “There are a lot of things that suck about this, but one of the harder things is that it’s not just like someone took my credit card number and bought gas in Minnesota.”

    RFK Jr. questions another publisher on expression of concern for a vaccine paper

    The U.S. Secretary for Health and Human Services is once again questioning a publisher on an editorial action regarding a paper on vaccination.

    Published in Sage Open Medicine in 2020, the article looks at health outcomes in vaccinated versus unvaccinated children. Its authors are Brian Hooker and Neil Z. Miller, both longtime vaccine critics. 

    Sage Open Medicine put an expression of concern on the paper on May 18. “The following article is under investigation,” it reads. “This expression of concern will remain in place until the investigation concludes, and a final decision has been made.”

    Last week in a post on X, HHS secretary  Robert F. Kennedy Jr. wrote, “Today, I sent a letter to SAGE Open Medicine requesting an explanation for its decision to append an expression of concern to the article … without publicly explaining the reasons for doing so.”

    In the screenshots of the letter, addressed to Prajakta Mane, the managing editor of the journal, Kennedy  referenced COPE’s guideline on expressions of concern, which states notices “should “state the reason or reasons for concern.” 

    He asked the journal to provide the “full internal written analysis and/or external scientific feedback” for the expression of concern, as well as “clarification of the ‘reason or reasons for concern’ that presumably justified your journal’s decision.” He gave a deadline of August 15. “To rebuild the American public’s trust in public health and medical science, full transparency regarding these decisions is essential,” he concluded. 

    A spokesperson for Sage said the publisher doesn’t “have any information to share beyond what’s in the Expression of Concern while the investigation is ongoing.”

    Hooker is the chief scientific officer for Children’s Health Defense, a nonprofit founded by Kennedy. Hooker and Kennedy coauthored the 2023 book Vax-Unvax: Let the Science Speak, which contains a chapter discussing the Sage paper at length. Miller is the author of numerous studies and books suggesting vaccines are harmful. Neither Hooker nor Miller responded to our request for comment.

    The Sage Open Medicine paper was one of three articles presented at last December’s meeting of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices that are now under scrutiny. A presentation by Aaron Siri, an attorney who specializes in class action cases, is an adviser to Kennedy and represented him during his presidential campaign, came just before ACIP decided to no longer recommend infants receive the hepatitis B vaccine at birth if the mother tests negative for the virus. The paper has been cited 14 times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science. 

    A complaint sent to the journal in January 2025 that Retraction Watch has seen outlined several concerns with the paper, including questions around conflict of interest and data collection. The physician who sent it anonymously told us they received an acknowledgement but nothing else from Sage until January of this year, after the physician posted one part of the original complaint on PubPeer. Based on publicly available videos of Hooker, the commenter alleges the Pediatric Health Outcomes Initiative, an organization founded by physician and vaccine critic Paul Thomas, solicited Hooker and Miller to write the article and provided data from Thomas’ private practice for it. 

    On July 30, Hooker posted on X, “The journal has yet to tell the coauthors (Neil Miller and me) what the specific allegations are.  Trying to rebut these “hidden lies” is like trying to beat the air…  Thanks SAGE Open Medicine.”

    According to a Substack post by independent researcher Jessica Rose, Hooker heard from Sage’s research integrity department on April 23, “regarding potential undisclosed conflicts of interest and re-use of data from another published article,” referencing the PubPeer comment. A screenshot of the email also alleges Hooker and Miller’s article uses the same dataset in a 2020 article by Thomas that has been retracted. The screenshot cuts off after that allegation.

    Hooker told The Defender, Children’s Health Defense’s news blog, “the investigation stems from conflict-of-interest allegations posted to the platform PubPeer by a handful of pro-pharma actors,” The Defender reported. Hooker told Sage the PubPeer allegations aren’t true, according to the article.

    Kennedy has previously used his platform to try to influence retraction decisions, with one critic suggesting he is “politicizing” the process. His latest letter follows a similar template to one he sent in June about the retraction of another of Miller’s papers, this one on vaccination and sudden infant death syndrome.  

    “Is Secretary Kennedy the best person to write this letter, given the huge (and undisclosed) conflict of interest regarding this specific case?” asked René Aquarius, a sleuth and neurosurgery researcher at Radboud University Medical Center in Nijmegen, the Netherlands, in a post on LinkedIn, referring to Hooker and Kennedy’s longstanding relationship, as well as the book they wrote together. 

    “Is Secretary Kennedy worried that this expression of concern will undermine the claims made in his book? Is Secretary Kennedy only interested in ‘Letting the Science Speak’ when it agrees with him?” Aquarius wrote. “This is a clear example of weaponizing research integrity to actually undermine science. Not surprising, but very worrying nonetheless.”

    A composer published a paper on theoretical physics. His lack of credentials drew criticism.

    Some people compose music or make YouTube videos for a hobby. That’s Brandon Yates’ job — so in his spare time, he tries his hand at theoretical physics. But his only published paper was questioned because of his lack of professional training in the field — despite the physicist who inspired his work saying the work looks legitimate.

    Based in Las Vegas, Yates earns a living as a composer for the animated series called “Death Battle.” He also has had an “intuitive understanding of, and love for, General Relativity for as long as I can remember,” he told Retraction Watch. But he has no formal training in physics and is self-taught, he said. 

    After watching a video lecture series online by Canadian physicist Eric Poisson, in January, Yates built a computer algebra program to look into rules in general relativity used to fuse two spacetimes across a thin shell, Yates said. By March, he had come across a conditional result he believed to be novel.

    He spent a few weeks trying to understand his finding, “and then sat on it a couple more weeks for a literature search and to decide whether to submit a paper despite my station,” Yates told us. He submitted his findings to the International Journal of Geometric Methods in Modern Physics and said the peer reviewers asked only for a few “presentational” fixes, including that he hadn’t used his key words enough. 

    While the paper was under review, Yates posted on X in March asking for an endorsement for a different paper he hoped to post on arXiv, which requires such backing for new authors.

    General relativity is a popular topic for amateur physicists or people working outside their expertise. Regardless of his lack of formal training, Yates’ paper on vanishing surface density in generalized Vaidya spacetimes was published on June 20, listing him as an independent researcher. 

    “It’s probably worth making explicit that I have no incentive to fudge anything. I’m successful in another field and already have a thriving career,” Yates told us.

    The day after it was published, a physicist who declined to give us his full name because he “would like to focus quietly on my academic research without troubles,” emailed the journal to express concern that Yates “appears to have no formal education in any STEM-related field.”

    “This lack of background, coupled with the highly technical nature of the work, strongly indicates that the manuscript was likely generated using an aggressive, AI-assisted workflow by someone outside the scientific community (specifically, a professional musician),” the physicist wrote. 

    Yates told us he used a generative AI tool, but within the confines of what is acceptable at the journal. The AI “was limited to assistance with phrasing in academic register, LaTeX formatting/typesetting, and assistance preparing the Python verification script,” he said. “The mathematical claims are supported by the exact symbolic verification materials and do not depend on AI output.” 

    Francesco Bajardi, co-executive editor at the International Journal of Geometric Methods in Modern Physics, told us the journal’s review of the article “has not identified evidence suggesting improper use of AI, though we cannot state this with certainty.” 

    “However, the lack of affiliation and background are clearly suspicious,” Bajardi said. He told us the journal was no longer investigating the article because “there is no certain proof that the manuscript has been entirely written by AI.”

    Poisson, whose work inspired Yates, told us the paper “doesn’t reveal any red flag that suggests to me a major AI involvement.” He noted the work “describes a rather straightforward exercise in applying junction conditions in general relativity, with no particular interest nor novelty, but I see nothing objectionable about it,” he said. 

    Yates told us “there was never a grand plan here. I found a result I thought might be novel, did my best to turn it into a proper paper, and submitted it through the normal process.” He is now taking online courses so he “can pursue proper accreditation,” Yates said. “A BA, and later if I’m lucky, a PhD in mathematical or computational physics. I’m also actively seeking out a mentor, though I’ve had no luck so far.”

    “I didn’t expect that working without a degree would turn a refereed paper into news,” he said. “I’m pursuing physics because it brings me joy, and submitted the paper when I’d found something.”
