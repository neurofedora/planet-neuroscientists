    ‘Cerebral torment’: IOP retracts nearly 100 conference papers for tortured phrases

    IOP Publishing has retracted nearly 100 papers from three of its conference proceedings after the articles were flagged for containing tortured phrases. 

    Tortured phrases are rephrasings and synonyms — often awkward and nonsensical — substituted in text to evade plagiarism detectors. For example, one of the retracted papers described “savvy lattices” and “shrewd homes” instead of “smart grids” and “smart homes.” Tortured phrases pre-date AI technology. 

    The recent batch includes 29 retractions from Journal of Physics: Conference Series; eight from Conference Series: Earth and Environmental Science; and 43 from Conference Series: Materials Science and Engineering. 

    According to the retraction notices, dated August 27, IOPP’s investigation followed flags from the Problematic Paper Screener and found the articles contain “a number of nonsensical phrases that feature throughout the paper to the extent that the article makes very little sense,” each of the notices states.

    A 2021 paper in Materials Science and Engineering on detecting brain tumors retracted as part of the efforts used the term “cerebral torment” in place of headache. Another from the same year published in Journal of Physics used “armada the board” in place of fleet management. 

    In some cases, the notices state the authors “neither agree nor disagree with the retraction,” whereas others say the publisher “would welcome receiving the authors’ version of events.”

    B Swapna and Kamalahasan Murugan of Dr MGR Educational and Research Institute in Chennai, India, are coauthors on three of the retracted papers from Materials Science and Engineering, the most for any authors in the batch. Neither responded to our request for comment. 

    To date, IOPP has retracted upward of 1,000 papers for tortured phrases, said Guillaume Cabanac, a computer scientist at the University of Toulouse in France and one of the creators of the Problematic Paper Screener. 

    Rachael Harper, head of marketing communications at IOPP, which is owned by the Institute of Physics, told us in the time since the papers were published, the publisher has “strengthened our editorial and peer review processes for our conference proceedings programme, investing in enhanced submission and monitoring technology, expanding the specialist team responsible for overseeing conference proceedings, and strengthening our Research Integrity team.”

    The Problematic Paper Screener has flagged other papers from the same journals that have yet to be retracted, including one that uses the phrase “flag to clamor” instead of the established phrase “signal to noise” and another that used “brilliant gadget” rather than “smart device.” A 2020 paper on machine learning algorithms from Materials Science Engineering, which has been cited 11 times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science, uses the phrase “Nearest Buddies” in place of (k-)nearest neighbors. 

    Harper did not respond to a question about whether the publisher planned to retract more papers flagged for tortured phrases. Cabanac told us another representative from IOPP “acknowledged that there is more on the [Problematic Paper Screener], and that they’re working on it.”

    OpenAI withdraws three preprints a day after releasing 722 manuscripts on unsolved math problems

    Less than 24 hours after OpenAI posted over 700 preprints describing major math results by an internal AI model, the company has withdrawn three of the manuscripts for errors. 

    On Oct. 6, OpenAI published 722 preprints in a GitHub repository about purported progress in solving 372 math problems in geometry, computer science, algebra and other subjects. The company called the release an effort “to push the frontier of human knowledge and enable further progress in mathematics.” 

    But on Oct. 7, OpenAI announced the withdrawal of three manuscripts because of a sign error. The error invalidated an argument in one manuscript and the construction used by two dependent papers, according to the announcement. Each of the withdrawn papers carry notices explaining “the gap,” and that OpenAI has “revised 14 other manuscripts with proof repairs, corrected statements, clearer hypotheses and dependencies, and one correction to an obsolete citation.” 

    That an error cascaded into three of the OpenAI manuscripts is not surprising, said Alex Townsend, an associate professor of mathematics at Cornell University in Ithaca. He suspects there will be more errors found, he said. 

    “Given the skepticism of AI in the mathematics community, I think that OpenAI should have announced the manuscripts that were lean verified first,” Townsend told us, referring to Lean, the open-source programming language that checks math proofs. “They could have made a separate announcement for the other ones and ask for help verifying them. This would have been more clear to the community and general public.”

    A spokesperson for OpenAI told us the company identified the errors during an audit. As with “other research manuscripts,” the process is iterative, the spokesperson said.

    “We welcome scrutiny and feedback from the mathematical community,” the spokesperson told us “Where errors are identified, we will work to correct them promptly and withdraw papers if no fixes can be found. We want to give the community time and space to assess the work, and we’re continuing to improve the clarity and presentation of future papers.”

    In a post on X, Dan Roberts, a research lead for the company, announced the withdrawals and wrote OpenAI will “continue to update the repo with new formalizations and with any errata we notice.”

    The OpenAI spokesperson told us the company’s collaborators at the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence recommended releasing the results without waiting for “full formalization,” and that roughly 50% of the results were released unconfirmed. 

    “We hope to learn from feedback on this release and work with the AGMAI to understand if they have updated recommendations for release protocol moving forward,” the spokesperson said in an email. “Our intent is to follow their protocol as best we can.”

    Andrew Sutherland, a senior research scientist in the department of mathematics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, said he appreciated OpenAI acting quickly after finding the errors, calling it “the responsible thing to do.” 

    However, Sutherland emphasized that many mathematicians are “unhappy with how OpenAI has behaved up to this point,” especially in its handling of the Sept. 8 announcement an AI system had solved the Navier-Stokes problem, which asks whether a series of equations describing how gases and liquids move become unreliable at some point. 

    Following the release, a group of leading math researchers expressed concern that OpenAI rushed the Navier-Stokes announcement “leaving no time for a proper writeup, the isolation of new methods and ideas, and citing relevant previous work of others.” More than 8,000 researchers have endorsed the sentiments.  

    “I think the withdrawals and corrections will be viewed positively by the mathematics community, but it will take a lot more than that to earn back the trust they have lost,” Sutherland told us. 

    The Association for Human Mathematics criticized the most recent release as well. “Mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done,” the group’s statement, released Oct. 7, said. “Releasing over 700 files at once is not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power. We urge mathematicians to discontinue their work with OpenAI and to return to a vision of science that centers human understanding.”

    A paper suggested a paleontologist’s fossil was forged. He wants it retracted.

    Nicholas Longrich would like to set the record straight. 

    The paleontologist at the University of Bath in England and his colleagues described what they believed to be a new species of marine reptile in 2021, based on a Moroccan fossil found in a mine. But in 2024, another group of researchers questioned the fossil’s authenticity, pointing out the arrangement and orientation of its teeth “most likely result from forgery” based on published photos and documented alteration in other specimens from that region.

    Late last year, Longrich and colleagues published CT scans of the original fossil as well as a new specimen of the species, saying it demonstrated their discovery was genuine. Longrich told us he sent the raw CT data confirming the sample “is authentic” to The Anatomical Record, which had published the critique, seeking an expression of concern or retraction of that paper. 

    “And then they refused,” he said. The Anatomical Record, its publisher and the authors of the paper maintain a retraction was not warranted. 

    “Being concerned about a forgery is a legitimate concern in paleontology,” said Michael Caldwell, a professor at University of Alberta and coauthor of the 2024 paper. Longrich and his coauthors addressed “that perfectly legitimate concern by doing what they should have done in their original study,” he said: “They published CT scans in the second paper to indicate that the type specimen was not forged by the fossil seller.” 

    But producing the scans “does not mean that our concerns that the specimen might have been forged were wrong or unethical, and that they merit a retraction of our paper,” Caldwell said.

    In an emailed statement, Heather Smith, the editor of The Anatomical Record, told us “changes in knowledge don’t conflict with the integrity of the scientific record. In fact, disproving earlier work is a fundamental part of the scientific method. It’s not the same thing as the earlier work being flawed or in major error. It wouldn’t make sense for the publication industry to go back into the record to publish corrections or retractions every time new evidence comes to light.”

    Xenodens calminechari was a small mosasaur with an unusual row of saw-like teeth that lived 66 million years ago. When Longrich and his colleagues first described it in 2021 in Cretaceous Research, the discovery drew a lot of attention, as the specimen had a unique set of teeth that had never been seen before in tetrapods. The paper has been cited 25 times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science.

    Longrich has named more than a dozen species of dinosaur and mosasaur in recent years. He has also faced scrutiny over his conduct; in 2018, the Leverhulme Trust revoked a £1 million (about $1.3 million USD) grant awarded to him, following an investigation that found he had breached the anti-harassment policy at the University of Bath.  

    Shortly after the Xenodens paper was published, a group led by Henry Sharpe, now at Harvard University, became concerned about modifications to the specimen, a partial jaw piece bearing just four tooth crowns, due to the unique placement of teeth and inconsistencies in the nomenclature used to describe the species.  They took a close look at the specimen photos in the original paper, as well as at photos of another mosasaur fossil known to have been altered that was purchased from the same area in Morocco.  

    Such specimens “don’t come from a place where the fossils coming out are scientifically collected, consolidated and prepared,” Sharpe added. “They’re excavated by miners,” and such fossils are then sold to scientists, museums and collectors, and so should be carefully examined. 

    Questions about the authenticity of commercially sourced fossils are not new to the field of paleontology. Researchers in the past have documented fossils that were artificially enhanced or assembled from genuine but unrelated pieces. To combat this issue, techniques such as X-rays, CT scans and chemical analysis have become the standard to identify alterations. 

    “The main problem is when it comes from commercial sources, mainly from Morocco, which is a big producer of these items, is that the fossil is not fake in a sense that it is a true fossil,” said Octávio Mateus, a paleontologist who has written about determining the authenticity of fossils. “The problem is that they are assembled in a fake way.”

    “The handicraft is excellent for a non-expert, and even for some experts, it’s sometimes hard to tell. Basic forgeries are easy to detect but the exquisitely well done ones, those are more challenging,” he continued. “So if you are publishing something from the commercial sources in Morocco, as in a scientific paper for a new species, in that particular case, I would say you should verify it and double verify all the time.”

    Longrich acknowledged issues with fossils coming from Morocco, with the specimen in question being collected from the Sidi Chennane phosphate mine. But Longrich maintains that the CT scans prove the authenticity of the fossils.

    Sharpe, Caldwell and Mark Powers, of the University of Alberta, wrote up their concerns and submitted a paper to The Anatomical Record. In the peer review process, reviewers pointed out the paper lacked evidence the authors had examined the specimen. Sharpe reached out to Longrich seeking access. In response, Longrich asked, “Can I ask what your interest in the specimen is, i.e. are you writing a paper and if so what’s your angle?” according to emails reviewed by Retraction Watch. 

    Sharpe told us they “viewed his request as unethical.” Caldwell separately reached out to Nour-Eddine Jalil, a coauthor on the original paper, for access to the fossil, and was denied. 

    “We were contacted about the specimen,” Longrich acknowledged. “And I got this email about it and they were talking about tooth implantation, but they were asking about where this specimen came from. And well, this is kind of odd.”

    In the December 2024 paper, the team questioned whether teeth in the Xenodens specimen were native to the jaw, citing CT scans of another Moroccan mosasaur fossil from the same region that showed artificially implanted teeth, using a “gummy” substance to glue the teeth in place. They called for CT scans of the original reference fossil, or holotype, to resolve the question. “The holotype material of X. calminechari is likely a forgery,” they wrote.

    The paper drew its own media coverage, with headlines like “Fossil claimed to be new species of mosasaur is suspected forgery.” 

    Longrich told us he didn’t know their paper was being published, and when it was, his university email wasn’t working due to “administrative dysfunction,” so he “didn’t see the emails and didn’t get to respond to it,” he said. “And it’s not that I wouldn’t have said anything.”

    “I used to believe that you let the evidence speak for itself. And the thing is, evidence can’t speak. So somebody’s got to speak for the evidence,” Longrich said. 

    Longrich and his colleagues published CT scans of the holotype and a second additional Xenodens specimen in Diversity in 2025, saying the scans confirmed the teeth were original to the jaw and the material Sharpe et al. had identified as a possible adhesive was fossilized tissue covered in Paraloid B-72, a standard conservation material. 

    Longrich reached out to Smith, editor-in-chief of The Anatomical Record, to ask for a retraction or an expression of concern on the 2024 paper. The journal denied both options.  “A thorough investigation of the available evidence indicates that the peer review process for the Sharpe et al. Xenodens paper was fair and objective, the analyses conducted on the comparative specimen were sound, and that no official action is warranted on the part of the journal,” according to emails seen by Retraction Watch. 

    Sharpe and his coauthors stand by their work. “I think our paper speaks plainly to itself, and the fact that Anatomical Record and Wiley have both looked at it speaks to the fact that our paper is sound,” Sharpe said. “Why get rid of the paper criticizing you if you were right in proving the criticism wrong?” 

    Acknowledging that the CT scans proved the fossil was authentic, Caldwell said, “but proving it was not a forgery is not science. It is merely a factual proof of object integrity.” 

    Smith told Longrich he was welcome to submit a response or follow-up study with new data to the journal, “but he did not take me up on the offer,” she said. 

    About the process of debate and rebuttals, Caldwell told us, “I genuinely do, at the level of philosophy, think that these sorts of conversations in the literature are critical. If you aren’t testing other people’s ideas and their approach to data, then you’re not actually performing philosophically and methodologically, what I think, science is all about.”

    Wildlife journal issues corrections for missing ethics statements

    In what one sleuth called a “systemic failure,” a wildlife journal has issued corrections to a handful of papers not for an error in the articles but to include an item that wasn’t: ethics statements. 

    The most recent such correction in the European Journal of Wildlife Research (EJWR) was on September 8. According to the notice, authors of a March article on mammal diversity in Mexico provided additional information on the camera trapping method employed in the study —  namely, that their research did not require approval from an animal ethics committee. 

    At least three other similar corrections have appeared in EJWR in the last four years, with several more articles with missing ethics statements flagged on PubPeer by commenter “Pseudotocinclus tietensis.” For example, for a 2021 paper on “understanding human controversies on the return of the wolf through the use of socio-cultural values,” which included a survey of nearly 1,500 people, Pseudotocinclus pointed out the ethics approval section for the paper says “Not applicable.” 

    The submission guidelines on the journal’s website state authors should include a section on “compliance with ethical standards” that addresses informed consent and approval for research involving humans or animals.

    “Taken together, the pattern suggests a systemic failure at EJWR to enforce basic ethics policies, both through inadequate corrections for missing ethics declarations and through the publication of papers that lack any meaningful ethical oversight,” Pseudotocinclus told Retraction Watch when they emailed us to flag the issue. They have asked to remain anonymous because of past correspondence with one of the editors. 

    Jacqueline Frair, editor-in-chief of The Wildlife Society’s Journal of Wildlife Management, told us ethics and animal care statements “are an important component of scientific transparency, particularly in wildlife research where studies may involve animal capture, handling, monitoring or interaction with people and communities.” She declined to comment on whether the cases at EJWR indicate a systemic issue, given she is not familiar with the journal’s editorial history. 

    Jacco Flipsen, the vice president of life sciences at Springer Nature, which publishes the journal, told us he would “caution against drawing conclusions about the journal’s processes from a small number of unrelated cases.”

    The journal issued its first correction addressing the missing ethics statements in May 2022, stating the authors “failed to include the complete ethical statement” for a paper on animals in a national park in Senegal. In addition to noting the authors took ethical considerations into account, the updated statement said: “It was confirmed by the Senegal Ministry of Health that no ethics approval was required.”

    The second was for a 2020 paper on movements of goat herds in Mexico. The correction, published May 2024, states “it was not necessary to request permission from national research ethics committees since the vertebrate species studied is not regulated and not native to Mexico.” 

    The journal published the third correction in July 2025 for a study published earlier that year on human-wildlife conflicts in areas adjacent to a research center in Zimbabwe. The statement says the research was guided by ethical guidelines “which prioritized ethical treatment of participants, ensuring no physical or psychological harm” and noted no formal approval was needed. 

    Pseudotocinclus told us they flagged each of these papers to the journal directly before discovering the PubPeer platform in October 2023.

    For a 2022 article about predator control by gamekeepers, Pseudotocinclus wrote the study “does not comply with normal standards of animal ethics, and was not ethically reviewed before the killing of animals was carried out for research.” 

    Flipsen told us the research in the publications “was conducted in different countries, under different regulatory and ethical frameworks.” 

    “Each case must therefore be assessed individually in light of its specific circumstances,” he said, and noted assessments of the papers were ongoing. 

    When we asked whether checking for ethics statements was a part of the editorial process, and how the papers were published without them, a representative for Springer Nature declined to comment. 

    Earlier this month, we wrote about the retraction of a PeerJ paper on tourists’ reactions to snakes for ethical oversight issues — the second snake retraction we’ve covered. 

    A paper on wildlife in Tanzania recently received a correction from EJWR for another reason: “Nelson Mandela” was “incorrectly” listed as a coauthor on a paper published the month prior rather than as the affiliate institution “Nelson Mandela African Institution of Science and Technology.”

    Editor-in-chief resigns amid probes of alleged citation manipulation, data errors 

    The editor-in-chief of The Egyptian Rheumatologist has resigned following claims of questionable citation practices and improper peer review at the journal. 

    At least three publishers are also investigating alleged data errors in papers in other journals by the former journal editor, Tamer Gheita, and his research group. Gheita, a professor of rheumatology at Cairo University in Giza, has denied the claims, calling the complaints “subjective” and “malicious.”

    The Egyptian Society of Rheumatic Diseases (ESRd) started investigating Gheita in June following concerns about his editorial practices at the society’s journal, including unusually rapid peer-review timelines and questionable citation patterns, according to Bassel Elzorkany, interim editor-in-chief for the journal. Elzorkany told Retraction Watch the ESRd board temporarily suspended Gheita, and Gheita resigned shortly afterward. 

    “We acted promptly once concerns were raised,” said Elzorkany, secretary general for the society. “As the investigation is still in progress, we cannot characterize its outcome or comment further on a specific allegation at this stage.” 

    An anonymous complaint sent to us accused Gheita of allegedly inflating his citation metrics by forcing authors to cite his work in the journal and accepting articles for publication in days, without proper peer review.

    Gheita told us he did not “instruct, pressure, or encourage authors to cite” his publications for “reasons unrelated to scientific relevance.” In an email, Gheita said he served as editor-in-chief for the journal from 2013 to July 2026. According to journal records Gheita was previously an associate editor for the journal and became editor-in-chief in July 2012. 

    “I do not agree that the available citation patterns establish a scheme to manipulate citations,” he told us. “Authors determine the references included in their manuscripts based on the scientific literature relevant to their work. There was no journal policy requiring or encouraging authors to cite me or any other specific individual.”

    Gheita said he conducted his own analysis of four journal issues published between October 2025 and June 2026, in which he identified 2,009 references in total. Of these, 69 references (3.4%) cited papers with him as a coauthor. References to articles published in The Egyptian Rheumatologist represented 117 of the 2,009 references, or about 5.8%, Gheita said. 

    But according to data in the anonymous complaint and confirmed through an analysis by Mariana Ribeiro, Retraction Watch Sleuth in Residence, between January 2011 and January 2026, 49% of articles in The Egyptian Rheumatologist cited Gheita’s publications. In  2011, there were no citations to Gheita’s work, rising to 12% of papers published in 2012 and up to 73% of those from 2020, according to data verified by Ribeiro. 

    A 2015 article in the journal about lupus, for example, cites Gheita at least 10 times. 

    During Gheita’s tenure, the journal also accepted hundreds of articles within three days of submission, many on the same day. A paper about Henoch–Schönlein purpura published online in July 2016, for example, shows a submission date of May 1 and an acceptance date of May 1. Another paper, about arthritis, shows the journal received the paper Nov. 30, 2022 and accepted it the same day. 

    Gheita said he would “caution” against drawing conclusions about peer review solely from dates displayed in public databases or submission systems. Historical publishing workflows and changes in electronic submission systems can result in dates appearing “deceptively close together,” he told us. 

    “A conclusion that a manuscript was accepted without peer review should, in my view, be based on the actual editorial records and workflow history held by the publisher, rather than solely on publicly available submission and acceptance dates,” he told us. “The underlying editorial history, including previous versions, transfers, reviewer assignments, and editorial decisions, would be the appropriate evidence for determining what actually occurred.”

    Elzorkany told us the ESRd board referred the anonymous complaints about Gheita’s work to the journal’s publisher, Elsevier, and requested a full investigation.  

    A spokesperson for Elsevier told us the publisher is “aware” of the claims involving the journal and that “an investigation is ongoing.” 

    “We cannot provide further information until this is complete,” the spokesperson told us. 

    Meanwhile, a sleuth has raised concerns on PubPeer about at least 13 papers that include Gheita as an author, involve subsets of his data or encompass his common research group.  The Egyptian Rheumatologist published one of the flagged papers and the other 12 were published in other journals. 

    After receiving an anonymous complaint, psychologist Nick Brown started investigating the research group earlier this year, finding what appears to be “numerous inconsistencies and errors in the statistical reporting,” he told us. The papers in question were published from 2019 to 2026 in various journals, many with large authorship groups and overlapping authors.

    Brown told us the article problems include “impossible p values,” issues with data formatting and rounding, and overall numbers that aren’t compatible with subgroups in the data, among other issues. 

    “I find the whole set of articles thoroughly implausible,” he told us.

    For example, Brown wrote on PubPeer that for seven measures in a 2021 article in Lupus about systemic lupus erythematosus children in Egypt, the overall mean doesn’t make sense  when compared to other data in the paper. In addition, 43 values in three columns of a data table are mathematically impossible given the number of participants in the study, according to Brown’s analysis.  

    Similarly, for three measures in a 2026 article in Scientific Reports about systemic sclerosis in children, the overall mean and standard deviation are inconsistent with other data in the paper,  Brown wrote on PubPeer. He noted further problems with incorrect or incompatible values in the paper. Brown also reached out to several journals to share his concerns, he said. 

    Gheita said he takes the PubPeer concerns “seriously.” He and his coauthors are reviewing the comments and will “respond to substantiated concerns on the basis of the underlying evidence,” he told us. 

    “If an error is confirmed, we support addressing it through the appropriate scientific and editorial processes,” he said. “At the same time, we have not received formal comments or requests for clarification from the journals concerning several of the matters referred to in your email.”

    Lupus, a Sage title, published some of the flagged articles. A spokesperson for Sage confirmed the articles are under investigation, but declined to share further information. 

    Scientific Reports, Rheumatology International and Clinical Rheumatology published several other flagged papers. Tim Kersjes, head of research integrity for Springer Nature, which publishes the three titles, told us Springer is “aware of concerns with these papers” and is “investigating them carefully.” 

    “We will take editorial action as appropriate once our investigations are complete,” Kersjes told us. 

    Wolters Kluwer, which publishes the Saudi Journal of Medicine and Medical Sciences, where one of the flagged papers appears, declined to comment, citing its policy not to comment on “ongoing editorial matters.” 

    A spokesperson for Taylor & Francis confirmed the publisher is investigating another paper in Open Access Rheumatology: Research and Reviews.

    Ahmed Elsaman, a professor of rheumatology at Sohag University in Egypt and an author on  seven of the papers, called some of the issues raised on PubPeer “relatively limited technical observations.” The publications being scrutinized originate from the Egyptian College of Rheumatology Study Groups, a longstanding multicenter research collaboration involving rheumatologists and investigators from across Egypt, Elsaman said in an email. 

    Since 2016, the groups have generated clinical and epidemiological data on several rheumatic diseases through large, multicentre cohorts, according to Elsaman. He pointed to a 2023 article by the group in Rheumatology International that included 10,364 patients recruited from 26 specialised centres across 22 Egyptian cities. (Brown critiqued this paper on PubPeer.)

    “This is just an example to show how huge is our work,” Elsaman told us. “We fully welcome legitimate post-publication scientific scrutiny. Large multicentre studies may contain typographical, transcriptional, reporting, or statistical errors, and we are prepared to investigate and correct any genuine error through the appropriate editorial process.”

    But Elsaman said his concern is the “apparent escalation of relatively limited technical observations into implications of fabrication or research misconduct without evidence proportionate to such serious allegations.”

    Elsaman added he and his research group attempted to respond to the critiques on PubPeer, but he said the replies were not accepted.  

    Samar Tharwat of Mansoura University in Egypt is an author on at least 11 of the papers critiqued by Brown, and Faten Ismail of Minia University in Egypt is an author on at least nine of the articles. Neither Tharwat nor Ismail returned messages seeking comment.  We contacted authors appearing in five or more of the papers. Elsaman is the only author who returned our messages. 

    Tharwat is also the author of a retracted 2023 COVID-19 paper in Open Medicine. Editors withdrew the work because of “significant overlap with other papers published by the same authors in Journal of Clinical Virology Plus,” according to the retraction notice.
