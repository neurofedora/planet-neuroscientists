    Human brain is NOT two separate organs

     

    A terrible headline on a press release made its way through social media the other day, misleading thousands of people.

     

    Human brain is two separate organs, Stanford Medicine-led research finds

    By Krista Conger

    September 18, 2026

    A new study led by Stanford Medicine found the brain is two separate organs adjacent to one another. The finding could aid research into devastating neurological diseases.

    For centuries, scientists have thought of the brain as a single, unified organ. But new research led by Stanford Medicine reveals that what we call the brain is two distinct organs that evolved independently over hundreds of millions of years.

     

    Except it's not true! 

     

    The senior author Dr. Kyle Loh came to the opposite conclusion, as he noted in a Nature News story:

    “The brain is one organ,” says study co-author Kyle Loh, a development biologist at Stanford... “But it’s built in two different parts that connect and work together.”

     

    There are two types of neural progenitor cells, which is not the same as saying “two separate organs”: 

    The research suggests that one type of precursor cell forms the segment called the hindbrain, which is crucial for basic functions such as heartbeat and breathing. A second type of precursor cell, meanwhile, generates two structures: the forebrain, a central player in higher-order functions such as reasoning and planning, and the midbrain.

     

    And some researchers even say “it's hard to rule out the existence of a single brain progenitor that appears only briefly and gives rise to the dual precursor...”

     

    But it's too late! All the so-called news outlets that faithfully reproduce press releases have parroted the same terrible headline (e.g., The Independent, The Telegraph, Neuroscience News, New York Post, Discover Magazine, etc.). It's on reddit and slashdot [that still exists?] and LinkedIn and Bluesky. 

    And now everyone believes the “two separate organs” story.

    We already live in a post-truth society. why am I upset?

    Paper mill studies get cited by clinical guidelines, policy docs and more, analysis finds

    Studies produced by a suspected paper mill are frequently cited in patents, clinical guidelines and policy documents, according to a new analysis. 

    The work, posted on arXiv on August 31, examined the fate of nearly 2,000 academic papers with one common factor: At least one coauthor lists the Pharmakon Neuroscience Network (PNN) as an institutional affiliation. 

    But there’s one problem: the PNN is not a real network of scientists. Instead, it’s an authorship-for-sale scheme, as Retraction Watch reported in 2022. 

    Papers listing PNN as an affiliation started to surface in academic papers in 2019 and often included coauthors based in several different countries. Author lists with researchers who haven’t collaborated in the past, don’t have any past common coauthors, or who work in entirely different fields are a sign a paper may originate from a paper mill. 

    To learn what influence paper mill studies may be having, Federica Silvi and Leslie McIntosh of scholarly services firm Digital Science in London studied 1,975 articles by PNN-affiliated authors and examined where those papers were cited. 

    Using the scholarly database Dimensions, the authors found that 480 papers mentioning PNN were cited in patents, 57 were referenced in policy documents, and 12 appeared in clinical guidelines. 

    “It’s a problem for sure,” said Malcolm Macleod, a neurologist and research integrity expert at the University of Edinburgh in Scotland, referring to paper mill studies receiving citations.  

    Another recently published study suggests that just over 400 U.S. patents contain references to retracted papers. 

    Most of the citations in clinical guidelines were to papers coauthored by pharmaceutical researcher Shaker Mousa, who was found to have faked data in two papers in a 2024 investigation by the U.S. Office of Research Integrity. By our count, Mousa — who was formerly the vice provost for research at the Albany College of Pharmacy and Health Sciences in New York — has at least 16 retractions to his name. 

    But it’s not clear if those mentions in clinical guidelines mean the recommendations were directly based on evidence from the problematic papers, McIntosh, vice president of research integrity and security at Digital Science, told Retraction Watch. “Clinical guidelines also have a lot of citations generally,” McIntosh said. “We don’t have a silver bullet saying that this information is misdirecting clinical guidelines severely.”

    Macleod said one would need to consider how the guideline would have been different if the paper mill study didn’t exist. Many such studies churn out results in line with what the rest of the literature reports, he noted, in which case, it wouldn’t make much difference if these studies were cited in the guidelines. Still, “in principle, it’s highly problematic,” he said. Putting an expression of concern to any clinical guideline citing a questionable study would be a good step, he added. 

    Silvi and McIntosh also tried to assess how often grant applications cite paper mill studies, but that’s difficult to determine as funders reject many proposals and the details of proposals are not published anywhere, McIntosh said. The only avenue to explore is whether researchers in the network go on to attract funding from legitimate funders after being exposed, she said. Nevertheless, “the whole corpus of your CV is used when you submit a grant, even if you’re only citing some of your own work,” she said.

    Since McIntosh’s revelations about PNN in the last few years, papers mentioning the network as an affiliation have ceased, she said: “They are now using other names.” 

    McIntosh and Silvi also studied the works of 278 researchers who collectively coauthored the papers in the PNN dataset. They found that 90% of these authors continued to publish studies after Retraction Watch reported on the network in 2022. 

    Since then, those authors have collectively published 12,508 studies, McIntosh and Silvi reported, and attracted significant funding to do so. Around a quarter of the researchers received at least one grant since the 2022 exposé, she noted. One of those papers has  been retracted. 

    Jennifer Byrne, an oncologist and a research integrity expert at the University of Sydney in Australia who has studied paper mill article citations in cancer, said it would be interesting to know whether earlier PNN papers are the ones cited in patents, clinical guidelines and grant applications, since citations take time to accumulate. 

    “Some coauthors of paper-mill supported papers might not be aware of their paper mill origins, and so some applicants who have coauthored paper mill papers could apply for grants in good faith,” Byrne said. “That said, researchers who have knowingly used paper mills to bolster their track record might also be willing to misuse grant funds, which is a problem in itself, regardless of the scientific value of the work that is funded.”

    Lancet union declares first-ever strike over staff wages

    Union members at The Lancet and its affiliated journals will go on strike Sept. 22 for one day with further action planned for the following week, after pay negotiations with parent publisher Elsevier fell apart, The Lancet’s union announced today. 

    The strike is the first in The Lancet’s 200-year history. Elsevier was ordered to recognize the union, a chapter of the National Union of Journalists (NUJ), in September 2025, and their collective bargaining agreement was finalized in February. As part of the action the union, which represents 110 employees in various roles across several of The Lancet’s 30 journals, is also calling on members to refuse to work hours outside their contracted amount.

    Negotiations on pay and support for workers began in March when the union asked for an 7.5% pay raise in the first year. According to an open letter from the union, workers’ pay had failed to keep up with inflation, leaving them “substantially worse off than they were five years ago despite their growing expertise and responsibilities.”

    Elsevier rejected the union’s request and countered with a raise of 4.1% for the first year and 2.9% for the second. Talks eventually broke down, and on Sept. 4, members voted 94.5% in favor of the strike. 

    “How the company have conducted themselves in these negotiations and been extremely stubborn and challenging, we think has inflamed the situation and made [The Lancet’s union] more determined than previously to secure its original pay demand,” Alex Maguire, National Union of Journalists organizer, told Retraction Watch. 

    The group said it plans to walk out on Sept. 22, and will “work to rule” from Sept. 23 to 30, performing only their contractually mandated duties and hours. Workers are already working the minimum hours required this week. Members of the union’s committee did not immediately respond to requests for comment, and auto-replies for several members noted that per the union action they would not be available outside working hours.

    “We found that a lot of The Lancet runs on goodwill, and it’s an organization that is dependent for most of its staff to work along above their hours,” Maguire said. “So it was felt that by people sticking to their contracts, that would show how much extra unpaid work they put in.”

    Despite the lack of growth in employee wages, Elsevier, the medical journal’s parent company, reported an adjusted operating profit of 1.04 billion pounds, or US$1.39 billion, in 2025, with a 38% profit margin – the highest of the “big four” academic publishing houses. It’s not clear what the specific contributions of The Lancet journals are to the overall budget. But The Lancet’s article processing fees have nearly doubled in recent years, going from $5,000 in 2020 to $9,550 in 2026, with other Lancet journals also increasing their processing fees.

    In the open letter to Erik Engstrom, the CEO of Elsevier’s parent company RELX, and Elsevier CEO Kumsal Bayazit dated Sept. 17, workers “urge Elsevier to return to negotiations with a pay offer that reflects the crucial value that these staff continue to bring to both the company and the scientific community.”

    James Butcher, a publishing expert and former executive editor of the Lancet, noted the union vote earlier this month in his blog Journalology, saying “editors working on selective journals create value for shareholders and it’s not unreasonable to expect to be treated fairly by employers.”

    In an emailed statement, an Elsevier spokesperson said, “After several rounds of constructive negotiations with the NUJ, regrettably, we have not yet been able to reach an agreement on pay, and NUJ Chapel members have voted for industrial action.”

    In 2024, NUJ workers at Nature held a two-day strike over similar concerns. The union called off the strike on the second day after the union and Springer Nature reached a two-year pay agreement. 

    The Lancet union seeks to find common ground with the publishing giant, the statement from NUJ said, as they believe “Elsevier’s aim, ‘Advancing human progress together,’ cannot succeed without them.”
