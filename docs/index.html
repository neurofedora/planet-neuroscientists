    Journal investigating paper on heart device with registration number for wrong trial

    The publisher of a European Heart Rhythm Association journal is investigating a paper that lists the incorrect registration number for a clinical study of a widely used but controversial heart implant.

    The paper, published in EP Europace in May, described a prospective study of the Watchman FLX, from Boston Scientific, for a procedure called left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) in 400 people with abnormal heart rhythms. The authors concluded the device was “feasible, safe, and resource efficient, achieving high procedural success and low complication rates.” 

    Cardiologists have warned of caveats with the device and the LAAO procedure itself, mainly that early studies found LAAO caused a “significantly higher rate of a primary composite endpoint that included stroke, systemic embolism, cardiovascular/unexplained death, and major bleeding.” A study earlier this year found LAAO with a Watchman device increased rates of stroke and other complications when compared to oral medications.

    The clinical trial registration in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry — ChiCTR2400087516 — published in the paper corresponds to a randomized controlled trial on high-power atrial fibrillation radiofrequency ablation, which doesn’t involve LAAO or the Watchman device. While the Europace paper claims the trial was prospective, the entry in the cited registration states the study was retrospective.

    A separate clinical trial registration in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2400085158, seems to describe the methods reported in the study. 

    Authors Qian Tang and Wei Li, both of Guizhou Medical University, are listed on the clinical trial record included in the study. Neither responded to our request for comment regarding whether the trial was included in error and if there is a corresponding registration for the described Europace study. Co-corresponding author Runze Huang also did not respond to our email. According to a 2023 news article, lead author Wei Zhou is the second surgeon in China to become a “Watchman FLX Global Training Expert,” a role that is not included in the study disclosures. 

    The article acknowledged funding from Guizhou Medical University, but the clinical trial registration likely corresponding to the study stated the funding came from Boston Scientific.

    John Mandrola, a cardiac electrophysiologist at Baptist Health in Louisville, Kentucky, told Retraction Watch the fact neither the editors or the peer reviewers picked up on the incorrect registration number “speaks to the poor attention to detail on the part of the authors and reviewers.”

    “Most critically however, the study itself isn’t really a study. It’s more of a report on ‘look what we did,’” said Mandrola, who has been critical of the evidence for the device. (Disclosure: Mandrola is a frequent contributor to Medscape, where one of our cofounders works.) “It hardly needs nor likely has a real registration.”

    A sleuth who did not provide their name flagged the trial to us earlier this month. They said they notified the journal of their concerns on June 11 and said Europace “subsequently indicated that the matter would be investigated.”

    Giuseppe Boriani, editor-in-chief of the journal, told us the ethics committee at Oxford University Press, which publishes the journal, “is evaluating this issue and will provide a [sic] feedback soon.” Boriani, of the University of Modena and Reggio Emilia in Italy, also forwarded us a September 24 email he sent to the authors requesting clarification from the authors following our request for information but did not respond when we asked whether the journal knew about the incorrect trial before we contacted him. 

    The wrong registration number might be “a simple mix-up,” Mandrola said. “I struggle to understand why Europace, a pretty decent journal in our field, published the study.”

    National Science Library, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and PLOS sign MOU on jointly advancing the OS10 Program

    In September 2026, the National Science Library, Chinese Academy of Sciences (NSL, CAS) and the Public Library of Science (PLOS) formally signed a Memorandum of Understanding on jointly advancing the Open for Science 10 (OS10) Program. Building on their respective strengths in open science infrastructure, open science publishing policies and practices, data resources, and services, the two parties will work together to promote the open sharing and innovative use of scientific knowledge and data, contributing to a more open, inclusive, equitable, and sustainable global open science ecosystem.

    Under the MOU, the two parties will explore cooperation in areas including the sharing of scientific research and open science resources, the development of the OS10 platform’s technical architecture, and open science capacity-building. PLOS may also participate in relevant OS10 working groups and contribute to resource coordination, technical standards, and service design. The two parties will strengthen open science capacity-building in China and other regions participating in OS10 through training, workshops, community engagement, and international exchange. In addition, they will explore opportunities to connect OS10 with the outcomes of PLOS Redefining Publishing initiative and further expand their cooperation on open science policy and practice.

    The program, entitled “Towards Open Science Infrastructure Sharing Network: Knowledge and Data Sharing Platform from China—Open for Science 10” (OS10), was initiated under the leadership of Professor Wei Yang, a Chinese scientist and Member of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. It was officially endorsed by UNESCO in February 2025. The Program aims to develop open science infrastructure in accordance with the guiding framework of the UNESCO Recommendation on Open Science and the strategic plan of the International Decade of Sciences for Sustainable Development, also known as the “Science Decade”. The National Science Library, Chinese Academy of Sciences and Zhejiang University serve as the Program’s liaison and supporting institutions, while the CAST-UN Consultative Committee on Open Science and Global Partnership (CCOS) serves as its lead organization.

    The OS10 program has delivered three key milestones: the launch of the Open4Science platform (open4science.cn), providing access to over 100 million open science and technology resources and reaching users in 105 countries; the development of the International Specification for Open Knowledge and Data Sharing and Application, advancing international collaboration and the sharing of open science infrastructures; and the release of the Open Science Readiness Index (OSRI) report and monitoring platform, mapping open science development and trends across all 193 UN Member States.

    PLOS is a non-profit organization on a mission to drive open science forward with measurable, meaningful change in research publishing, policy, and practice. PLOS believes in a better future where science is open to all, for all. Building on a strong legacy of pioneering innovation, PLOS continues to be a catalyst, reimagining models to meet open science principles, removing barriers and promoting inclusion in knowledge creation and sharing, and publishing research outputs that enable everyone to learn from, reuse and build upon scientific knowledge.

    The research outputs PLOS publishes promote multi-disciplinary discussions on complex problems and enable all stakeholders to learn from, reuse, and build upon scientific knowledge. PLOS reimagines models and mechanisms for sharing to meet open science principles and uses its journals to test and refine innovations at all stages of the research lifecycle.

    Above and beyond: An editor wanted authors to know they’d been falsely cited

    Like many college professors today, Sarah S. Richardson has seen AI-hallucinated citations in work submitted by her students. But she was surprised when an editor recently contacted her about a hallucinated citation involving her own work. 

    A March 31 email from the editor in chief of Disability Studies Quarterly explained Richardson was one of six scholars falsely cited in a journal article. The editor apologized for the error and alerted Richardson of the paper’s retraction. 

    “The editor took the extra step of informing all scholars who could have been harmed by the hallucinated citation[s] and did so in an exceptionally thoughtful and caring manner,” said Richardson, the Aramont Professor of the History of Science and Professor of Studies of Women, Gender, and Sexuality at Harvard University. “I suspect that most hallucinated citations are never discovered, let alone redressed by a retraction and outreach to those affected.”

    Richardson shared the editor’s email with Retraction Watch because she thought his response was “highly meritorious,” and may help “inform best practice,” she told us. 

    The retracted paper, by Shawna Sheperd-Murtagh, was published in March and explored how disabled content creators enact “joy, desire, and collective care as resistant practices in the face of ableist and racialized digital infrastructures.” Disability Studies Quarterly is the journal of the Society for Disability Studies.

    Two weeks after the article was published, a reader emailed the journal to report being falsely cited in the work, according to a June editorial by the journal’s editor-in-chief Jeffrey A. Brune and his assistant editors. 

    The journal audited all 117 footnotes in the article and found a total of six citations that appeared to be AI hallucinations. 

    Brune told us most of the hallucinations included the names of known scholars in the field who were attributed to publications they didn’t write. The journal discovered all the false citations were added after the first peer review, the editorial notes.  

    “The main step toward accountability was to retract the article, which we did right away, but I also felt it was important to be accountable and transparent with the scholars who were falsely cited,” he told us. “For one, we owed them an apology. In addition, I thought it was important that they should be aware of this because they might come across the false citation at some point.”

    Brune’s email to the scholars described what happened and the steps the journal was taking to address it. As a scholar himself, Brune said he “certainly would want to know if a publication had been falsely attributed to me.”

    Sheperd-Murtagh told us she understood the journal’s decision to retract the article and said she takes responsibility for the errors. 

    Her paper resulted from several years of research and writing, followed by an “extensive revision process,” said Sheperd-Murtagh, who was a lecturer at North Carolina State University when she submitted the paper in January of this year and is now director of development for Meals on Wheels Durham. She did not use AI to generate the content, she told us, but “experimented with generative AI” late in the editing process while searching for additional scholarship to “deepen particular areas of the article.” 

    The AI suggested many scholars who she recognized, and because their names and the subject matter “made sense together,” she assumed the information was legitimate, she said. 

    “The citation issues were serious, and it was my responsibility to ensure that every source I cited was accurate and verifiable,” she told us. “I should have caught these errors, and I regret that I did not.” 

    Since discovering the hallucinations, Brune said the journal has implemented a new step to prevent such problems. The journal now requires doi hyperlinks for all citations, when available, so reviewers can easily confirm citations during copyediting. The journal also plans to revise its AI policies this summer and add an AI statement to its instructions for peer reviewers, according to Brune’s editorial.  

    The Disability Studies Quarterly paper is the latest with hallucinated references we’ve reported on recently. Last month, we wrote about the editors of a higher education journal who coauthored an editorial with fake references. In March we reported on a librarian who discovered 12 of 14 references in a Springer Nature paper didn’t exist. 

    Richardson said Brune’s email was the first time she’s ever been contacted by a journal regarding a false citation linked to her name and she hopes more journals adopt the practice. 

    “In my view, hallucinated citations are a prime example of the threat of LLM usage to our expert knowledge systems,” she told us. “The articles can be retracted but they are already online and stay online, and the damage of muddle and disinformation is done if others duplicate those citations or LLMs imbibe them as credible.”
