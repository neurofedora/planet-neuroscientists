    Scheduled Database Maintenance | October 28th, 2026

    Attention arXiv users: a week from today, on Wednesday, October 28th, 2026, arXiv will be migrating our database to the cloud. This is scheduled maintenance for arxiv.org and will result in some disruptions to service, mailings, and new announcements.

    Why are we migrating the arXiv database?

    We’re migrating the arXiv database as an important step in arXiv-CE, a critical project to modernize arXiv’s technology and code to better serve arXiv users and set arXiv up for stable, scalable growth. The arXiv engineering team has been working diligently to modernize our technology since 2023, thanks to generous funding and community support. We’ve made a lot of progress in the past three years, and we are very close to wrapping up the arXiv-CE project.

    arXiv’s services and database are currently hosted on Cornell University servers, where they have been for the past 25 years. The goal of this part of the arXiv-CE project is to move arXiv’s systems from Cornell to the cloud. Migrating the arXiv database, which stores arXiv’s core information, is the first step – once it has moved, we will move the rest of our services.

    By migrating to the cloud, we will:

    • Improve reliability and resilience: cloud hosting gives us stronger redundancy, automated backups, and faster disaster recovery.
    • Support growth: we can add capacity as submissions and readership grow, without buying and maintaining physical hardware.
    • Reduce operational risk and overhead: we will rely less on aging on-premises infrastructure, and our team can spend more time improving arXiv.
    • Simplify our architecture: with all systems in one environment, arXiv will be easier to maintain, secure, and develop.

    How will this scheduled maintenance affect me?

    The arXiv team strives to make updates with as few disruptions to service as possible. However, sometimes outages are necessary to safely and efficiently make updates to arxiv.org. When we migrate the arXiv database on Wednesday, October 28th, there will be limited downtime and a temporary disruption to the announcement schedule.

    The database migration will begin at 7:00 AM EDT (11:00 UTC) and is scheduled to have a service maintenance window of 5 hours, ending at 12:00 PM EDT (16:00 UTC).

    During the maintenance window, arXiv will be in “read-only” mode. This means all functions that require login (submission, moderation, account editing, endorsement, etc) will be unavailable. Browsing, viewing, and searching papers on arxiv.org will continue to work as normal.

    From 7AM – 12PM ET, arXiv users will not be able to login or make any changes to their accounts. There will be no access to the submission system. arXiv users intending to submit work to arXiv, please plan accordingly.

    At noon (12:00PM EDT | 16:00 UTC) arxiv.org will exit read-only mode and login functionality will return.

    General service on arxiv.org will return to normal. arXiv users will be able to login to make changes to their account, submit papers, etc.

    Announcements of new submissions will be deferred.

    Similar to when a federal holiday disrupts the regular arXiv announcement schedule, this scheduled maintenance will cause a temporary deferral of arXiv announcements. There will be no announcement or mailings on Wednesday. This temporary disruption will allow arXiv staff and volunteer moderators time to focus on responding to user questions and test for any potential issues. The regular announcement schedule will resume Thursday, October 29th.

    • Wednesday, October 28, 2026: No announcement. Articles received and accepted at or after 14:00EDT Tuesday, October 27, 2026, and before 14:00ET Thursday, October 29, 2026 will be announced on Thursday, October 29, 2026 at 20:00EDT.

    Where can I check for updates on arxiv.org status and/or the status of the arXiv-CE project?

    We know that disruptions to service impact researchers and we strive to minimize that disruption as much as possible with open, timely, and frequent communication across all possible channels.

    The arXiv status page will regularly be updated with information as we migrate the database, and there will be alerts in the submission portal in the week leading up to the database migration, as well as public banners across arxiv.org.

    If you want more ways to stay up to date on arXiv news and changes to the announcement schedule, subscribe to the arXiv blog or follow us in your social media feed (LinkedIn, Twitter/X, Mastodon, Bluesky and Facebook) for timely updates.

    Thank you to all our users for their patience while we make these changes, and as always, feedback is welcome as we continue to improve arXiv.

    Journal founded by plastic surgeon touts studies of products he’s tied to – including one that has been retracted

    In a February 2018 editorial, Rod Rohrich, a plastic surgeon and the former president of the World Association of Medical Editors, blasted predatory journals for “the damage that these illegitimate open access journals do to the reputation of legitimate, credible ones.” 

    Two years later, he wrote about how his team at Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery–Global Open, the journal he founded in 2013, had demonstrated to authors and readers that they had created a rigorous peer-review process and were “advancing patient safety and outcomes and elevating the science of plastic surgery globally.”

    But an investigation by Retraction Watch raises questions about the way Rohrich, who is now editor emeritus at the journal, has promoted preliminary and sometimes misleading research about products to which he has financial ties. That includes exosomes, which are often derived from stem cells and have increasingly been touted — with limited evidence — for skin care, hair loss and wound healing.

    Paul Knoepfler, a cell biologist at the University of California, Davis and publisher of the stem cell blog The Niche, said the field of exosomes is a “hugely hyped area” rife with dubious research: According to the Retraction Watch Database, 181 papers on the topic have been retracted for issues including image duplication, unreliable data, compromised peer review and links to paper mills.

    “I get concerned when I see papers being published by journals where one or more authors have or have had leadership roles at the same journal,” Knoepfler told Retraction Watch, in response to Rohrich’s publications on exosomes and his role at the journal. “At a minimum it raises questions about how that connection could have potentially impacted editorial decisions and peer review.”

    Rohrich, who founded the Dallas Plastic Surgery Institute, has served as a consultant for Rion, a Mayo Clinic spinoff that has been testing platelet exosomes for therapeutic applications in clinical trials, but is already marketing its “plated” skin care line for cosmetic purposes. “In Dallas, we’re going to be the first practice to ever use exosomes in skincare,” he said on his podcast during a 2022 interview with Rion’s founder, Atta Behfar. “Tell us about this amazing new product and what it’s going to do for our skin.”

    Three years later, a review paper Rohrich wrote with Behfar received an award from his journal. A report on a clinical trial of the product Rohrich and Behfar published the following year appears to contain “serious design errors” and “misleading analyses,” according to one expert who reviewed it for Retraction Watch. Meanwhile, a third study on exosomes by a different research group, which the journal’s social media feed publicized, has since been retracted.

    In an email to Retraction Watch, Rohrich pushed back against the criticism of his work and reiterated his support for sound science. “I welcome legitimate scientific criticism … and strongly support an accurate and appropriately measured account of what these publications do and do not establish,” he wrote. “I remain optimistic about the potential of regenerative medicine, but optimism is not a substitute for evidence.”

    Rohrich is one of the best-known plastic surgeons in the country. He once earned more than $1.75 million per year as the chairman of the Department of Plastic Surgery at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center in Dallas, making him one of the highest paid public employees in the state.

    In 2004, he became the president of the American Society of Plastic Surgeons and the editor-in-chief of its journal, Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery. He launched PRS Global Open in April 2013 and strove to make it “the best peer-reviewed open-access plastic surgery literature in the world.”

    Three years later, he departed the university after it emerged that he had an affair with a former patient. The woman’s husband accused the pair of secretly selling a $1 million diamond to fund travel and marketing expenses related to a book about  plastic surgery they wrote, Navigate your Beauty: Smart and Safe Plastic Surgery Solutions. 

    “Once the gem is on a woman’s finger, it is there forever,” Rohrich wrote in a blog post after his departure. “It technically belongs to his wife and she can sell it whenever she wants to.” 

    Now at the Dallas Plastic Surgery Institute, he has maintained a high profile through his podcast Rohrich Knose, which has 12,000 subscribers on YouTube, his role organizing the popular Dallas Cosmetic Medicine and Rhinoplasty Meetings, and his work as a journal editor.

    He has also been an informal mentor to Ankur Khajuria, a prolific British surgeon who joined the journal’s resident advisory board in 2020. The two have authored three papers together, and Rohrich wrote a foreword to Khajuria’s book, Landmark Papers in Plastic Surgery. 

    As Retraction Watch previously reported, Khajuria has offered guaranteed publications to students in his research academy, and has published 29 papers in the two plastic surgery journals Rohrich once ran. One of those papers, which claimed to test one of his own products, was retracted by the editors in September due to “the unreliability of the reported results.”

    “I was concerned to learn that questions had been raised about that paper,” Rohrich told us. None of his own articles with Khajuria has been called into question. “I would characterize my relationship with Dr. Khajuria as supportive and professional, but not as a formal or close mentorship,” he said.

    Khajuria, who was the first author of the retracted study, did not respond to a request for comment on our original story, which was published in January. Through more recent correspondence facilitated by his attorney, he told us he did not have access to the underlying dataset and that he received “pre-calculated p-values in a statistical summary file” from the Korean hospital where the experimental work took place and incorporated them into the manuscript in “good faith.”

    Rohrich’s interest in exosomes developed after stepping into the editor emeritus role in December 2020 and subsequently becoming a consultant for Rion. In 2022, he and Behfar, a Mayo Clinic cardiologist focused on regenerative medicine, first spoke about the topic on Rohrich’s podcast.

    Two years later, they were coauthors of a review paper in PRS Global Open, which detailed the benefits of Rion’s formulation of platelet-derived exosomes (“a room temperature-stable final drug product”) in comparison to the traditional exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (“pro-oncogenic” and suffer from “lot-to-lot variability”).

    “In the authors’ experience, identification of antioxidant enzymes and proangiogenic cytokines contained within platelet exosomes have been essential for their therapeutic utility,” they wrote, citing another Rion-funded study in Cosmetic Medicine. 

    But the only sentence in that study about antioxidant enzymes and angiogenic growth factors includes the tag “data not shown.” (That paper’s abstract also claimed Rion’s platelet-derived exosomes combined with a standardized skin care regimen increased skin health by 224% without noting that number referred only to the “top-quartile cohort.”)

    In his email to Retraction Watch, Rohrich agreed that the “supporting reference could have been more precise” and offered other examples from the literature. He said he believed the review nevertheless offered a balanced description of exosomes from multiple sources and that it “repeatedly emphasizes the current limitations of the field.”

    The review received PRS Global Open’s “Best Research Paper” award in 2025. The journal told us Rohrich “has not been involved in PRS Global Open editorial operations since his term ended in December 2020” and that awards are based on “readership and citation data.” The review has been cited 12 times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science.

    Rohrich and Behfar followed that paper with a clinical trial, published last March in PRS Global Open, showing Rion’s hair serum “improves hair density, volume fullness, and hair and scalp health.” Although Behfar and several of the authors disclosed their relationships to Rion, Rohrich did not.

    “It’s a phenomenal skin care product, and it seemed natural to transition it to hair and that’s what we did in a double-blind study, placebo-control with nine months duration,” Rohrich said in a video posted on plated’s Instagram page. “The results were absolutely amazing.” Rohrich said his failure to disclose his relationship to Rion was “an oversight” he will correct.

    But the clinical trial departed from widely accepted practice in assigning only eight of the 39 participants to the placebo group. Even if the placebo and treatment groups showed the same increase in hair growth over time, those differences would be more likely to be statistically significant due to the larger treatment group, according to experts. 

    In fact, the placebo group ended up with a slightly higher average increase in the number of hair follicles and the strength and quality of their hair, but the researchers found statistically significant increases only among subjects who received the exosomes.

    “The authors made serious study design errors by using a minimum-power minimum-precision allocation ratio that is far from 1:1,” said Frank Harrell Jr., a biostatistician at Vanderbilt University Medical Center in Nashville. In addition, he said, they “jettisoned the actual design in favor of ad hoc and misleading analyses.” As one commenter on PubPeer explained, these errors will tilt “the study toward a positive result.”

    Rohrich disputed that the study was misleading and said the “80:20 allocation was intentionally selected to provide greater exposure to the active formulation for initial efficacy and safety evaluation.” He said he agrees “a larger, more evenly powered trial is suggested to further confirm and extend these findings.”

    The tentative nature of the findings hasn’t stopped companies from touting the product as a treatment.

    “This is not a quick ‘shine serum’ — it’s a treatment,” reads the product description at The Swell Score, a wellness marketplace that claims to “vet everything for what’s clean and what actually works.”

    Mayo Clinic’s store is more cautious in its own wording, noting the substance “supports hair follicle proliferation and a healthy growth cycle.” Neither Behfar, Rion, nor the Mayo Clinic’s media relations team responded to multiple requests for comment.

    Knoepfler, the cell biologist, said topical formulations of exosomes, such as the product from Rion, fall into a “regulatory gray area” for the Food and Drug Administration and have been a way for companies to get their products on the market more quickly than injections.

    PRS Global Open has also promoted another paper on a topical exosome product that was subsequently retracted. In October 2025, the journal published a study testing what it called “adipose-derived stem-cell exosomes” on four adults with chronic wounds on their legs. 

    The study’s authors included Hee-Jin Ahn, the director of Primoris International, the Korean company that makes the product, dubbed Exo-HL. “Exosome therapy was well tolerated and associated with rapid granulation, improved perfusion, and durable closure of refractory chronic ulcers,” the authors wrote in their abstract.

    Soon after, the journal’s social media editor, plastic surgeon Roy Kim, who has touted exosomes in his own practice for skin rejuvenation and hair restoration and has discussed Korean skincare on Rohrich’s podcast, posted a video about the new study on the journal’s Instagram feed. 

    “The patients had remarkable wound healing,” he said. “It’s a very interesting study on how exosomes can be used to help patients.”

    In November, an anonymous commenter on PubPeer noted that none of the patients’ ages in a table matched their descriptions in the text and the article lacked any financial disclosures. In addition, the commenter pointed out that the study had no control group and chronic wounds can heal on their own. 

    “Without a comparator group no valid conclusions can be drawn about treatment efficacy,” the commenter wrote.

    Ahn and the other authors requested the paper be retracted the following month. The retraction notice indicates only that they learned the product was made from cultured plant cells rather than human stem cells. “This discrepancy compromises the validity of the findings and conclusions,” the editors wrote. 

    Kim has not updated his Instagram post on the journal’s feed and referred questions about the selection to the journal. The journal said articles are highlighted based on “general interest, clinical relevance, and the availability of engaging visual content” and that the social media post directs readers to the article’s official version of record, which notes the retraction.

    The authors revised the paper to make the ages of their patients consistent (and added an additional patient), and JPRAS Open, an Elsevier journal, published it in April. The new paper removed its quantitative findings related to blood flow to the tissue and emphasized that the study was a “small, uncontrolled series” and that the results were “hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.”

    The authors did not respond to multiple requests for comment.

    Rohrich, for his part, remains proud of his work as an editor, researcher, and mentor. 

    “My commitment throughout my career — including in founding Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery–Global Open — has been to encourage transparent, evidence-based discussion and to improve the quality of plastic surgery research worldwide,” he said.

    Japanese institute finds materials researcher with 41 retractions guilty of ‘extensive’ manipulation

    An investigation by a national research institute in Japan has concluded one of its former researchers fabricated and falsified figures in seven papers. Two publishers confirmed they are investigating the articles. 

    Joseph Raj Xavier, of Saveetha Institute of Medical and Technical Sciences in Chennai, India, has lost 10 other papers this year alone, according to the Retraction Watch Database. His total is currently 41 retractions, earning him a spot on the Retraction Watch Leaderboard. 

    The National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS) in Tsukuba, Japan, found Xavier guilty of fabrication and falsification in “scanning electron microscope images, atomic force microscope images, and scanning electrochemical microscope images, among others,” according to a translation of an investigation report released September 29. 

    The investigation found it was “highly likely that figures were reused; there were signs of extensive manipulation, such as scaling the figures to full size, stretching or rotating them, and traces of cutting and pasting parts of images were found in multiple figures,” according to the report. Xavier committed self-plagiarism in six of the papers published from 2014 to 2017, the investigation found. The articles cover the detection and prevention of metal corrosion. 

    “Based on these findings, it was concluded that specific acts of misconduct were committed intentionally,” the report states. 

    The institute looked into 23 papers in total, eight of which listed his affiliation as NIMS. One of the eight did not contain evidence of misconduct, NIMS found. For the other 15 papers listing Xavier at other institutions, published from 2016 to 2019, the committee said there was an “extremely high likelihood” Xavier altered figures used in papers he published while with NIMS and “improperly used them as figures” in papers “under the affiliation of another institution.” 

    The case was brought to our attention by Haklak Rockbill, professor emeritus at Ochanomizu University who tracks and writes about research ethics and misconduct. 

    Xavier’s work has been cited over 4,000 times, roughly one-third of which are self citations, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science. 

    Xavier lists Saveetha Institute of Medical and Technical Sciences  as his affiliation on several recent papers. Repeated emails to his Saveetha and multiple personal email addresses went unanswered. We have flagged Saveetha in the past for its excessive self-citation rates and, last year, we reported its retraction rate has risen drastically in recent years. 

    NIMS alerted Xavier’s current institution of their findings, the representative told us, but did not confirm whether Saveetha was that institution. 

    “After being informed that an interview would be conducted as part of the investigation, Dr. Xavier stopped responding to communications,” the NIMS representative said.

    As part of the investigation, the NIMS committee looked into the work of Xavier’s frequent coauthor, his supervisor Toshiya Nishimura. They concluded Nishimura had not intentionally committed misconduct, but noted for one of the papers where Nishimura was the corresponding author, “there was a lack of communication with Mr. Xavier and insufficient review of the manuscript.” For six other papers the committee examined, “it was confirmed that Mr. Xavier had added Mr. Nishimura as a co-author without permission after leaving the organization and submitted the papers.”

    The institute became aware of PubPeer comments flagging Xavier’s work on January 22 when it received a call on their research misconduct reporting hotline, the report states. Nishimura had retired from NIMS before then, the Legal Affairs and Compliance Office at NIMS told Retraction Watch. 

    Of the seven papers the committee said contained misconduct, four appear in Springer Nature journals and two in Elsevier journals. The investigation committee recommended Xavier and Nishimura withdraw the papers. “However, we have not yet received an indication from Mr. Xavier regarding whether or not he will comply with the recommendation within the period stipulated in the regulations,” the report reads. 

    The Legal Affairs and Compliance Office at NIMS told us they “plan to inform the relevant journals of the investigation findings in due course.” 

    Tim Kersjes, head of Research Integrity at Springer Nature, did not directly respond to our question of whether Xavier had informed the publisher of the investigation. However, he said the publisher is “aware of concerns with a number of papers authored by this individual, and our Research Integrity Team are conducting thorough, ongoing investigations into relevant articles.” Authors found to have violated the publisher’s Act with Integrity policy “may face restrictions on their engagement with our journals,” Kersjes said. 

    An Elsevier spokesperson said the publisher is “aware of the issues raised regarding this author” and that the case is under investigation. 

    The seventh paper in the investigation was published in 2017 by the International Journal of Petrochemical Science & Engineering. The journal’s publisher, MedCrave — which once appeared on the now-defunct Beall’s List and was the target of a Seinfeld-themed sting in 2017 — did not respond to our request for comment.
