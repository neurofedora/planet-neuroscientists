    A composer published a paper on theoretical physics. His lack of credentials drew criticism.

    Some people compose music or make YouTube videos for a hobby. That’s Brandon Yates’ job — so in his spare time, he tries his hand at theoretical physics. But his only published paper was questioned because of his lack of professional training in the field — despite the physicist who inspired his work saying the work looks legitimate.

    Based in Las Vegas, Yates earns a living as a composer for the animated series called “Death Battle.” He also has had an “intuitive understanding of, and love for, General Relativity for as long as I can remember,” he told Retraction Watch. But he has no formal training in physics and is self-taught, he said. 

    After watching a video lecture series online by Canadian physicist Eric Poisson, in January, Yates built a computer algebra program to look into rules in general relativity used to fuse two spacetimes across a thin shell, Yates said. By March, he had come across a conditional result he believed to be novel.

    He spent a few weeks trying to understand his finding, “and then sat on it a couple more weeks for a literature search and to decide whether to submit a paper despite my station,” Yates told us. He submitted his findings to the International Journal of Geometric Methods in Modern Physics and said the peer reviewers asked only for a few “presentational” fixes, including that he hadn’t used his key words enough. 

    While the paper was under review, Yates posted on X in March asking for an endorsement so he could post a preprint on arXiv, which requires such backing for new authors.

    General relativity is a popular topic for amateur physicists or people working outside their expertise. Regardless of his lack of formal training, Yates’ paper on vanishing surface density in generalized Vaidya spacetimes was published on June 20, listing him as an independent researcher. 

    “It’s probably worth making explicit that I have no incentive to fudge anything. I’m successful in another field and already have a thriving career,” Yates told us.

    The day after it was published, a physicist who declined to give us his full name because he “would like to focus quietly on my academic research without troubles,” emailed the journal to express concern that Yates “appears to have no formal education in any STEM-related field.”

    “This lack of background, coupled with the highly technical nature of the work, strongly indicates that the manuscript was likely generated using an aggressive, AI-assisted workflow by someone outside the scientific community (specifically, a professional musician),” the physicist wrote. 

    Yates told us he used a generative AI tool, but within the confines of what is acceptable at the journal. The AI “was limited to assistance with phrasing in academic register, LaTeX formatting/typesetting, and assistance preparing the Python verification script,” he said. “The mathematical claims are supported by the exact symbolic verification materials and do not depend on AI output.” 

    Francesco Bajardi, co-executive editor at the International Journal of Geometric Methods in Modern Physics, told us the journal’s review of the article “has not identified evidence suggesting improper use of AI, though we cannot state this with certainty.” 

    “However, the lack of affiliation and background are clearly suspicious,” Bajardi said. He told us the journal was no longer investigating the article because “there is no certain proof that the manuscript has been entirely written by AI.”

    Poisson, whose work inspired Yates, told us the paper “doesn’t reveal any red flag that suggests to me a major AI involvement.” He noted the work “describes a rather straightforward exercise in applying junction conditions in general relativity, with no particular interest nor novelty, but I see nothing objectionable about it,” he said. 

    Yates told us “there was never a grand plan here. I found a result I thought might be novel, did my best to turn it into a proper paper, and submitted it through the normal process.” He is now taking online courses so he “can pursue proper accreditation,” Yates said. “A BA, and later if I’m lucky, a PhD in mathematical or computational physics. I’m also actively seeking out a mentor, though I’ve had no luck so far.”

    “I didn’t expect that working without a degree would turn a refereed paper into news,” he said. “I’m pursuing physics because it brings me joy, and submitted the paper when I’d found something.”

    High-profile anesthesiologists earning retractions for data, image problems

    In July 2021, John Loadsman received a manuscript about a clinical trial that immediately roused his suspicion. Then the editor-in-chief of Anaesthesia and Intensive Care, Loadsman asked the authors for the raw data.

    They weren’t pretty. “When I looked carefully at the spreadsheet, I could see entire blocks of data that were repeated,” recalled Loadsman, an anesthesiologist and scientific sleuth at the University of Sydney. “It was obvious that this was cooked.”

    He rejected the manuscript, informing both the corresponding author, Reyhan Polat of the University of Health Sciences in Ankara and Etlik City Hospital, in Turkey, and her institution about his concerns. He also made a mental note to have a look at the researcher’s published work. Over the following years, that effort drew in another sleuth and eventually prompted inquiries into more than two dozen papers by a network of researchers in Turkey.

    So far, Loadsman has flagged four papers by Polat, a professor of anesthesiology and vice president of the Anesthesiology and Reanimation Specialists Society, a national organization. 

    Some of the articles showed error bars for mean values that, inexplicably, did not include the means themselves, Loadsman pointed out on PubPeer. Another contained impossible scores on an anxiety scale, among other problems. The raw data also contained inexplicable duplications.

    Polat was first author on two of the articles, all of which have now been retracted – in some cases years after Loadsman alerted the publishers to the problems; the latest retraction notice appeared in June in Elsevier’s Journal of Clinical Anesthesia.

    Polat, who has led several clinical trials, did not respond to requests for comment.

    The field of anesthesiology has seen its share of fabricated research – or, in Loadsman’s words, “large volumes of fraudulent crap.” In 2009, our cofounder Adam Marcus broke the story about Scott Reuben, an  American anesthesiology researcher who faked data in numerous clinical trials. The Retraction Watch Leaderboard includes five anesthesiologists, two of whom occupy the top two slots.  

    As Loadsman discovered, two of the now-retracted papers were coauthored by Polat’s head of department, Jülide Ergil. Like Polat, Ergil is a professor at the University of Health Sciences. She is also president of the Turkish Society of Anaesthesiology and Reanimation and earlier this year was made a fellow of the European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care. The organization describes the fellowship as a “prestigious honour” recognizing “doctors who demonstrate integrity, clinical excellence, and dedication to patient care.” 

    Ergil also had other problematic publications, Loadsman found. In 2024, he teamed up with fellow sleuth René Aquarius, a neurosurgery researcher at Radboud University Medical Center in Nijmegen, the Netherlands, and the two dug into the Turkish anesthesiologists’ publications. They found lots to worry about.

    One paper from 2015 on which Ergil was first author contained a table with nine values for standard deviations that exactly matched data in a 2012 paper by the same group, although the two publications purported to report on different experiments. (Ergil was not among the authors on the earlier article.)

    A histology image in Ergil’s article, published in Archives of Medical Science, also showed a repeated pattern suggesting “copy-pasting in the digital file,” according to Aquarius.

    “As first author, you’re probably in the best position to notice that something is wrong or something is incorrect,” he told us.

    The problems extended beyond Ergil and Polat. Some of the images in the 2015 paper later turned up in studies from 2019 and 2020 on which neither researcher was an author, Aquarius and Loadsman discovered. The 2019 paper, which included images from three earlier publications, has since been retracted.

    Ergil told us she had not been involved in the “histopathological evaluations” for the 2015 paper, but had “relied in good faith on the pathological assessments and images provided by the responsible specialist.” She had been in touch with “the responsible pathologist,” she said, and the pathologist had explained that “an inadvertent archival mix-up occurred during the preparation of a separate article published in 2019, resulting in the inclusion of an incorrect image.”

    Her lack of involvement in “conducting the animal experiments, generating laboratory data, preparing histopathological specimens, selecting pathology images, or performing statistical analyses” was also why she wasn’t aware of the data duplication. “I had no independent means of verifying whether any of the materials provided to me had previously been used in other publications.”

    As for the issues Loadsman and Aquarius had found in her other works, including the retracted paper, she said her role on each was limited to reviewing and revising the manuscript, not the experiments or statistical analysis.

    An editor at Termedia Publishing House, which publishes Archives of Medical Science, said the company was looking into the 2015 paper.

    The European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care is also reviewing the concerns over Ergil’s work. The society “takes matters of scientific integrity seriously,” a spokesperson said, adding that it would provide an update by the end of August. 

    In total, Loadsman and Aquarius have flagged 30 papers by the same network of Turkish researchers on PubPeer, with Ergil or Polat involved in seven of the publications. There could be more questionable works, Aquarius said, but at some point he had to “draw a line in the sand because I don’t want to spend more time on this.”

    In 2024, Aquarius shared a spreadsheet listing the 30 papers with publishers. “In many cases, nothing has happened,” he said.

    Celebrating Retraction Watch’s 16th birthday, how sweet it is

    Suddenly, we’re 16. And what a year it has been. 

    Our birthday (and annual birthday post) comes on the heels of one of the biggest stories we’ve told this year: The unreported death of a 6-year-old girl whose parents paid more than $850,000 to a lab in China to develop a therapy for her rare genetic disorder. Seven days after getting the treatment, the young girl died. 

    Retraction Watch contributing writer Brendan Borrell spent countless hours seeking out experts in the field, chasing down documents and translations, and talking to the girl’s parents, who, after all they have been through, have one simple request: the retraction of a paper in Nature, in which the lab reports its preclinical results of the very therapy that caused their daughter’s death. 

    It’s an utterly heartbreaking and important story brought to light in collaboration with Science, which published the story on July 23. Just a few days later, the researchers’ university announced it would launch an investigation into the girl’s death. And a few days after that, Nature placed an editor’s note on the lab’s paper, saying it would be launching an investigation as well. The story has attracted international coverage by news outlets including the New York Post, Bloomberg, Times Higher Education, Southern Weekly and the South China Morning Post. 

    The story is one of more than 250 our team — Alicia Gallegos, Avery Orrall and Kate Travis, plus a small team of regular contributors — has reported in the last year. Some more highlights:

    Unusual publication — and retraction — behavior regularly catches our eye. That was the case for a researcher who had 35 retractions in 24 months; a journal with half its papers citing its editor-in-chief; and a set of researchers whose h-indexes showed unusual growth. That last story came in part through work done through the Retraction Watch Sleuths in Residence Program. 

    And we continued our reporting on the deluge of papers from health databases with a story in Science about the rise of misleading studies based on TriNetX, a research platform that accesses data from more than 300 million electronic health records. 

    We wrote about a surgeon’s publication academy guaranteeing attendees a peer-reviewed paper, but that wasn’t why he caught our notice. An analysis by our Medical Evidence Project found statistical flaws in an animal study the doctor authored that was the only published report supporting the signature product of a company he founded. 

    This work wouldn’t be possible without you. Your tax-deductible donation helps ensure we can keep telling stories like these. 

    Donate Now

    There were several government rulings and court decisions in the last several months, including rulings from the U.S. Office of Research Integrity. Our coverage of one of them prompted a closer look at a paper not explicitly mentioned in the investigation — but still warranted retraction.  

    We wrote about an administrative law judge upholding the debarment of a researcher who once threatened to sue Retraction Watch. And we’ve covered the False Claims Act, both in terms of its tenets as well as specific cases, including a $15 million settlement by Dana-Farber Cancer Institute and a case in progress filed against four major publishers. 

    Then there was the pediatrics journal in Canada that issued corrections for 138 case studies it had published over 25 years, as they neglected to mention the cases were fictionalized. Our story got picked up by NRC Handelsblad (The Netherlands), AusDoc (Australia), the National Post (Canada) and Gizmodo (US).

    Our list of mass resignations continues to grow; we’ve added seven to the list just this year, with editors resigning over publishing quotas, changes to peer review and author fees. 

    And a bit of levity: We published a guest post by a researcher frustrated by irrelevant solicitations from predatory journals, so he used ChatGPT to write a paper on how teaching math to pregnant women prompts cravings for prime numbers. And the journal published it. 

    Beyond the pages of Retraction Watch, we were honored to accept an invitation to testify before the Investigations and Oversight Subcommittee of the House Science, Space and Technology Committee. They sought our input for an April hearing on scientific publishing that covered open access, reproducibility, predatory journals, paper mills and the incentive structures in science. The hearing attracted coverage by Nature and Inside Higher Ed. We also testified before a Canadian Parliament committee on accountability in research, our second time in front of the Standing Committee on Science and Research.

    And we’ve published pieces elsewhere too. Our cofounders published an opinion piece in STAT about the mess at the American Diabetes Association — and how they seem to have lost the courage they praised them for nearly a decade ago. They also coauthored a perspective on sanctions for misconduct in anesthesia research, published in June. And an editorial in The Sunday Times Review discussed what nearly 500 retractions per month really means. 

    This year our parent nonprofit, the Center for Scientific Integrity, launched two new initiatives. The Retraction Watch Research Accountability Reporting Fellowship, in partnership with The Open Notebook, will provide training and mentorship to local journalists over six months. And the Ctrl-Z Award will recognize a junior and a senior researcher for the courage it takes to correct or retract their own published work.

    Recipients for both of those initiatives have been chosen and we can’t wait to tell you more in the coming weeks. 

    The Hijacked Journal Checker has more than 450 entries. The list formed the basis of a study examining the phenomenon.

    Our Retraction Watch Database now contains more than 66,000 retractions. Researchers used the database for articles on topics including conflicts of interest, the afterlife of retracted articles, potential paper mill papers in cancer research, and many more. 

    A fun moment: The Retraction Watch Database made it into the Harper’s Index.  

    We’re thrilled by this growth and progress, all of which center on keeping individuals, institutions and publishers accountable to upholding the integrity of the research enterprise.

    Federal lawsuit accuses four major publishers of extorting article processing charges

    A lawsuit filed under the U.S. False Claims Act accuses four major publishers of perpetrating a decade-long scheme to defraud the government by charging exorbitant article processing charges.  

    The case, which became unsealed July 30, claims Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley and Informa forced U.S. research institutions to submit false claims to the government for reimbursement through article fees up to 10 times the cost of processing the articles. 

    Juan Pablo Alperin, a professor at Simon Fraser University and codirector of the Scholarly Communications Lab based in Vancouver, is represented by two attorneys who have had blockbuster success in FCA cases over the past decade. He and his attorneys originally filed the case under seal in March 2024. The government declined to intervene in the case on July 23 of this year, and a judge ordered the complaint unsealed, which now allows Alperin to pursue the case independently.

    Sage was also originally listed as a defendant in the case, according to the court docket, and University of Ottawa researcher Stefanie Haustein was a plaintiff. Both were dropped from the suit last week. Alperin told us after reviewing the numbers, Sage had “far fewer articles” published through open access with authors who were funded by U.S. federal agencies, prompting “a focus on the four defendants with the most articles that fell under the allegations in the complaint.”

    Publishers’ fees vary, but APCs for open access can range from hundreds of dollars for small journals up to thousands, with some topping $10,000 per paper. The real costs  of processing such articles are between $200 and $1,000, Alperin claims in the complaint. The U.S. government requires papers based on federally funded research be freely available. The lawsuit alleges the publishers address this mandate by “extorting” APCs and copyright transfers from “unknowing” PIs who have an “understandable desire to publish in prestigious venues to advance in their careers.”

    Alperin alleges he learned insider details about APC pricing as a member of the Open Access Scholarly Publishing Association, including “outright unlawful conduct” and “conspiratorial discussions” among the publishers, the complaint states. This included strategies by the companies to shield the viability of free open access options to ensure U.S. grantee institutions continued paying unnecessary APCs, according to his complaint. 

    Alperin told Retraction Watch he brought the FCA case to spotlight how pricing practices by some publishers are not in the public interest and to hold them accountable for those practices. He has conducted extensive research on APCs, which has found publishers use APC pricing strategically, rather than based on reasonable production expenses. 

    “More broadly, the goal is to help to create a more transparent and functioning market, because the market of open access publishing is one that is in many ways very dysfunctional,” he told us. “In part, the goal is helping to create a market that is transparent, equitable, and that ensures as much of the money that’s been set aside for research can go towards” that intent.  

    Debate about APCs is not new, said Eugenie Reich, a Boston-based attorney who is representing Alperin. What is new is identifying a legal claim where the public discussion intersects with the law, she said. 

    “That discussion has definitely existed, but it’s never, as far as I’m aware, been publicly alleged that this is fraud on the government,” she told us. “I think that’s where publishers have gotten themselves into trouble per the complaint, by using public access policies as a way of increasing charges, knowing that the charges would hit government grants.” 

    The complaint alleges the publishers have “fixed the market” to make it difficult to publish without paying “prestige prices, luxury prices,” both through market concentration and through copyright terms, Reich added. 

    “That’s why I think it is important to distinguish between a business that just wants to make money from a luxury good and a business that is knowingly passing such charges through to the government,” she said in an interview.  

    Reich represented sleuth Sholto David in an FCA action against Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, which settled the case in December 2025 for $15 million. The institute admitted researchers used “misrepresented and/or duplicated” images and data in support of grant applications to the National Institutes of Health. As the relator in the case, David received a portion of the settlement, with the bulk of the amount going back to the federal government.

    Reich was also part of the team representing the whistleblower in an FCA case against Biogen, which agreed to pay $900 million to resolve claims the company unlawfully paid kickbacks to physicians. The government also declined to intervene in that case. 

    John R. Thomas of Hafemann, Magee & Thomas, LLC, in Roanoke, Virginia, the other lawyer representing Alperin, was one of the attorneys in an FCA case against Duke University that resulted in a $112.5 million settlement, nearly $34 million of which went to his brother Joseph, the whistleblower in the case. He has written for Retraction Watch about the act as well as about what whistleblowers should consider in the context of an FCA claim. 

    A Springer Nature spokesperson said the publisher could not immediately comment on the matter. Wiley and Elsevier declined to comment. Informa did not respond. 

    FCA attorneys had mixed views of the complaint. Pamela Coyle Brecht, a whistleblower attorney in Philadelphia, called the filing a “niche case” with a “unique theory.” 

    However, Renée Brooker, a Washington, D.C.-based attorney with Tycko & Zavareei LLP, said the fact the defendants are journal publishers may be “eye-grabbing,” but the legal theories underpinning the case are tried and true in the FCA realm.. A wide swath of qui tam cases involve defendants that did not directly submit the alleged false claims, Brooker said, particularly complaints against the pharmaceutical industry. Such companies are frequently accused of FCA violations, while a pharmacy or doctor’s office unknowingly filed the false claims.

    The case is a prime example of parties that don’t directly submit false claims to the government still facing liability, said Eva Gunasekera, a whistleblower attorney at Tycko & Zavareei and former senior counsel for healthcare fraud at the DOJ. 

    “It’s a great reminder of how broad in scope the False Claims Act is,” she said.

    By law, FCA cases are filed under seal to protect whistleblowers and to give the government time to investigate the claims and decide if it wishes to intervene. That the government did not intervene in this case is not surprising, Brooker added. 

    “It says nothing at all about the merits of the case,” she said. “Over the last 15 years, the relator’s bar [has been] more and more successful with helping the DOJ to litigate declined qui tam cases because DOJ cannot do it all.”

    The U.S. Attorney for the District of Massachusetts declined to comment. The DOJ did not respond to a message for comment. 

    In February, a federal judge dismissed a lawsuit that alleged six major publishers violated antitrust law by colluding to “fix the price of peer review services at $0.”  Filed by UCLA neuroscientist Lucina Uddin, the case also included Elsevier, Springer Nature and Wiley as defendants, as well as Sage, Taylor & Francis and Wolters Kluwer.

    Retraction Watch story exposing research factory fuels investigations, preprint withdrawals, termination

    A preprint server has withdrawn three papers by an author at the center of a Retraction Watch story revealing a prolific research factory operated through the WhatsApp messaging system.  

    Last week, we reported on Raghabendra Kumar Mahato, a second-year medical student in Nepal, who runs an international research network that churns out conference abstracts and journal papers at a rapid-fire pace. Mahato’s network includes nearly 1,000 members connected through WhatsApp and produces suspected fast-churn science, while recruiting authorship for the works. Papers by the network have been accepted by major journals and presented at conferences by leading medical societies. 

    On July 24, Research Square, a preprint server owned by Springer Nature, withdrew three manuscripts by Mahato due to concerns “regarding integrity arising from the submitting author’s involvement in a paper mill network,” according to notices placed on the work. The notices include a link to Retraction Watch’s investigation. 

    Following our coverage, Mahato has also been kicked out of the Global Association of Indian Medical Students, where he served on the editorial board, according to correspondence we’ve seen.  

    Mahato did not return messages seeking comment. 

    A Research Square spokesperson told us removal of the preprints followed concerns raised by “an independent assessment.”

    “Consistent with our public policies, preprints may be withdrawn where concerns have been identified that could undermine trust in the content reported,” the spokesperson told us. “Authors are contacted directly to keep them informed of any decision taken.” 

    Anit Sinha, a disillusioned member of Mahato’s network who previously shared details with us about the operation, said he was “shocked” but “deeply hopeful” to see Research Square’s prompt removal of the preprints. Sinha recently graduated from Gandaki Medical College in Nepal, the same school Mahato now attends. He joined Mahato’s network to boost his CV, but became suspicious of the network’s activities after he started working with the group, he previously told us. 

    “I had no idea whether any concrete action would be taken against these ethical violations, so seeing Research Square remove the preprints so promptly was incredibly validating,” Sinha said in an email. He credited our coverage with prompting the move.

    Following our story, Sinha wrote to Research Square with concerns about the preprints and shared our coverage. In a July 25 email to Sinha, “Mark B.” with Springer Nature publishing support, thanked Sinha for “bringing” the matter to “our attention,” and wrote the preprints were withdrawn “after looking into the information you provided,” according to correspondence we’ve seen.

    The three papers examined various mortality trends based on the CDC WONDER database, a publicly accessible repository that collects public health data. There has been a steep rise in published studies using publicly available datasets, many of which show hallmarks of being produced by paper mills. One recent preprint found exponential growth in publications based CDC WONDER, with an increasing number of authors from Pakistan.

    Of 10 institutions listed across the three withdrawn preprints, eight were in Pakistan. 

    Palwasha Asghar, a coauthor on two of the papers, disagreed with the removal and told us her studies were “conceived, conducted, and submitted honestly, independently, and in good faith in accordance with established academic practice.” Asghar, a student at Khyber Medical College in Peshawar, Pakistan, cowrote another paper based on data from CDC WONDER with Mahato in BMC Neurology. Her ORCID profile shows 16 preprints and journal articles that were published between 2025 and 2026.   

    “The withdrawal of our Research Square preprints was both shocking and deeply distressing,” Asghar told us. “As a final-year medical student, I was particularly concerned because the preprints were withdrawn without prior notice, without being allowed to respond, and without any evidence supporting the allegations being disclosed to me.”

    Asghar said neither she nor her coauthors were aware of any “allegations or publicly reported concerns” regarding Mahato before their works were completed. The collaboration with Mahato did not arise through “any WhatsApp group,” but rather the authors were introduced to Mahato “through another researcher,” who recommended him as a potential collaborator, she told us. 

    Mahato’s contributions were limited to reviewing and revising manuscripts and managing the submission process, Asghar told us. The other authors meanwhile, carried out the data analyses and manuscript preparation, she said.

    Asghar provided us with documentation which she said shows the development of one of the withdrawn preprints, along with a folder containing screenshots and supporting records that “demonstrate the chronological progression of the research,” she told us. The screenshots appear to show author exchanges on WhatsApp.

    Asghar said the withdrawal of the preprints, without prior notice, an opportunity to respond, or disclosure of the underlying evidence has caused “significant academic and professional harm” and she has since sought legal advice.  

    Sinha also wrote to the Global Association of Indian Medical Students (GAIMS), to express his concerns about Mahato and share our recent coverage. Mahato joined the GAIMS Axis editorial board in June 2026, according to correspondence we’ve seen. Following an “internal review of the concerns raised,” GAIMS has terminated Mahato’s editorship and association with the GAIMS Axis Editorial Board with “immediate effect,” according to a July 28 email from GAIMS national president Abhishek Kashyap to Sinha. 

    Kashyap did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

    Sinha noted Mahato’s network, formally called the Global Clinical Research Consortium, “conveniently” rebranded itself as  “Research Helpers’” immediately following the publication of our story. He said he hopes other journals, preprint servers and associations remove more papers tied to the group.

    “Beyond polluting the research sphere, operators like Mahato are directly stealing opportunities from honest, hardworking students,” he told us. “They are taking hospital placements and scholarships from people who actually do the grueling work.”
