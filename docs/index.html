    University press removes book about intelligent design for misquoting ID proponent

    New York University Press is pulling all copies of a book about intelligent design after the author falsely quoted another scholar in the work.  

    Designer Science: A History of Intelligent Design in America, by C.W. Howell, was published by NYU Press in 2025. Earlier this month, the publisher said it was “pulling the current edition from the market” and “destroying all existing inventory” after learning Howell attributed an inaccurate quote to Michael Behe, a professor at Lehigh University in Pennsylvania, according to an Aug. 3 email Retraction Watch has seen. 

    The misquote stemmed from a passage in a court ruling Howell “mistakenly” read as a direct quote from Behe and attributed as such, he told us. Howell writes about the intersection of faith and science, and serves as an academic director for the C.S. Lewis Foundation. 

    The publisher has removed the work from its website and plans to reissue the book after the misattributed quote is corrected, James Devitt, managing director of public affairs for New York University, told us Friday. While no longer available on the NYU Press site or on Amazon, the book is still visible on the Publishers Weekly site.  

    “After reviewing his notes earlier this month, C.W. Howell, the author, realized the passage was written by Judge John E. Jones about Behe (an expert witness in the case)—and not said by Behe himself—as part of the judge’s opinion in Kitzmiller v Dover,” Devitt told us in an email. 

    Kitzmiller v. Dover was a landmark federal case that tested whether intelligent design (ID) should be taught in public schools as an alternative to Darwinian evolution. In 2004, the parents of 11 students filed a lawsuit against the Dover, Pennsylvania, school district over its decision to include ID in its schools’ science curriculum. The court ultimately ruled teaching ID in public schools was unconstitutional. Behe, who has written several books on ID and has faced extensive criticism from the mainstream scientific community, was a witness for the defense. 

    Casey Luskin, a research director at Discovery Institute, alerted the publisher to the error. Discovery Institute is a public policy think tank that promotes ID. Luskin discovered the quote as he prepared to write a review of Designer Science, which he told us “did not pass the smell test” because “the quote did not sound like Dr. Behe.” 

    The passage by Howell, on page 132 of his book, states Behe made a “damning admission” during court testimony when he “confessed” on the stand that “plausibility of the argument for ID depends upon the extent to which one believes in the existence of God.” 

    After further research, Luskin determined the false quote resulted from “a game of bad-scholarship-telephone,” he told us. 

    The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU), which represented the plaintiffs in Kitzmiller, wrote a court brief that purported to summarize an article by Behe published in 2001 in Biology and Philosophy. The brief makes the “plausibility” assertion about Behe’s beliefs with a citation to his article. In his Dec. 20, 2005 ruling, Jones repeated the words from ACLU’s brief, italicized them, and added the phrase “emphasis added.” Howell then quoted the court passage in his book, attributing the words to Behe. 

    “I believe correcting the mistake is the best course of action, and I welcome the opportunity to do so,” Howell told us. “I have worked closely with NYU Press to remove and replace the quote for the paperback reissue and the digital editions.” 

    Behe told us that the quote is not only inaccurate but “the polar opposite of my views.” 

    “Apparently, Dr. Howell thought the italics meant it was a direct quotation of my testimony on the stand, rather than a tendentious summary of my journal article,” said Behe, a senior fellow at Discovery Institute. “That translated into an entirely fictional courtroom drama in his book.” 

    Behe said NYU Press did “the admirable thing” in retracting the book, and that he appreciated Howell’s “apparent willingness to accede.”

    Japan institute defends Nobel Prize winner amid allegations of image duplication

    The Center for iPS Cell Research and Application (CiRA) at Kyoto University in Japan is defending Nobel Prize winner Shinya Yamanaka against claims of  image manipulation in multiple studies on which he was an author. The institute says questioning Yamanaka’s integrity without sufficient evidence is “completely unacceptable.”

    Yamanaka shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2012 and works at CiRA. 

    On August 7, Gotham City Research LLC, a short-selling firm based in the United States, released a report alleging image manipulation in several papers by Yamanaka appearing between 2000 and 2020.

    The targets of the report and another issued a few days earlier were two Japanese firms, Sumitomo Chemical And Sumitomo Pharmaceutical. These companies have provided funding to CiRA, where Yamanaka and his colleague Kazutoshi Takahashi conducted early proof-of-concept research on reprogramming mature cells into induced pluripotent stem cells. 

    Earlier this year, Japan’s health ministry gave conditional approval to Amchepry, a treatment for Parkinson’s disease based on reprogrammed stem cells. But some researchers worry that the first-of-its-kind treatment has only been tested in seven patients.

    Gotham has shorted all stocks of the two companies, the firm’s founder Daniel Yu, who is based in New York, told Retraction Watch. Gotham’s previous short-selling campaigns have yielded some success but it has rarely previously targeted pharmaceutical companies. “I generally root for them to succeed,” Yu told us. 

    In 2024, Gotham shorted the stocks for Spanish healthcare company Grifols SA, arguing that the firm was masking its true debt. Gotham’s report wiped a third of the company’s value and the company had to undergo governance restructuring to win back investors. 

    Short sellers have targeted scientific findings before. In 2021, a group expressed doubts about biotech Cassava Science’s experimental Alzheimer’s drug, which eventually failed clinical trials. Cassava sued them after a researcher associated with the treatment was charged with fraud, but those charges were later dropped. Cassava dropped its defamation lawsuit in 2024. 

    Gotham’s report on Yamanaka raises allegations of image duplication in 10 papers, most of which are already flagged on PubPeer. Yamanaka — who served as CiRA’s director between 2008 and 2022 — was either first or corresponding author on three of the papers. 

    For two of them, the issues have already been investigated, CiRA’s statement reads, “and the necessary actions have already been completed.” One of those papers, published in Science in 2008, has been corrected. The other, published by The EMBO Journal in 2000, the editors saw “no concrete evidence” to suggest images in the paper were duplicated, the journal said in a 2014 statement.

    For the third study, published in 2003 in Cell, Yamanaka “reviewed the matter in response to the current report and concluded that the two images alleged to be similar are not identical, and that this does not constitute duplicate use or misidentification of an image,” the CiRA statement reads.  

    Another study mentioned in Gotham’s report, published in 2011 in Human Molecular Genetics, was corrected last year for an apparent image duplication. According to the correction notice, the raw data were not fully available “but the authors determined that during the final figure assembly, inadvertent errors occurred.”

    For four other studies in the Gotham report, Yamanaka was listed as a coauthor because he provided research materials such as cells or mice, the CiRA statement explained.

    “Treating these different cases collectively as though they demonstrate an ongoing pattern of research misconduct by Professor Yamanaka or by CiRA disregards the facts specific to each case and is completely unacceptable,” CiRA stated. “We strongly object to statements that, without sufficient basis, damage a researcher’s integrity or reputation.”

    Gotham also included two studies in its report on which Yamanaka was not a coauthor. 

    One of them was retracted in 2018 by Stem Cell Reports. As Retracted Watch reported at the time, Kyoto University “punitively dismissed” Yamanaka’s colleague Kohei Yamamizu after an investigation found that he had fabricated and falsified data in that study. In 2019, another paper listing Yamamizu — but not Yamanaka — was retracted. 

    While the Kyoto probe had found that Yamamizu was solely responsible for the manipulation, Yamanaka issued a statement at the time saying he felt “a strong responsibility for not having prevented research misconduct at our institute.” Yamanaka was also penalized by CiRA, according to his statement, but the nature of that punishment is unclear.  

    For the final paper, published in 2020 in the Journal of Neuroscience Research, the study’s corresponding author Jun Takahashi, who served as CiRA’s director between April 2022 and March 2026, is reviewing the concerns and will take “appropriate action,” according to the CiRA statement.

    We reached out to Yamanaka for a comment but heard back from Hiroyuki Wadahama from CiRA’s international public communications office. 

    Wadahama told us CiRA officials are not aware of any ongoing institutional probes into these papers. “We have not been in touch with Gotham City Research regarding their report, nor did we receive inquiries from them prior to its publication,” he said. “We believe their report disregards the specific facts of each paper and draws unfounded conclusions.”
