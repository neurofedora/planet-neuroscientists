    Wildlife journal issues corrections for missing ethics statements

    In what one sleuth called a “systemic failure,” a wildlife journal has issued corrections to a handful of papers not for an error in the articles but to include an item that wasn’t: ethics statements. 

    The most recent such correction in the European Journal of Wildlife Research (EJWR) was on September 8. According to the notice, authors of a March article on mammal diversity in Mexico provided additional information on the camera trapping method employed in the study —  namely, that their research did not require approval from an animal ethics committee. 

    At least three other similar corrections have appeared in EJWR in the last four years, with several more articles with missing ethics statements flagged on PubPeer by commenter “Pseudotocinclus tietensis.” For example, for a 2021 paper on “understanding human controversies on the return of the wolf through the use of socio-cultural values,” which included a survey of nearly 1,500 people, Pseudotocinclus pointed out the ethics approval section for the paper says “Not applicable.” 

    The submission guidelines on the journal’s website state authors should include a section on “compliance with ethical standards” that includes informed consent and research involving humans or animals.

    “Taken together, the pattern suggests a systemic failure at EJWR to enforce basic ethics policies, both through inadequate corrections for missing ethics declarations and through the publication of papers that lack any meaningful ethical oversight,” Pseudotocinclus told Retraction Watch when they emailed us to flag the issue. They have asked to remain anonymous because of past correspondence with one of the editors. 

    Jacqueline Frair, editor-in-chief of The Wildlife Society’s Journal of Wildlife Management, told us ethics and animal care statements “are an important component of scientific transparency, particularly in wildlife research where studies may involve animal capture, handling, monitoring or interaction with people and communities.” She declined to comment on whether the cases at EJWR indicate a systemic issue, given she is not familiar with the journal’s editorial history. 

    Jacco Flipsen, the vice president of life sciences at Springer Nature, which publishes the journal, told us he would “caution against drawing conclusions about the journal’s processes from a small number of unrelated cases.”

    The journal issued its first correction addressing the missing ethics statements in May 2022, stating the authors “failed to include the complete ethical statement” for a paper on animals in a national park in Senegal. In addition to noting the authors took ethical considerations into account, the updated statement said: “It was confirmed by the Senegal Ministry of Health that no ethics approval was required.”

    The second was for a 2020 paper on movements of goat herds in Mexico. The correction, published May 2024, states “it was not necessary to request permission from national research ethics committees since the vertebrate species studied is not regulated and not native to Mexico.” 

    The journal published the third correction in July 2025 for a study published earlier that year on human-wildlife conflicts in areas adjacent to a research center in Zimbabwe. The statement says the research was guided by ethical guidelines “which prioritized ethical treatment of participants, ensuring no physical or psychological harm” and noted no formal approval was needed. 

    Pseudotocinclus told us they flagged each of these papers to the journal directly before discovering the PubPeer platform in October 2023.

    For a 2022 article about predator control by gamekeepers, Pseudotocinclus wrote the study “does not comply with normal standards of animal ethics, and was not ethically reviewed before the killing of animals was carried out for research.” 

    Flipsen told us the research in the publications “was conducted in different countries, under different regulatory and ethical frameworks.” 

    “Each case must therefore be assessed individually in light of its specific circumstances,” he said, and noted assessments of the papers were ongoing. 

    When we asked whether checking for ethics statements was a part of the editorial process, and how the papers were published without them, a representative for Springer Nature declined to comment. 

    Earlier this month, we wrote about the retraction of a PeerJ paper on tourists’ reactions to snakes for ethical oversight issues — the second snake retraction we’ve covered. 

    A paper on wildlife in Tanzania recently received a correction from EJWR for another reason: “Nelson Mandela” was “incorrectly” listed as a coauthor on a paper published the month prior rather than as the affiliate institution “Nelson Mandela African Institution of Science and Technology.”

    Editor-in-chief resigns amid probes of alleged citation manipulation, data errors 

    The editor-in-chief of The Egyptian Rheumatologist has resigned following claims of questionable citation practices and improper peer review at the journal. 

    At least three publishers are also investigating alleged data errors in papers in other journals by the former journal editor, Tamer Gheita, and his research group. Gheita, a professor of rheumatology at Cairo University in Giza, has denied the claims, calling the complaints “subjective” and “malicious.”

    The Egyptian Society of Rheumatic Diseases (ESRd) started investigating Gheita in June following concerns about his editorial practices at the society’s journal, including unusually rapid peer-review timelines and questionable citation patterns, according to Bassel Elzorkany, interim editor-in-chief for the journal. Elzorkany told Retraction Watch the ESRd board temporarily suspended Gheita, and Gheita resigned shortly afterward. 

    “We acted promptly once concerns were raised,” said Elzorkany, secretary general for the society. “As the investigation is still in progress, we cannot characterize its outcome or comment further on a specific allegation at this stage.” 

    An anonymous complaint sent to us accused Gheita of allegedly inflating his citation metrics by forcing authors to cite his work in the journal and accepting articles for publication in days, without proper peer review.

    Gheita told us he did not “instruct, pressure, or encourage authors to cite” his publications for “reasons unrelated to scientific relevance.” In an email, Gheita said he served as editor-in-chief for the journal from 2013 to July 2026. According to journal records Gheita was previously an associate editor for the journal and became editor-in-chief in July 2012. 

    “I do not agree that the available citation patterns establish a scheme to manipulate citations,” he told us. “Authors determine the references included in their manuscripts based on the scientific literature relevant to their work. There was no journal policy requiring or encouraging authors to cite me or any other specific individual.”

    Gheita said he conducted his own analysis of four journal issues published between October 2025 and June 2026, in which he identified 2,009 references in total. Of these, 69 references (3.4%) cited papers with him as a coauthor. References to articles published in The Egyptian Rheumatologist represented 117 of the 2,009 references, or about 5.8%, Gheita said. 

    But according to data in the anonymous complaint and confirmed through an analysis by Mariana Ribeiro, Retraction Watch Sleuth in Residence, between January 2011 and January 2026, 49% of articles in The Egyptian Rheumatologist cited Gheita’s publications. In  2011, there were no citations to Gheita’s work, rising to 12% of papers published in 2012 and up to 73% of those from 2020, according to data verified by Ribeiro. 

    A 2015 article in the journal about lupus, for example, cites Gheita at least 10 times. 

    During Gheita’s tenure, the journal also accepted hundreds of articles within three days of submission, many on the same day. A paper about Henoch–Schönlein purpura published online in July 2016, for example, shows a submission date of May 1 and an acceptance date of May 1. Another paper, about arthritis, shows the journal received the paper Nov. 30, 2022 and accepted it the same day. 

    Gheita said he would “caution” against drawing conclusions about peer review solely from dates displayed in public databases or submission systems. Historical publishing workflows and changes in electronic submission systems can result in dates appearing “deceptively close together,” he told us. 

    “A conclusion that a manuscript was accepted without peer review should, in my view, be based on the actual editorial records and workflow history held by the publisher, rather than solely on publicly available submission and acceptance dates,” he told us. “The underlying editorial history, including previous versions, transfers, reviewer assignments, and editorial decisions, would be the appropriate evidence for determining what actually occurred.”

    Elzorkany told us the ESRd board referred the anonymous complaints about Gheita’s work to the journal’s publisher, Elsevier, and requested a full investigation.  

    A spokesperson for Elsevier told us the publisher is “aware” of the claims involving the journal and that “an investigation is ongoing.” 

    “We cannot provide further information until this is complete,” the spokesperson told us. 

    Meanwhile, a sleuth has raised concerns on PubPeer about at least 13 papers that include Gheita as an author, involve subsets of his data or encompass his common research group.  The Egyptian Rheumatologist published one of the flagged papers and the other 12 were published in other journals. 

    After receiving an anonymous complaint, psychologist Nick Brown started investigating the research group earlier this year, finding what appears to be “numerous inconsistencies and errors in the statistical reporting,” he told us. The papers in question were published from 2019 to 2026 in various journals, many with large authorship groups and overlapping authors.

    Brown told us the article problems include “impossible p values,” issues with data formatting and rounding, and overall numbers that aren’t compatible with subgroups in the data, among other issues. 

    “I find the whole set of articles thoroughly implausible,” he told us.

    For example, Brown wrote on PubPeer that for seven measures in a 2021 article in Lupus about systemic lupus erythematosus children in Egypt, the overall mean doesn’t make sense  when compared to other data in the paper. In addition, 43 values in three columns of a data table are mathematically impossible given the number of participants in the study, according to Brown’s analysis.  

    Similarly, for three measures in a 2026 article in Scientific Reports about systemic sclerosis in children, the overall mean and standard deviation are inconsistent with other data in the paper,  Brown wrote on PubPeer. He noted further problems with incorrect or incompatible values in the paper. Brown also reached out to several journals to share his concerns, he said. 

    Gheita said he takes the PubPeer concerns “seriously.” He and his coauthors are reviewing the comments and will “respond to substantiated concerns on the basis of the underlying evidence,” he told us. 

    “If an error is confirmed, we support addressing it through the appropriate scientific and editorial processes,” he said. “At the same time, we have not received formal comments or requests for clarification from the journals concerning several of the matters referred to in your email.”

    Lupus, a Sage title, published some of the flagged articles. A spokesperson for Sage confirmed the articles are under investigation, but declined to share further information. 

    Scientific Reports, Rheumatology International and Clinical Rheumatology published several other flagged papers. Tim Kersjes, head of research integrity for Springer Nature, which publishes the three titles, told us Springer is “aware of concerns with these papers” and is “investigating them carefully.” 

    “We will take editorial action as appropriate once our investigations are complete,” Kersjes told us. 

    Wolters Kluwer, which publishes the Saudi Journal of Medicine and Medical Sciences, where one of the flagged papers appears, declined to comment, citing its policy not to comment on “ongoing editorial matters.” 

    A spokesperson for Taylor & Francis confirmed the publisher is investigating another paper in Open Access Rheumatology: Research and Reviews.

    Ahmed Elsaman, a professor of rheumatology at Sohag University in Egypt and an author on  seven of the papers, called some of the issues raised on PubPeer “relatively limited technical observations.” The publications being scrutinized originate from the Egyptian College of Rheumatology Study Groups, a longstanding multicenter research collaboration involving rheumatologists and investigators from across Egypt, Elsaman said in an email. 

    Since 2016, the groups have generated clinical and epidemiological data on several rheumatic diseases through large, multicentre cohorts, according to Elsaman. He pointed to a 2023 article by the group in Rheumatology International that included 10,364 patients recruited from 26 specialised centres across 22 Egyptian cities. (Brown critiqued this paper on PubPeer.)

    “This is just an example to show how huge is our work,” Elsaman told us. “We fully welcome legitimate post-publication scientific scrutiny. Large multicentre studies may contain typographical, transcriptional, reporting, or statistical errors, and we are prepared to investigate and correct any genuine error through the appropriate editorial process.”

    But Elsaman said his concern is the “apparent escalation of relatively limited technical observations into implications of fabrication or research misconduct without evidence proportionate to such serious allegations.”

    Elsaman added he and his research group attempted to respond to the critiques on PubPeer, but he said the replies were not accepted.  

    Samar Tharwat of Mansoura University in Egypt is an author on at least 11 of the papers critiqued by Brown, and Faten Ismail of Minia University in Egypt is an author on at least nine of the articles. Neither Tharwat nor Ismail returned messages seeking comment.  We contacted authors appearing in five or more of the papers. Elsaman is the only author who returned our messages. 

    Tharwat is also the author of a retracted 2023 COVID-19 paper in Open Medicine. Editors withdrew the work because of “significant overlap with other papers published by the same authors in Journal of Clinical Virology Plus,” according to the retraction notice.

    ‘Hot mess’ paper questioning CO2 and climate change gets retracted

    A paper that called into question many central data linking carbon dioxide to the rapidly warming climate has been retracted for “significant concerns,” including “unsupported conclusions” and “inaccuracies” in the interpretation of the data.

    The paper, published in August 2023 in the MDPI journal Atmosphere, was written by Stuart Harris, a former geography professor at the University of Calgary. It has 26,000 views and was mentioned 869 times on the social media platform X, according to Altmetric. It was also covered by the climate skeptic website The Daily Sceptic. The article was cited four times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science.

    “It is a hot mess,” Marcus Sarofim, a climate scientist at Johns Hopkins University, told Retraction Watch. “I certainly don’t think this paper has any business being in the peer-reviewed literature.” He also pointed out the paper was submitted and accepted in under a month, saying the short timeframe “is always a bad sign.”

    Harris did not respond to multiple emails requesting comment.

    John Kennedy, a climate scientist with the World Meteorological Organization at the time, blogged about the paper shortly after it was published, highlighting a handful of its many errors.

    “From a distance, it looks just like a regular paper, but up close it’s chaos,” he wrote. 

    In one paragraph, Harris claimed carbon dioxide is primarily measured at one observatory in Hawaii. “The increase in this gas is measured primarily at a single station at the summit of Mount Kea on the island of Hawaii, and the increase parallels the change in air temperature at that station since about 1900 A.D.,” Harris wrote in the paper. 

    But the observatory is on Mauna Loa — not Mauna Kea — and only began measuring carbon dioxide in 1958. 

    “The contention that carbon dioxide is measured primarily at this single station is also completely wrong,” Kennedy wrote in 2023. More than 221 monitoring stations and many additional satellites currently track carbon dioxide emissions. 

    Harris also criticized the logic inherent to global heat maps, which illustrate how different parts of Earth have warmed over a given time period. 

    “An obvious problem is found when examining the map of the distribution of climate change (mean yearly air temperature) obtained by NASA from satellites,” Harris wrote. “The main areas of warming are in Northern Canada and the Arctic, with lesser warming in the Sahara and the Australian Outback! Eastern China and Germany show no obvious warming. Obviously, this does not fit in with the main industrial centers in the world!”

    As Kennedy noted, the data are not pulled from NASA satellites but from “weather stations, ships, and buoys.” 

    Harris also attributed recent climate change to solar radiation, which is not uncommon among climate science denialists, Sarofim said. While solar radiation certainly affects Earth’s climate, “believing that CO2 is not involved in climate change is on par with believing that the Moon is made of green cheese.”

    Aside from the misunderstandings and misrepresentations of climate research, the study also claimed Neanderthals went extinct only 3,200 years ago. (The true number is closer to 40,000 years ago.)

    A spokesperson for MDPI told us the journal launched an investigation “following concerns raised after publication,” but did not respond to questions regarding specific concerns  or where the concerns originated. They found “significant concerns,” “including an insufficient assessment of the relevant literature, unsupported conclusions, inaccuracies in the interpretation of the presented data, and issues relating to the use and attribution of references,” according to the retraction notice. MDPI declined to provide details of the investigation, citing confidentiality.

    Harris responded to the investigation but the journal determined the responses “did not adequately resolve the issues.” The journal retracted the paper on September 16. 

    David Ho, a climate scientist at the University of Hawaii, responded to our request for comment on the paper by saying he was not interested in reading it. “I’m sorry, but I’d rather keep my brain cells,” he said.
