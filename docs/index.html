    Elsevier to ‘soon investigate’ two Macchiarini papers more than a year after retraction request

    Elsevier says it will begin investigating two papers coauthored by disgraced surgeon Paolo Macchiarini “soon,” more than a year after two critics of his work formally requested the publisher retract the articles — and nearly a decade after two institutions found him guilty of misconduct for related work. 

    The papers, published in Biomaterials in 2009 and 2010, include descriptions of a 2008 trachea transplant in Barcelona conducted by Macchiarini — described in The Lancet that year, as well as in a five-year follow-up published in 2013. Both Lancet papers reported a successful surgery and that the patient had a “normal social and working life.” In reality, she needed stents, her lung function did not improve and she eventually had a lung removed. 

    The Barcelona patient survived, but at least three others who underwent Macchiarini’s synthetic trachea transplants eventually died. In 2023, a Swedish court sentenced Macchiarini to 2.5 years in prison for gross assault related to his failed transplants. So far he has accumulated 13 retractions. 

    Macchiarini told Retraction Watch our questions were the first he had heard of an effort to retract the two Biomaterials papers, and that neither the journal nor Elsevier had contacted him about them, when we reached him through his Substack account.

    “The 2008 Lancet paper has been retracted, and the reasons given for that decision are publicly available,” Macchiarini said, noting that he is neither first nor last author of the two Biomaterials papers in question. “That fact does not, by itself, permit me to determine whether every allegation now being advanced concerning two separate Biomaterials publications is scientifically or factually established.”

    In 2017, an ethical review board in Sweden had called for six retractions after finding Macchiarini and several coauthors had committed misconduct. A year later,  the Karolinska Institutet, where Macchiarini was employed until his dismissal in 2016, issued similar findings and also called for the retraction of the six papers. These included two other Biomaterials papers. The KI said the authors had “fabricated and distorted” descriptions of the patients and their surgical outcomes. 

    The Lancet retracted the two papers reporting the Barcelona transplant in 2023, five years after the Swedish ethical review board and Karolinska found Macchiarini committed misconduct.. Those retractions came after another Swedish authority — the National Board for Assessment of Research Misconduct — found Macchiarini guilty of misconduct in his falsified descriptions of the 2008 surgery.

    The two Biomaterials papers claimed that a year after the 2008 operation, the graft and the patient were healthy, and that the graft was functioning well. They stated the patient was “fully active and well,” and leading a “near normal life.” 

    Peter Wilmshurst, a retired cardiologist from the United Kingdom known for exposing flawed medical research, and Patricia Murray, a stem cell biologist at the University of Liverpool, have been pursuing retractions of Macchiarini’s work for years. They requested the retraction of both articles in a letter to Kam Leong, editor-in-chief of Biomaterials, in July last year. 

    The two papers were part of an effort to “support the original lies,” Murray and Wilmshurst wrote in their letter. The authors “attempt to give undeserved scientific credence to the original false 2008 Lancet paper by suggesting that the authors had spent a long period in incremental scientific developments,” they wrote. 

    The 2009 article described a “step-wise work plan” that included animal testing before the first human operation. “They didn’t do any animal work before they put these in people,” Wilmshurst told Retraction Watch. “And they claimed that this was years of research, and we know it was actually cooked up in a couple of weeks.” 

    In their July 2025 letter to Leong, Wilmshurst and Murray also pointed out inconsistent data across the papers. Two histology images in the 2009 article were described as biopsies taken after the transplant. But in the 2010 paper, the same images are said to depict biopsies taken before the procedure. Thirteen of the 14 authors on the 2009 article were also authors of the original paper in The Lancet describing the transplant, including Philipp Jungebluth, also found guilty of scientific misconduct by Karolinska Institute in 2018. In a recent blog post, Wilmshurst and Murray also call out other authors for their role in harming patients. 

    Loredana De Bartolo, a research director at a membrane technology institute in Italy and coauthor of the 2010 Biomaterials paper Wilmshurst flagged, said she had not heard from either Elsevier or the journal about the article. She said the work identified limitations rather than supporting the transplants, and that the calculations were based on the scientific literature rather than Macchiarini’s studies. 

    Regarding the inconsistent histology images, she said Macchiarini  provided the data, which were incorporated “in good faith” and “could be omitted without in any way compromising the scientific basis, validity, or conclusions of the study.”

    “For these reasons, I do not believe that the content of the article provides any grounds for retraction,” she told us. Sara Mantero, of the Polytechnic University of Milan, and corresponding author of the other Biomaterials paper, has not responded to requests for comment. 

    In July last year, after emailing Leong, who never responded to the letter, Wilmshurst corresponded with staff at Elsevier who told him his concerns had been forwarded to the editor-in-chief and publisher for review, according to emails we have seen. “In early 2018 the editors of the Lancet were aware that the Macchiarini et al paper was false, but it took more than 5 years for the Lancet to retract it,” Wilmshurst wrote in response. “We are concerned that this is a measure of the speed at which Elsevier journals deal with research fraud.”

    After a follow up in September 2025, operations staff at the publisher told Wilmshurst his concerns were being investigated, and to expect updates. 

    Two days later, Wilmshurst followed up again: “If Biomaterials is unwilling to act quickly, we will have little alternative to posting on-line our letter to Biomaterials.” 

    The next day, Emma Xu, publisher of Biomaterials, told Wilmshurst Macchiarini’s was a “serious case” being handled by a central ethics team. She said Biomaterials had already retracted several of Macchiarini’s articles. “Obviously there are more articles published by Macchiarini” in the journal, “and we need to do individual investigation before actions,” she said. 

    “Hopefully we could have the solutions soon,” Xu added. Wilmshurst last followed up at the end of July and said he has yet to receive a reply. “They initially said they’re looking into it,” Wilmshurst told us. “They’ve just stopped responding.” Xu and Leong did not respond to our request for comment. 

    Elsevier told Retraction Watch it had retracted nine papers by Macchiarini in recent years. Rebecca Clear, a spokesperson for the company, said it would “soon investigate the two articles” Wilmshurst flagged, despite telling him last September it already was looking into these papers.

    “If deemed appropriate by the journal editors they will be corrected,” Clear said. She could not confirm whether Elsevier had begun investigating the articles at all, nor whether it would only pursue corrections, not retractions. 

    “Someone may look at it and think, in a few years’ time, that this [transplant] actually worked,” Wilmshurst said. “They may just look at this one paper and think, oh gosh, this works, we’ll try this, and not look at the 2008 Lancet paper and see it’s retracted.” 

    “Everything that’s false needs to be retracted, doesn’t it?”

    Guest post: The uncomfortable truth an explosion of papers reveals about authors’ access to data

    The involvement of independent academic researchers in examining data and conducting analyses is central to producing trustworthy research. Because of this, many journals require authors to sign a statement acknowledging they “had full access to all the data in the study,” and take “responsibility for the integrity of the data and the accuracy of the data analysis.” Indeed, the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) recommendations for authorship note that authors should be “accountable for all aspects of the work.” 

    A rapidly increasing number of published articles rely on analyses produced via the TriNetX platform. However, TriNetX users don’t get full access to the information in the platform, muddying the truthfulness of any analyses of the data and compromising the integrity statements offered by authors and accepted by journals.

    Instead, users see aggregate counts, descriptive statistics and estimated quantities, such as hazard ratios, without ever interacting with line-level data. And because the underlying data are constantly changing as new records are added, analyses run on the platform are often irreproducible, even by the researchers who originally conducted them.  

    A PubMed search for “TriNetX” returns over 4,400 articles (as of August 19, 2026) and TriNetX has recently celebrated reaching over 2,000 citations, describing itself as “the most cited real-world data source in peer-reviewed research.” While it is possible to request line-level data for download from TriNetX, the majority of studies we encounter, including those in high impact journals, use the dashboard interface. A recent article by Retraction Watch and Science highlighted the use of this dashboard by medical trainees to produce fast, but unreliable papers. 

    What has received less attention is what dashboard-based research means for author responsibility and accountability. In our opinion, lack of access to the underlying line-level data and outsourcing of the analysis to a proprietary dashboard is a major deviation from standard research practices. This is because it is not possible for these authors to take “responsibility for the integrity of the data and accuracy of the analysis.” Important decisions, such as mapping different medical coding systems onto one another, cleaning data (e.g., dealing with clinical events that occur outside of their associated encounter, identifying inconsistent diagnoses, etc.) and writing analysis code are outsourced to a company.

    While standardizing some aspects of electronic health record data processing might be desirable, the specific decisions the TriNetX platform makes cannot be checked or verified by authors and readers alike, as the software is proprietary.  

    Allowing a for-profit enterprise to make important analytic decisions at scale in the literature would typically be a cause for concern and would motivate measures to mitigate bias. TriNetX likely has no stake in the outcome of individual analyses that are run on their platform, other than perhaps an interest in research that generates headlines. Just as new standards and norms have had to be established given the rising use of LLMs in research and writing, we need norms regarding dashboard-based analysis.  

    First, editors and reviewers need to decide whether authors should be permitted to outsource important analytic decisions to a dashboard. Given that line-level electronic health records data are available via other sources (e.g., Epic Cosmos, All of Us), we would suggest the answer is no. One could argue that the sheer number of articles using this platform is proof of its acceptability. But it’s not clear that reviewers and editors are generally aware of the extent that the TriNetX platform deviates from normal research practice, especially when articles come with misleading statements regarding data access. It is certainly not apparent to readers. 

    If this kind of dashboard-based research is acceptable, we would argue that reporting standards need to be updated to more clearly state how the data were handled and the analysis was performed. This should be transparent to reviewers, readers and editors. 

    This would require attestations different from those many journals currently require. We suggest a disclosure along the lines of: “The authors did not have access to the data for this study. Cohort creation, descriptive statistics, and analyses were performed on the TriNetX platform, a graphical user interface that provides access to aggregate data. The code to implement patient selection and analysis is not available to the authors, reviewers, or readers as it is the intellectual property of TriNetX LLC. Exports from the TriNetX platform showing complete cohort definition and results are provided in supplementary material.” 

    We further propose that TriNetX, or representatives of the company involved in the analysis, should be acknowledged as non-author contributors to an article, consistent with ICMJE criteria. While the consensus has emerged that LLMs cannot take credit on articles because they cannot take responsibility, a private company or its employees certainly can. 

    Finally, published articles containing incorrect statements regarding the level of access that authors had to data should be corrected, perhaps to include a more accurate statement like the example above. If journal editors determine the authors have misrepresented their level of access to data and responsibility for analyses, those papers should be retracted. 

    Stephen Rhodes, Ph.D., is a biostatistician for the Urology Institute at University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center. He co-authored articles using the TriNetX platform prior to thinking through the issues raised here. 

    Fredrick R. Schumacher, Ph.D., is an Associate Professor in the Department of Population and Quantitative Health Sciences, School of Medicine at Case Western Reserve University.

    The 2026 Global Young Academy call for new members is now open

    We are pleased to share that applications for the 2027 cohort membership in the Global Young Academy (GYA) are now open until September 15, 2026. The GYA brings together young scientists committed to developing, connecting, mobilizing, and empowering early‑career researchers to drive international, interdisciplinary, and intergenerational dialogue. PLOS partners with the GYA to advance Open Science and support its principles in research practice.

    Apply now

    The academy invites applications from young, independent scholars who combine research excellence with a clear commitment to impact. Apply for membership on the Global Young Academy website, and learn more about the GYA in our interview with former GYA co-chairs Yensi Flores Bueso and Sam Chan Siok Yee, and current co-chair Natisha Dukh below.

    PLOS: Please introduce yourselves and your role at the GYA.

    Yensi: I am Yensi Flores Bueso, a Honduran molecular and synthetic biologist. I trained through internationally competitive scholarships from a BSc in Honduras to a PhD at University College Cork, and I now design proteins using AI-based computational tools, most recently at the Institute for Protein Design in Seattle. Alongside my research, I work on research culture and science policy, and I engage on bodies such as serving on the inaugural Steering Board of the Coalition for Advancing Research Assessment and the UN’s Independent Group of Scientists for the 2027 Global Sustainable Development Report. I served as Co-Chair of the GYA until June 2026, together with Sam. Our current Co-Chairs are Francesco Maurelli and Natisha Dukhi, who are leading the academy through this membership call.

    Natisha: I’m Natisha Dukhi, a Senior Research Specialist at South Africa’s Human Sciences Research Council, working across public health, nutrition, adolescent health and science diplomacy. Within the GYA, I currently serve as the current Co-Chair, helping steer the Academy’s strategic direction, with a particular focus on making sure the perspectives and constraints of researchers from the Global South and Global North are genuinely reflected in how we operate. 

    Sam: I’m an academic at Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM), based in the School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, where I lead work in the Thoughts Formulation Lab. My research centers on the intersection of pharmaceutical innovation and sustainable, locally sourced materials. Within the Global Young Academy, I recently served as Immediate Past Co-Chair, having previously held the Co-Chair position. In this capacity, I’ve been closely involved in coordinating leadership and portfolio assignments across our team, supporting strategic direction, and representing GYA at international platforms. I’m active in GYA’s Women in Science efforts and broader science education and outreach initiatives, which connect naturally to my work back home running STEM outreach programs for children and underprivileged communities in Malaysia. 

    PLOS: What is the GYA, and what is it trying to achieve?

    All: The Global Young Academy is an international, member-led academy consisting of approximately 200 outstanding early- to mid-career researchers and over 500 alumni spanning more than 100 countries and every global region. Its mission is to empower young researchers to lead international, interdisciplinary, and intergenerational dialogue by giving a strong voice to scientists from diverse backgrounds, ensuring that perspectives from both the Global North and Global South are genuinely reflected in global science policy and advice. A place of possibilities and hope, the GYA enables meaningful scientific, curiosity-driven, and cultural exchange to strengthen the global science ecosystem and promote science for society. In practice, this involves advocating for evidence-informed decision-making, building leadership capacity, improving research environments and culture, and pushing for a more inclusive global system where a researcher’s origin does not determine their influence. By working as a genuine bridge between regions and disciplines, the GYA strives for inclusivity and empowerment, aligning with a vision to build scientific capacity where it is most needed.

    PLOS: What’s the relationship between the GYA and PLOS?

    PLOS and the GYA signed a Memorandum of Understanding in December 2023 to advance open science together. The partnership grew from a shared conviction that science works better when it is open, and that early-career researchers can shape what open science looks like. Our Open Science working group provides input to PLOS, and we have collaborated on shared priorities such as research assessment reform and ending publication bias, including joint work published in PLOS Biology. This Q&A is itself a nice example of the partnership in action, with PLOS helping us reach researchers who may want to apply.

    PLOS: What impact does the GYA have on its members’ professional development?

    ALL: Membership in the GYA functions as a high-level, five-year leadership apprenticeship, offering early-to-mid career researchers opportunities that are rarely available at their home institutions. Members gain hands-on experience in governance, fundraising, and project management by running international working groups and organizing global meetings, often at a scale that accelerates professional development. This experience is complemented by access to a genuinely global peer network, which fosters cross-disciplinary and cross-cultural collaborations that frequently lead to long-term partnerships and friendships. Furthermore, the GYA significantly amplifies members’ professional visibility and impact, providing training in science leadership and opening doors to influential platforms like the UN, UNESCO, and the International Science Council. Whether through securing speaking engagements at major forums or facilitating science diplomacy, members develop critical skills and networks—such as consensus building and strategic communication—that prepare them for future leadership roles in national academies, science advisory boards, and beyond. Ultimately, the five-year membership structure encourages a sense of purposeful contribution, helping researchers transcend the boundaries of their own disciplines to become leaders who can shape global science policy and practice.

    PLOS: How has being involved in the GYA impacted you?

    Yensi: Profoundly, both as a scientist and as a leader. The GYA gave me the platform to co-lead the first global study of research assessment practices, coordinating a voluntary team of nearly thirty researchers across some twenty countries, with no dedicated research funding. That work is now informing reform discussions internationally. It also opened doors I could not have imagined, from the CoARA Steering Board to the UN. And as Co-Chair I learned what it takes to actually hold an organisation together. But the deepest impact is the community. As someone who began her career in Honduras, finding peers who understand both the realities of the Global South and the workings of global science policy, and who genuinely want to change things, has shaped who I am.

    Natisha: Since joining in 2024, the GYA has genuinely reshaped how I see my own career. I came in as a working group member, and within a couple of years found myself leading initiatives, sitting on the Executive Committee, and now serving as Co-Chair – that trajectory alone tells you how quickly the Academy trusts you with responsibility if you show up and contribute. It’s pushed me well beyond my home discipline of public health nutrition and  science diplomacy into science communication, and institutional leadership, and it’s given me a genuinely global vantage point on issues, like early-career researcher mobility and visa access, that I would never have engaged with through my HSRC role alone. Beyond professional growth, it’s connected me to a network of peers across the world who are wrestling with the same structural barriers I see in my own context, which has been both grounding and energizing. If I had to sum it up: the GYA didn’t just add lines to my CV, it changed the scale at which I think about my own work. 

    Sam: Being part of the Global Young Academy has fundamentally reshaped how I see my role as a scientist. Coming from a pharmaceutical formulation background, I could easily have stayed within the boundaries of the lab — but GYA opened my eyes to how research connects to policy, education, and community impact on a global scale. This all started with coleading a project about my formulation research to my STEM outreach work with children and underprivileged communities through funding support of GYA. Serving in leadership capacities, including as Co-Chair, taught me skills no amount of bench work could — coordinating across cultures, time zones, and disciplines; building consensus among diverse voices; and translating scientific expertise into strategic action. Managing portfolio assignments across a team of colleagues from different continents has sharpened my organizational and diplomatic instincts in ways that now inform how I run my own lab and mentor students. All in all, GYA gave me a global peer network who understands the specific pressures of early-career science while also pushing me to think beyond my own discipline and country. 

    PLOS: What would you say to someone thinking about applying to be a member of the GYA?

    ALL: Apply, and don’t self-select out. The GYA is member-led, so what you take from it is proportional to what you bring, and you can bring more than you think. If you are an early- or mid-career researcher who wants your work to matter beyond your own field, this is your academy. I would especially encourage women, researchers from underrepresented regions and disciplines, and colleagues working outside traditional academia to apply. The call for the 2027 cohort is open until 15 September 2026.

    Editors of economics journal resign after Elsevier doesn’t renew EIC’s contract

    Six editors and nearly 60 advisory editors of Games and Economic Behavior resigned after the journal’s publisher decided not to renew the editor-in-chief’s contract. 

    In late July, Elsevier told Hervé Moulin, a business professor of the University of Glasgow, his six-year tenure as editor-in-chief of the journal would end in December. Moulin told Retraction Watch he was disappointed by the news, as he had expressed a desire to continue in his role. 

    The journal’s six editors resigned in the weeks following, according to an August 16 email, the text of which is also posted on the website for the Game Theory Society. Games and Economic Behavior is one of the group’s two official journals. As of August 19, 58 of the 62 advisory editors had resigned as well.

    “Requests for explanations, first by me, then in a collective letter by the other six Editors, were dismissed by Elsevier which simply reaffirmed its legal power to make such decisions,” Moulin wrote in the email, which was cosigned by the six resigning editors. In the email, he also said Elsevier cited “closer alignment with Elsevier strategic priorities and publishing policies” as a reason for the move. 

    The journal joins our Mass Resignations List at number 57, marking the seventh mass exodus we have covered this year. In April, we reported on the resignation of editors from another Elsevier journal after the publisher similarly informed the editor-in-chief the company would not be renewing his contract. 

    Because the editors are required to give three months notice, the resigning board members wrote they plan to continue their duties at the journal until November 14. Moulin chose to resign at the same time rather than complete his contract. 

    Roberto Serrano, another editor resigning in November, told us Elsevier “has behaved in this incident arrogantly and  inappropriately, by refusing to answer key questions about the future direction of the journal, which we had asked, and by suggesting names of new editors with no input from the academic community.”

    Serrano, a professor of economics at Brown University in Providence, Rhode Island, added: “As the other editors of the journal, we wanted to own what those new initiatives and directions for the journal were, and their answer was complete silence.” 

    Moulin told us the publisher “crossed a fundamental red line in the good faith agreement between the Editorial Board and the publisher” by not consulting with the editorial board regarding future editorial appointments.

    The publisher told the editorial board in an August 20 email: “We respect Hervé’s leadership experience, hence the outreach about potential successors was made with a view to including him in shaping GEB’s future — but he chose not to respond further and withdrew from those discussions.”

    “Leadership transitions are a normal part of a journal’s evolution, and Elsevier’s priority is ensuring GEB continues to serve its editors, authors, and the research community well,” the publisher wrote. 

    An Elsevier spokesperson told us Moulin “reached the end of its three-year timespan, and the team at Elsevier deeply appreciates the Editor’s years of contribution to GEB.” 

    “No respectable academic journal can work under these rules, and so it is understandable that the resignations of our entire board of Advisory Editors —about 60 distinguished scholars— have followed the resignation of us, the seven editors,” Serrano said. “[W]hen a publisher goes as far as to suggest who the new editors should be, without any input from the academic community, I can’t work for a journal like that.”
