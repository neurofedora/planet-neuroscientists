    Japanese institute finds materials researcher with 41 retractions guilty of ‘extensive’ manipulation

    An investigation by a national research institute in Japan has concluded one of its former researchers fabricated and falsified figures in seven papers. Two publishers confirmed they are investigating the articles. 

    Joseph Raj Xavier, of Saveetha Institute of Medical and Technical Sciences in Chennai, India, has lost 10 other papers this year alone, according to the Retraction Watch Database. His total is currently 41 retractions, earning him a spot on the Retraction Watch Leaderboard. 

    The National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS) in Tsukuba, Japan, found Xavier guilty of fabrication and falsification in “scanning electron microscope images, atomic force microscope images, and scanning electrochemical microscope images, among others,” according to a translation of an investigation report released September 29. 

    The investigation found it was “highly likely that figures were reused; there were signs of extensive manipulation, such as scaling the figures to full size, stretching or rotating them, and traces of cutting and pasting parts of images were found in multiple figures,” according to the report. Xavier committed self-plagiarism in six of the papers published from 2014 to 2017, the investigation found. The articles cover the detection and prevention of metal corrosion. 

    “Based on these findings, it was concluded that specific acts of misconduct were committed intentionally,” the report states. 

    The institute looked into 23 papers in total, eight of which listed his affiliation as NIMS. One of the eight did not contain evidence of misconduct, NIMS found. For the other 15 papers listing Xavier at other institutions, published from 2016 to 2019, the committee said there was an “extremely high likelihood” Xavier altered figures used in papers he published while with NIMS and “improperly used them as figures” in papers “under the affiliation of another institution.” 

    The case was brought to our attention by Haklak Rockbill, professor emeritus at Ochanomizu University who tracks and writes about research ethics and misconduct. 

    Xavier’s work has been cited over 4,000 times, roughly one-third of which are self citations, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science. 

    Xavier lists Saveetha Institute of Medical and Technical Sciences  as his affiliation on several recent papers. Repeated emails to his Saveetha and multiple personal email addresses went unanswered. We have flagged Saveetha in the past for its excessive self-citation rates and, last year, we reported its retraction rate has risen drastically in recent years. 

    NIMS alerted Xavier’s current institution of their findings, the representative told us, but did not confirm whether Saveetha was that institution. 

    “After being informed that an interview would be conducted as part of the investigation, Dr. Xavier stopped responding to communications,” the NIMS representative said.

    As part of the investigation, the NIMS committee looked into the work of Xavier’s frequent coauthor, his supervisor Toshiya Nishimura. They concluded Nishimura had not intentionally committed misconduct, but noted for one of the papers where Nishimura was the corresponding author, “there was a lack of communication with Mr. Xavier and insufficient review of the manuscript.” For six other papers the committee examined, “it was confirmed that Mr. Xavier had added Mr. Nishimura as a co-author without permission after leaving the organization and submitted the papers.”

    The institute became aware of PubPeer comments flagging Xavier’s work on January 22 when it received a call on their research misconduct reporting hotline, the report states. Nishimura had retired from NIMS before then, the Legal Affairs and Compliance Office at NIMS told Retraction Watch. 

    Of the seven papers the committee said contained misconduct, four appear in Springer Nature journals and two in Elsevier journals. The investigation committee recommended Xavier and Nishimura withdraw the papers. “However, we have not yet received an indication from Mr. Xavier regarding whether or not he will comply with the recommendation within the period stipulated in the regulations,” the report reads. 

    The Legal Affairs and Compliance Office at NIMS told us they “plan to inform the relevant journals of the investigation findings in due course.” 

    Tim Kersjes, head of Research Integrity at Springer Nature, did not directly respond to our question of whether Xavier had informed the publisher of the investigation. However, he said the publisher is “aware of concerns with a number of papers authored by this individual, and our Research Integrity Team are conducting thorough, ongoing investigations into relevant articles.” Authors found to have violated the publisher’s Act with Integrity policy “may face restrictions on their engagement with our journals,” Kersjes said. 

    An Elsevier spokesperson said the publisher is “aware of the issues raised regarding this author” and that the case is under investigation. 

    The seventh paper in the investigation was published in 2017 by the International Journal of Petrochemical Science & Engineering. The journal’s publisher, MedCrave — which once appeared on the now-defunct Beall’s List and was the target of a Seinfeld-themed sting in 2017 — did not respond to our request for comment.

    Modernizing arXiv technology: Updates on the arXiv-CE project

    This past August, arXiv turned 35 years old. That’s 35 years of open science – and 35 years of legacy tech.

    arXiv is one of the largest and most widely used open repositories in the world. It’s also one of the oldest – and that comes with its share of technical debt. As arXiv has expanded and submissions have increased, aging infrastructure and growing requirements have made it clear that arXiv’s tech stack needed to be modernized so that we can scale sustainably and continue to serve the research community.

    In 2022, arXiv proposed a plan to modernize our technology and migrate arxiv.org to the cloud – the arXiv-CE (Cloud Edition) project. Since 2023, the arXiv team has been diligently working to complete this plan, which includes updating our code base and migrating off of Cornell IT servers.

    Thanks to generous support from major funders, arXiv has been able to hire new members to our engineering team who dedicate their talents specifically to the arXiv-CE project. arXiv has also recently welcomed our inaugural Engineering Director, Jeff Sank, who hit the ground running in April of this year. With over 20 years of experience, Jeff’s guidance and leadership as we near the completion of this critical project have been invaluable to arXiv.

    The tech goals for the arXiv-CE project include improving service for all users, securing arXiv and maintaining stability for years to come, and providing updated features for researchers using arxiv.org. Meeting these goals – while ensuring minimal disruptions to service – is a huge task. arXiv’s engineering team has already made marked progress on these goals that support arXiv submitters and readers.

    Other arXiv-CE completed updates include our update to Submission 1.5, reducing delays in new submission announcements, and steady improvement in arXiv’s Experimental HTML, which makes research more accessible to researchers with disabilities. In August 2026, arXiv made its entire collection available in HTML: 4.6 million article versions, with 99% of all articles submitted as LaTeX now readable in HTML. arXiv browse has already been completely migrated to the cloud as of August 2023. As we continue to make progress on arXiv-CE, arxiv.org has also seen fewer outages, despite more traffic than ever. From October 2025 to October 2026, arXiv had no outages at all, with 99.96% availability.

    As of October 2026, the arXiv-CE project is very close to the finish line. There are still many critical updates to complete, including launching Submission 2.0 and migrating arXiv’s database. We anticipate some of these updates will require scheduled maintenance on arxiv.org and potentially disrupt submission service and announcements. arXiv is a critical service to researchers who visit arxiv.org every day, and our goal is to keep disruptions as minimal as possible. All outages will be announced in advance so that arXiv users can plan their submissions and other work on arxiv.org as needed.

    Thank you to our funders and the scientific community at large who support arXiv and make these massive undertakings and important projects possible. And thank you to Cornell University for 25 years of collaboration, support, and hosting arxiv.org on Cornell IT servers. The completion of arXiv-CE will make arXiv’s technology more secure, flexible, and truly open, independent, and modern.

    ‘Springer’ agency website offering authorship for sale goes down following Retraction Watch story

    The website for Springer Journals Online, an agency offering authorship slots in articles in exchange for payment, has gone offline, four days after Retraction Watch published its investigation into its activities.

    As we reported on Monday, the agency was soliciting researchers offering services that included securing authorship in research articles intended for journals published by Elsevier, Springer Nature, Taylor & Francis, Wiley and SAGE, among others. 

    Springer Journals Online did not immediately respond to a request for comment by email asking why the website was unavailable, if it had halted operations, or if it wished to address our previous story. No one answered calls made via WhatsApp.

    The agency uses an obviously similar name to Springer Nature, one of the world’s largest academic publishers. A spokesperson for Springer Nature told Retraction Watch that the publisher had no connection to the agency and said they were “working with the relevant authorities to address this issue.”

    There was an update to the “springerjournalsonline.net” domain dated October 16, but it’s not clear from the domain record what that update was or if it’s responsible for the website outage. Retraction Watch could not determine if the agency has completely ceased operating or if it is continuing operations through other channels.

    In August, Richard Fateman, a retired computer scientist from the University of California, Berkeley, received an email offering authorship in an article in exchange for a share of the publication costs. The offer prompted an investigation into the agency’s operations and their use of established publishers’ and researchers’ names to solicit researchers.

    Journal retracts, republishes paper on Long COVID treatment trial

    Annals of Internal Medicine has retracted a paper on treatments for Long COVID because the authors did not report dosing information for metformin, one of the drugs tested in the study. The editor-in-chief of the journal called the exclusion “sloppy” and “inaccurate.” 

    The same day the journal retracted the paper, it published a new version addressing the exclusion. But one researcher told Retraction Watch the replacement still leaves unanswered questions. 

    Published in March, the clinical trial found the antidepressant fluvoxamine – marketed as Luvox – reduced fatigue and metformin showed no significant benefit for patients with long-term fatigue and other chronic symptoms following COVID illness. Other studies have found fluvoxamine helped reduce depression in patients with Long COVID, and researchers have found starting metformin within three days of being diagnosed with COVID-19 can help prevent long-term effects of the disease. 

    During the trial, doctors reduced the twice-a-day metformin dose from 750 mg to 500 mg because patients were experiencing side effects including gastrointestinal problems, and 9% of patients in that group had dropped out. In their analysis, the authors pooled the two groups because they considered the doses to be “equivalent,” senior author Edward Mills told Retraction Watch. 

    “Someone didn’t like that and wrote to the editor demanding we keep them separate, so we did the new analysis that has them as two different groups,” Mills said. “It makes no difference to the results or interpretation.”

    Carolyn Bramante, a metabolic health and COVID-19 researcher at the University of Minnesota, told us both the retracted and replacement papers had different inclusion and exclusion criteria for the fluvoxamine, placebo and metformin arms. “Having the same inclusion/exclusion criteria is a fundamental, basic requirement of randomization,” she said.

    Bramante has studied the use of metformin in people with acute COVID-19 and has advocated for patients with the condition to take the drug to protect against Long COVID.

    Bramante said she also was concerned with the high number of adverse events reported in the paper. She pointed to another article published in March in Annals of Internal Medicine led by So Yun Lim, a researcher at the University of Ulsan College of Medicine in Seoul, which found few adverse events when testing the efficacy of 14 days of metformin in Long COVID patients. Conversely, nearly half of patients in Mills’ study who took metformin reported adverse events.

    Lim’s study had “values you would expect” for adverse events, she said. “You typically don’t expect trials to have such different findings.”

    Christine Laine, the journal’s editor-in-chief, told us Annals of Internal Medicine decided to retract the paper “because the authors did not report the changes in metformin dosing that occurred during the conduct of the trial.” 

    In the new version of the paper, the authors included the reduction in the dose of metformin and an explanation of why that arm of the study was stopped for futility, both of which were recommended by the trial’s data and safety monitoring committee. 

    The retracted article concluded metformin showed no benefit to patients with Long COVID, but the new version states the metformin findings were inconclusive. 

    Several of the same authors, including Mills, a part-time professor of health research methods at McMaster University in Hamilton, Ontario, lost a similar paper on acute COVID-19 from The Lancet for concerns with the metformin arm of an earlier version of the REVIVE-TOGETHER trial. Both the retracted paper and replacement in Annals cite the retracted Lancet paper. 

    “This isn’t really a retraction but rather a republication as I think formatting the manuscript would have been difficult or impossible,” Mills told us in an email. 

    Laine told us, “The editors do not know whether the reason for the inaccurate reporting was intentional,” she said. “We do know that it was sloppy, inaccurate, and the scientific record needed to be corrected.”

    When we asked why Annals chose to republish the paper, Laine said: “Not making these results available in a republished article would have been disrespectful to the 399 patients who altruistically participated in the study.” She also said the article underwent further peer review prior to publication in October. 

    Corresponding author Jamie Forrest, a health informatics researcher at Northeastern University’s Vancouver campus who was also on the Lancet article, told us Mills’ comments “represent those of the authorship.”

    Before its retraction, the paper was cited four times, according to Clarivate’s Web of Science. The paper joins our list of almost 800 retracted COVID-19 articles, which includes a 2020 retraction from the journal for a paper claiming masks were ineffective in preventing the spread of COVID-19.
