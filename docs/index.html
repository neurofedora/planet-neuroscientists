    Above and beyond: An editor wanted authors to know they’d been falsely cited

    Like many college professors today, Sarah S. Richardson has seen AI-hallucinated citations in work submitted by her students. But she was surprised when an editor recently contacted her about a hallucinated citation involving her own work. 

    A March 31 email from the editor in chief of Disability Studies Quarterly explained Richardson was one of six scholars falsely cited in a journal article. The editor apologized for the error and alerted Richardson of the paper’s retraction. 

    “The editor took the extra step of informing all scholars who could have been harmed by the hallucinated citation[s] and did so in an exceptionally thoughtful and caring manner,” said Richardson, the Aramont Professor of the History of Science and Professor of Studies of Women, Gender, and Sexuality at Harvard University. “I suspect that most hallucinated citations are never discovered, let alone redressed by a retraction and outreach to those affected.”

    Richardson shared the editor’s email with Retraction Watch because she thought his response was “highly meritorious,” and may help “inform best practice,” she told us. 

    The retracted paper, by Shawna Sheperd-Murtagh, was published in March and explored how disabled content creators enact “joy, desire, and collective care as resistant practices in the face of ableist and racialized digital infrastructures.” Disability Studies Quarterly is the journal of the Society for Disability Studies.

    Two weeks after the article was published, a reader emailed the journal to report being falsely cited in the work, according to a June editorial by the journal’s editor-in-chief Jeffrey A. Brune and his assistant editors. 

    The journal audited all 117 footnotes in the article and found a total of six citations that appeared to be AI hallucinations. 

    Brune told us most of the hallucinations included the names of known scholars in the field who were attributed to publications they didn’t write. The journal discovered all the false citations were added after the first peer review, the editorial notes.  

    “The main step toward accountability was to retract the article, which we did right away, but I also felt it was important to be accountable and transparent with the scholars who were falsely cited,” he told us. “For one, we owed them an apology. In addition, I thought it was important that they should be aware of this because they might come across the false citation at some point.”

    Brune’s email to the scholars described what happened and the steps the journal was taking to address it. As a scholar himself, Brune said he “certainly would want to know if a publication had been falsely attributed to me.”

    Sheperd-Murtagh told us she understood the journal’s decision to retract the article and said she takes responsibility for the errors. 

    Her paper resulted from several years of research and writing, followed by an “extensive revision process,” said Sheperd-Murtagh, who was a lecturer at North Carolina State University when she submitted the paper in January of this year and is now director of development for Meals on Wheels Durham. She did not use AI to generate the content, she told us, but “experimented with generative AI” late in the editing process while searching for additional scholarship to “deepen particular areas of the article.” 

    The AI suggested many scholars who she recognized, and because their names and the subject matter “made sense together,” she assumed the information was legitimate, she said. 

    “The citation issues were serious, and it was my responsibility to ensure that every source I cited was accurate and verifiable,” she told us. “I should have caught these errors, and I regret that I did not.” 

    Since discovering the hallucinations, Brune said the journal has implemented a new step to prevent such problems. The journal now requires doi hyperlinks for all citations, when available, so reviewers can easily confirm citations during copyediting. The journal also plans to revise its AI policies this summer and add an AI statement to its instructions for peer reviewers, according to Brune’s editorial.  

    The Disability Studies Quarterly paper is the latest with hallucinated references we’ve reported on recently. Last month, we wrote about the editors of a higher education journal who coauthored an editorial with fake references. In March we reported on a librarian who discovered 12 of 14 references in a Springer Nature paper didn’t exist. 

    Richardson said Brune’s email was the first time she’s ever been contacted by a journal regarding a false citation linked to her name and she hopes more journals adopt the practice. 

    “In my view, hallucinated citations are a prime example of the threat of LLM usage to our expert knowledge systems,” she told us. “The articles can be retracted but they are already online and stay online, and the damage of muddle and disinformation is done if others duplicate those citations or LLMs imbibe them as credible.”

    Tennessee researcher heads to court amid claims of theft, forgery and revenge

    In May 2025, Tennessee authorities were preparing to arrest a university math professor for allegedly soliciting university donations from his students and pocketing the money. 

    Investigative records paint the picture of a professor who took financial and personal advantage of his students for years – from manipulating them into paying him rent to making them clean up after his dog – and grew vengeful when they spoke out about his alleged crimes.

    As state investigators closed in, the professor, Don Hong, tried to get revenge against a student who reported him by accusing her of research misconduct and telling a journal to retract a study she recently authored, according to an investigative report from the Tennessee Comptroller of the Treasury. When a grand jury indicted Hong on May 5, 2025, for theft and forgery, it tacked on a retaliation charge for his actions against the student.  

    But later that month, the International Journal of Computer Mathematics retracted a paper about mortgage rates by Ph.D. student Danlei Zhu, an advisee of Hong’s, and four others in the department of mathematical science at Middle Tennessee State University (MTSU) in Murfreesboro. According to the retraction notice, “significant concerns” were raised about the study’s methodology following publication, and an independent external review determined the methodology and statistical analysis “were incorrect” and the conclusions “unreliable.”

    Whether the IJCM paper is the same article referenced in the state’s investigative report is unclear. Taylor & Francis, which publishes the title, would not tell us what prompted the journal’s investigation. Jennifer McMillan, vice president for external communications for Taylor & Francis, told us the company did not have “anything further to add” to the retraction notice. 

    However, the details may soon be revealed in an upcoming court trial. Hong has a status hearing in his theft case scheduled for Sept. 25. He has not yet entered a plea, according to court officials. A jury trial is set for March 1, 2027. 

    Hong was among the faculty who taught and supervised research in the university’s Computational & Data Science Ph.D. program, home to all authors on the retracted International Journal of Computer Mathematics at the time of publication. 

    Hong did not return messages seeking comment. 

    Zhu and her coauthors did not return multiple messages seeking comment. Zhu was vice president of graduate affairs for the university’s Actuarial Math Student Association between 2022 and 2023. Hong, coordinator of MTSU’s Actuarial Science Program, previously served as faculty advisor for the student group. 

    Hong has worked at MTSU since 2005 and remains an active professor of mathematical sciences at the institution, according to a university spokesperson. As part of his role, Hong travels to China to recruit students and create partnerships with other universities, according to the Comptroller’s report. 

    State authorities started investigating Hong after an MTSU internal audit revealed questionable financial transactions between the professor and students. Investigators learned Hong encouraged students to donate to the Actuarial Science Program, but the donations were transferred to Hong’s personal bank account rather than to the university. Between January 2020 and May 2024, Hong stole at least $8,225.48 in university donations, the state alleges. 

    Hong gave conflicting and inconsistent explanations about why he held onto the donations, including that he planned to “gradually” pay the money back, that he planned to invest the funds, and that he planned to give the money to the university at the end of the semester, according to investigators. 

    During their investigation, authorities interviewed various students from China who reported that before coming to the United States, Hong told them of available housing near the university.

    However, Hong concealed that he or his spouse owned the housing, and he used an alias to create fictitious lease agreements with students, according to the Comptroller’s report. The professor acted as a middleman between the property arrangements, but the students were unaware to whom they were paying rent. Some students also told authorities Hong required them to take care of his dog during spring break without payment. Hong also took cash from students for “airport transportation,” which he claimed as a reimbursable expense from the university, according to investigators.  

    A Ph.D. student and research graduate assistant, identified as “Student 4” in the state’s report, was among the students who lived in a rental property operated by Hong. The student went to Hong’s office “multiple times” to report “an act of violence” that occurred at one of Hong’s properties between the student and her roommate and to ask for help, according to the Comptroller’s report. But “Hong advised her not to contact the police, saying it would bring shame because they are Chinese,” according to the report. Hong told the student if she moved out, “she would be responsible for the rent for the whole year.” 

    The student moved out of the property around February 2022, but continued paying Hong until her lease agreement ended, according to the state’s report. She told authorities that in China, “students are taught to listen to their professor(s), and she believed Hong.”

    Shortly after “Student 4” and another student reported Hong’s actions to investigators, a person who called himself “Peter Panelist” began levying research misconduct accusations against her, according to the state. Hong emailed a journal under the alias to report a paper the student wrote contained plagiarism. The email, obtained by investigators, stated:

    “Given the lack of methodological rigor and the apparent duplication of previous work, the paper…should be retracted. It is concerning that such a manuscript was accepted, especially in a reputable journal..”

    Authorities obtained subscriber information for the email account used to send the message and traced it back to Hong. The registration IP address for “Peter Panelist” was an IP address connected to Hong’s university Microsoft account, according to state investigators. 

    “The investigation revealed that the accusations against Student 4 appear to be retaliatory, based on the fact that they were submitted after she reported Hong’s potential misconduct to state investigators,” the report states. “The investigation further showed that the email account for “Peter Panelist” was created approximately four days before an accusation was submitted against Student 4, indicating this account was designed merely to transmit anonymous allegations against her.” 

    The report does not identify which journal investigators contacted or the authors involved in the paper. 

    MTSU would not tell us if the research misconduct allegations against the student and her coauthors  were found to be credible or if it opened an investigation into the concerns. In a statement, the university said the information we requested is “confidential under the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act” and under the university’s students records policy. 

    “Our research misconduct policy also requires that such matters be treated confidentially by the university,” the statement said.

    Wiley investigating book chapter on biodiversity for fake references

    While reading a chapter from a 2024 book on restoring biodiversity in post-industrial land earlier this month, an ecologist came across a reference to a study on rewilding she wanted to look up. 

    But the ecologist, who has asked to remain anonymous for fear of retaliation from the chapter authors, couldn’t find a record of the 2020 paper, purportedly published in Ambio by “Knijn et al.” She decided to check the other references. She told Retraction Watch she found at least eight that didn’t exist. 

    The ecologist flagged the issue to the book’s publisher, Wiley, on September 7 but told us she never heard back, aside from an automated response.

    A Wiley spokesperson told us the publisher “takes integrity seriously and investigates all concerns when they are flagged to us.” They said Wiley would “take appropriate measures once the investigation has concluded.”

    The publisher owned Edifix, a reference-checking service, from 2019 until it was acquired by another company earlier this year. The spokesperson did not answer our question asking whether books, including this one, were run through the program, but said Wiley “has published AI use guidelines for book authors and safeguards the integrity and quality of the books we publish through established checks at three stages: book proposal, contracting, and manuscript review and development. Edifix is not one of the tools that we currently use.”

    The Wiley representative did not respond when we asked if the publisher was aware of issues with the book chapter before we contacted them last week. 

    Bhanu Pandey, one of the four authors of the chapter in the book, Restoring Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services on Post-Industrial Land, told us he wasn’t aware of the fake citations before we reached out. All four authors were researchers at CSIR-Central Institute of Mining and Fuel Research in Jharkhand, India, at the time of the study. 

    Bhanu Pandey told us first author, Sameer Shekhar, “took the lead in compiling the chapter materials and coordinating the submission.” He said he is looking into the book chapter and planning to submit a request for a correction to Wiley: “Where a citation is inaccurate or the associated statement is unsupported, I will propose the appropriate correction, revision, or removal and submit it to the chapter editor and publisher through their formal editorial process.”

    None of the other authors responded to our request for comment, including Shekhar, who we reached out to multiple times on both his professional and personal email addresses.

    Vimal Chandra Pandey (no relation to Bhanu), a researcher at CSIR’s National Botanical Research Institute, edited the book. He told us he would “check in this regard” but did not answer questions asking how the fake references may have been overlooked. Wiley’s page on potential conflicts for editors says they “must refrain from handling manuscripts if they have a COI, like a professional or personal relationship with the authors.”

    Other publishers have dealt with similar cases: After retracting a book on machine learning containing non-existent references following our coverage of the case, Springer Nature said in June they would begin issuing expressions of concern for books. In October, research integrity specialist Michał Wójcik wrote a Python script to identify several books with fake citations, which can be a sign of misuse of large language models.
