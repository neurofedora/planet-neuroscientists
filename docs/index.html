    Federal lawsuit accuses four major publishers of extorting article processing charges

    A lawsuit filed under the U.S. False Claims Act accuses four major publishers of perpetrating a decade-long scheme to defraud the government by charging exorbitant article processing charges.  

    The case, which became unsealed July 30, claims Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley and Informa forced U.S. research institutions to submit false claims to the government for reimbursement through article fees up to 10 times the cost of processing the articles. 

    Juan Pablo Alperin, a professor at Simon Fraser University and codirector of the Scholarly Communications Lab based in Vancouver, is represented by two attorneys who have had blockbuster success in FCA cases over the past decade. He and his attorneys originally filed the case under seal in March 2024. The government declined to intervene in the case on July 23 of this year, and a judge ordered the complaint unsealed, which now allows Alperin to pursue the case independently.

    Sage was also originally listed as a defendant in the case, according to the court docket, and University of Ottawa researcher Stefanie Haustein was a plaintiff. Both were dropped from the suit last week. 

    Publishers’ fees vary, but APCs for open access can range from hundreds of dollars for small journals up to thousands, with some topping $10,000 per paper. The real costs  of processing such articles are between $200 and $1,000, Alperin claims in the complaint. The U.S. government requires papers based on federally funded research be freely available. The lawsuit alleges the publishers address this mandate by “extorting” APCs and copyright transfers from “unknowing” PIs who have an “understandable desire to publish in prestigious venues to advance in their careers.”

    Alperin alleges he learned insider details about APC pricing as a member of the Open Access Scholarly Publishing Association, including “outright unlawful conduct” and “conspiratorial discussions” among the publishers, the complaint states. This included strategies by the companies to shield the viability of free open access options to ensure U.S. grantee institutions continued paying unnecessary APCs, according to his complaint. 

    Alperin told Retraction Watch he brought the FCA case to spotlight how pricing practices by some publishers are not in the public interest and to hold them accountable for those practices. He has conducted extensive research on APCs, which has found publishers use APC pricing strategically, rather than based on reasonable production expenses. 

    “More broadly, the goal is to help to create a more transparent and functioning market, because the market of open access publishing is one that is in many ways very dysfunctional,” he told us. “In part, the goal is helping to create a market that is transparent, equitable, and that ensures as much of the money that’s been set aside for research can go towards” that intent.  

    Debate about APCs is not new, said Eugenie Reich, a Boston-based attorney who is representing Alperin. What is new is identifying a legal claim where the public discussion intersects with the law, she said. 

    “That discussion has definitely existed, but it’s never, as far as I’m aware, been publicly alleged that this is fraud on the government,” she told us. “I think that’s where publishers have gotten themselves into trouble per the complaint, by using public access policies as a way of increasing charges, knowing that the charges would hit government grants.” 

    The complaint alleges the publishers have “fixed the market” to make it difficult to publish without paying “prestige prices, luxury prices,” both through market concentration and through copyright terms, Reich added. 

    “That’s why I think it is important to distinguish between a business that just wants to make money from a luxury good and a business that is knowingly passing such charges through to the government,” she said in an interview.  

    Reich represented sleuth Sholto David in an FCA action against Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, which settled the case in December 2025 for $15 million. The institute admitted researchers used “misrepresented and/or duplicated” images and data in support of grant applications to the National Institutes of Health. As the relator in the case, David received a portion of the settlement, with the bulk of the amount going back to the federal government.

    Reich was also part of the team representing the whistleblower in an FCA case against Biogen, which agreed to pay $900 million to resolve claims the company unlawfully paid kickbacks to physicians. The government also declined to intervene in that case. 

    John R. Thomas of Hafemann, Magee & Thomas, LLC, in Roanoke, Virginia, the other lawyer representing Alperin, was one of the attorneys in an FCA case against Duke University that resulted in a $112.5 million settlement, nearly $34 million of which went to his brother Joseph, the whistleblower in the case. He has written for Retraction Watch about the act as well as about what whistleblowers should consider in the context of an FCA claim. 

    A Springer Nature spokesperson said the publisher could not immediately comment on the matter. Wiley and Elsevier declined to comment. Informa did not respond. 

    FCA attorneys had mixed views of the complaint. Pamela Coyle Brecht, a whistleblower attorney in Philadelphia, called the filing a “niche case” with a “unique theory.” 

    However, Renée Brooker, a Washington, D.C.-based attorney with Tycko & Zavareei LLP, said the fact the defendants are journal publishers may be “eye-grabbing,” but the legal theories underpinning the case are tried and true in the FCA realm.. A wide swath of qui tam cases involve defendants that did not directly submit the alleged false claims, Brooker said, particularly complaints against the pharmaceutical industry. Such companies are frequently accused of FCA violations, while a pharmacy or doctor’s office unknowingly filed the false claims.

    The case is a prime example of parties that don’t directly submit false claims to the government still facing liability, said Eva Gunasekera, a whistleblower attorney at Tycko & Zavareei and former senior counsel for healthcare fraud at the DOJ. 

    “It’s a great reminder of how broad in scope the False Claims Act is,” she said.

    By law, FCA cases are filed under seal to protect whistleblowers and to give the government time to investigate the claims and decide if it wishes to intervene. That the government did not intervene in this case is not surprising, Brooker added. 

    “It says nothing at all about the merits of the case,” she said. “Over the last 15 years, the relator’s bar [has been] more and more successful with helping the DOJ to litigate declined qui tam cases because DOJ cannot do it all.”

    The U.S. Attorney for the District of Massachusetts declined to comment. The DOJ did not respond to a message for comment. 

    In February, a federal judge dismissed a lawsuit that alleged six major publishers violated antitrust law by colluding to “fix the price of peer review services at $0.”  Filed by UCLA neuroscientist Lucina Uddin, the case also included Elsevier, Springer Nature and Wiley as defendants, as well as Sage, Taylor & Francis and Wolters Kluwer.

    Retraction Watch story exposing research factory fuels investigations, preprint withdrawals, termination

    A preprint server has withdrawn three papers by an author at the center of a Retraction Watch story revealing a prolific research factory operated through the WhatsApp messaging system.  

    Last week, we reported on Raghabendra Kumar Mahato, a second-year medical student in Nepal, who runs an international research network that churns out conference abstracts and journal papers at a rapid-fire pace. Mahato’s network includes nearly 1,000 members connected through WhatsApp and produces suspected fast-churn science, while recruiting authorship for the works. Papers by the network have been accepted by major journals and presented at conferences by leading medical societies. 

    On July 24, Research Square, a preprint server owned by Springer Nature, withdrew three manuscripts by Mahato due to concerns “regarding integrity arising from the submitting author’s involvement in a paper mill network,” according to notices placed on the work. The notices include a link to Retraction Watch’s investigation. 

    Following our coverage, Mahato has also been kicked out of the Global Association of Indian Medical Students, where he served on the editorial board, according to correspondence we’ve seen.  

    Mahato did not return messages seeking comment. 

    A Research Square spokesperson told us removal of the preprints followed concerns raised by “an independent assessment.”

    “Consistent with our public policies, preprints may be withdrawn where concerns have been identified that could undermine trust in the content reported,” the spokesperson told us. “Authors are contacted directly to keep them informed of any decision taken.” 

    Anit Sinha, a disillusioned member of Mahato’s network who previously shared details with us about the operation, said he was “shocked” but “deeply hopeful” to see Research Square’s prompt removal of the preprints. Sinha recently graduated from Gandaki Medical College in Nepal, the same school Mahato now attends. He joined Mahato’s network to boost his CV, but became suspicious of the network’s activities after he started working with the group, he previously told us. 

    “I had no idea whether any concrete action would be taken against these ethical violations, so seeing Research Square remove the preprints so promptly was incredibly validating,” Sinha said in an email. He credited our coverage with prompting the move.

    Following our story, Sinha wrote to Research Square with concerns about the preprints and shared our coverage. In a July 25 email to Sinha, “Mark B.” with Springer Nature publishing support, thanked Sinha for “bringing” the matter to “our attention,” and wrote the preprints were withdrawn “after looking into the information you provided,” according to correspondence we’ve seen.

    The three papers examined various mortality trends based on the CDC WONDER database, a publicly accessible repository that collects public health data. There has been a steep rise in published studies using publicly available datasets, many of which show hallmarks of being produced by paper mills. One recent preprint found exponential growth in publications based CDC WONDER, with an increasing number of authors from Pakistan.

    Of 10 institutions listed across the three withdrawn preprints, eight were in Pakistan. 

    Palwasha Asghar, a coauthor on two of the papers, disagreed with the removal and told us her studies were “conceived, conducted, and submitted honestly, independently, and in good faith in accordance with established academic practice.” Asghar, a student at Khyber Medical College in Peshawar, Pakistan, cowrote another paper based on data from CDC WONDER with Mahato in BMC Neurology. Her ORCID profile shows 16 preprints and journal articles that were published between 2025 and 2026.   

    “The withdrawal of our Research Square preprints was both shocking and deeply distressing,” Asghar told us. “As a final-year medical student, I was particularly concerned because the preprints were withdrawn without prior notice, without being allowed to respond, and without any evidence supporting the allegations being disclosed to me.”

    Asghar said neither she nor her coauthors were aware of any “allegations or publicly reported concerns” regarding Mahato before their works were completed. The collaboration with Mahato did not arise through “any WhatsApp group,” but rather the authors were introduced to Mahato “through another researcher,” who recommended him as a potential collaborator, she told us. 

    Mahato’s contributions were limited to reviewing and revising manuscripts and managing the submission process, Asghar told us. The other authors meanwhile, carried out the data analyses and manuscript preparation, she said.

    Asghar provided us with documentation which she said shows the development of one of the withdrawn preprints, along with a folder containing screenshots and supporting records that “demonstrate the chronological progression of the research,” she told us. The screenshots appear to show author exchanges on WhatsApp.

    Asghar said the withdrawal of the preprints, without prior notice, an opportunity to respond, or disclosure of the underlying evidence has caused “significant academic and professional harm” and she has since sought legal advice.  

    Sinha also wrote to the Global Association of Indian Medical Students (GAIMS), to express his concerns about Mahato and share our recent coverage. Mahato joined the GAIMS Axis editorial board in June 2026, according to correspondence we’ve seen. Following an “internal review of the concerns raised,” GAIMS has terminated Mahato’s editorship and association with the GAIMS Axis Editorial Board with “immediate effect,” according to a July 28 email from GAIMS national president Abhishek Kashyap to Sinha. 

    Kashyap did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

    Sinha noted Mahato’s network, formally called the Global Clinical Research Consortium, “conveniently” rebranded itself as  “Research Helpers’” immediately following the publication of our story. He said he hopes other journals, preprint servers and associations remove more papers tied to the group.

    “Beyond polluting the research sphere, operators like Mahato are directly stealing opportunities from honest, hardworking students,” he told us. “They are taking hospital placements and scholarships from people who actually do the grueling work.”

    Journal, university investigations launched into gene-editing death following Science-Retraction Watch reporting

    Nature is investigating concerns surrounding a paper at the heart of a news story published last week on the death of a 6-year-old girl following a gene therapy treatment in China.

    The paper, published in February by a team led by neuroscientist Zilong Qiu of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine in China, described preclinical work for a gene-editing treatment for Snijders Blok-Campeau syndrome, a neurodevelopmental disorder. 

    As we reported in collaboration with Science, the work was funded in part by the family of a 6-year-old girl with the condition. The family had approached Qiu based on work he had done with gene therapy for Rett syndrome, a disorder that also causes developmental delay. The parents ended up paying $860,000 to fund preclinical work for a gene-editing treatment for their daughter, who died of a severe immune reaction to the therapy seven days after receiving it. 

    The paper did not disclose the girl’s death or the family’s financial contribution, even though a preprint of the work did “thank the families with SNIBCPS patients for their participation and support in this study.”   

    In addition, several experts interviewed for the article questioned some of the study’s efficacy data. 

    Victoria Aranda, deputy editor of Nature, said no information about a planned trial in people was ever shared with the journal or mentioned in the submitted study. “The manuscript that was submitted to us … described preclinical feasibility work only — a mouse model and non-human primate experiments,” she told Retraction Watch by email. “We are now investigating concerns that have been raised around this manuscript and an Editors’ Note has been placed on the paper to reflect this.”

    The editor’s note, dated July 29, states:

    Readers are alerted that concerns have been raised regarding this article, including with the data presented. Further editorial action will be taken if appropriate as soon as the investigation into the concerns is complete and all parties have been given an opportunity to respond in full.

    Qiu’s university has also launched an investigation, Science reported earlier this week. Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine said in a statement it would establish “a special task force to conduct a comprehensive investigation into the incident.”

    Neither the university nor Xinhua Hospital, where the young girl was treated, responded to requests for comment prior to the publication of the story in Science.

    The story has attracted international coverage by news outlets including the New York Post, Bloomberg, Times Higher Education, Southern Weekly and the South China Morning Post.

    arXiv welcomes inaugural CEO and Board of Directors

    We have exciting news to share today: the appointment of Dr. Penelope Lewis as arXiv’s inaugural Chief Executive Officer and the establishment of our Board of Directors.

    If you’ve been following arXiv’s transition to an independent nonprofit organization, these leadership appointments mark a significant step in our journey. Having a CEO and Board in place strengthens our governance, sustainability, and capacity to serve the global research community for generations to come.

    For more than three decades, arXiv has enabled rapid and open sharing of scientific research. It has operated largely on the support and dedication of its staff, moderators, volunteers, members, funders, and, for the past 25 years, the institutional backing of Cornell. As the scale, reach, and importance of the platform have grown, so too has the need for more formal leadership and governance structures that support long-term sustainability.

    The appointment of the first CEO and creation of an international governing board reflect our commitment to maintaining arXiv as a trusted, community-centered resource while preparing for future opportunities and evolution.

    We are delighted to welcome Dr. Penelope Lewis as arXiv’s inaugural CEO, effective August 17, 2026.

    Dr. Lewis is a scientist by training, holding a Ph.D. in Chemistry from Penn State University, a B.S. in Chemistry from Indiana University, and completing post-doctoral work at Columbia University. She brings to the role nearly 20 years of leadership in scholarly publishing and communication in mission-driven, nonprofit scientific organizations, most recently as Chief Publishing Officer at AIP Publishing, a subsidiary of the American Institute of Physics. At AIP Publishing, she led a comprehensive transformation of the organization’s publishing program, revitalizing the publication portfolio and restoring sustained growth while advancing open access, operational excellence, research integrity, and global engagement. Prior to that, Dr. Lewis held editorial and strategy leadership roles in the Publications Division at the American Chemical Society. Throughout her career, her leadership has been characterized by a strategic, collaborative approach that brings together diverse stakeholders around a shared vision.

    Dr. Lewis’ scientific background is foundational to her longstanding commitment to supporting the research communities that are critical to advancing the scientific research enterprise. For Dr. Lewis, arXiv’s appeal is that it was created by researchers, for researchers to solve a clear need: rapid and unfettered access to scientific research results on a global scale. While the research and publishing landscape has changed dramatically in the 35 years since arXiv was introduced, the need for rapid distribution, access, validation, and curation of research reports persists and is more important than ever. Dr. Lewis aims to evolve arXiv to meet the challenges it is facing – the rise of AI-generated preprints, modernization of the underlying technology, and a fast-growing and increasingly global user base, among others, – while preserving its mission and the trust of the community it supports.

    “I am excited and honored to join arXiv at such a significant time for the organization and for scientific research communication more broadly,” said Dr. Lewis. “arXiv has been a vital resource for researchers for over three decades, a testament to both its value and the dedication of the team and volunteers who have sustained it. While its move to independence will have its challenges, we have an opportunity to work with its community to build an even brighter future for arXiv.”

    “I’m thrilled about Penelope Lewis’ appointment as arXiv’s inaugural CEO. Her deep credibility in scholarly publishing, steady leadership through change, and collaborative approach are qualities that matter enormously in a community-driven organization like arXiv,” said Dr. Tracy Teal, CEO of openRxiv and member of the arXiv CEO search committee. “I’ve already seen how she builds trust across stakeholders and brings clarity to strategy and decision-making, and I’m excited for openRxiv to continue working with arXiv under her leadership to advance preprints and new ways of sharing science.”

    Just over 400 U.S. patents contain citations to retracted scientific papers

    Around 400 U.S. patents contain at least one citation to a retracted scientific paper, a new study has found. 

    The analysis, posted last month to the preprint server SSRN, analyzed granted patents in the United States in PatentsView, a database developed by the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) using artificial intelligence algorithms, and the Retraction Watch Database. 

    Of the more than 13 million patents in the USPTO database, 136,162 contained citations to at least one scientific paper. Of these, 401 patents cited retracted papers: 195 cited the retracted paper before it was filed, 92 cited papers retracted while being examined, and 114 cited a paper that was retracted after the patent was granted, the study revealed. 

    “Science has correction mechanisms, but correction signals often do not travel into patent records,” Hana Kim, of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology in Daejeon, South Korea, who conducted the study, told Retraction Watch in an interview. 

    Kim said citing a retracted paper shouldn’t automatically invalidate a patent but argued patent offices and information platforms must be able to identify retracted papers easily. “That is a fixable infrastructure problem,” she said, noting that patent systems should be linked to metadata from the Retraction Watch Database and Crossref. Kim said patents that cite retracted papers should be corrected. 

    Of the 401 patents that cite retracted papers, Kim conducted a further audit of 39 high-impact filings — defined by how many citations the patent itself has received, among other reasons. Nearly half (19) relied substantially on the retracted papers they cited. 

    Those 39 patents include one on DNA microtechnologies and nanotechnologies, one on a novel way of engineering transgenic animal models, and one for delivering therapeutic proteins into cells using gene editing techniques. We reached out to the lead scientists named on all three patents to find out why their patents cite retracted papers or if they plan to do anything about it, but didn’t hear back. 

    Janet Freilich, a law professor at Boston University who was not involved with the new study but has written about inaccurate information in patents, agreed with Kim. “There are a lot of inaccuracies that are permissible in a patent that would not be permissible in a paper,” she told us. “The standards are simply different.” 

    “It’s an embarrassment that the patent office doesn’t pay attention and then act upon information about scientific papers being tainted by retraction,” said Michael Meurer, a law professor also based at Boston University who has written a book on failures in the patent system. 

    Freilich said she thinks the conclusions of Kim’s study are likely to be “broadly correct.”  Patents should recognize when papers supporting them have been pulled, she said, because one purpose of issuing patents is to disseminate scientific information. 

    “The reason that many countries make patents public is because the patent system is supposed to be a repository of cutting-edge information that other scientists can then learn from and build on,” Freilich said. “That works a lot better if there’s a process for identifying and dealing with erroneous information, which otherwise will propagate and confuse readers.”

    Rules for amending patents when mistakes or errors are found differ in different jurisdictions, noted Freilich, who has previously called for new mechanisms to retract patents. The United States has no process to formally retract patents, she said, but if a patent contains inaccurate information, a judge may choose to invalidate it in court, which would mean it can’t be enforced against other patents. 

    “That would happen if the informational problems are severe enough that the patent is then deemed not to meet the required disclosure standards but those required legal standards don’t fully match up with the scientific notion of retraction,” Frelich said. “That doesn’t really do anything for the information because the information is still out there.” 

    Although in some cases a certificate of correction can be appended to the patent, Freilich said, it does not change the text of the patent itself and is usually not easy to access. “It’s not as good as a journal that will put the red line through information,” she said.

    Certificates of correction are also not common, Freilich said, and often don’t correct the record for initial patent filings, so earlier, incorrect versions could still be accessible. “It ought to be a more commonly used mechanism,” she said. “The patent office is not used to the idea of these kinds of corrections.”

    But Freilich said “no new matter” is a long-standing rule in U.S. patent law that forbids adding new material to a patent that wasn’t in the originally filed document. “The concern is that you would try to broaden your patent or strengthen your patent in ways that would not be fair to either the public or other inventors,” she said.

    Kaggle removes problematic stroke dataset for copyright infringement 

    The online data repository Kaggle has removed a problematic dataset for violating intellectual property rights months after it was flagged by sleuths for containing celebrity photos and lacking information on data provenance or confirmed medical diagnoses. 

    Researchers had used the images to train machine learning algorithms to detect stroke, but the dataset instead contained images of people with Bell’s palsy — as well as pictures of celebrities from movies and on the red carpet, as we reported in May. Adrian Barnett, a medical statistician at Queensland University of Technology in Australia, flagged the dataset after discovering two others he and his colleagues investigated in a February preprint that showed signs of being fabricated. 

    Using a reverse image search, Barnett matched four of the photos in the dataset, called “Facial droop and facial paralysis image,” to a paper published years earlier in the Journal of the Canadian Dental Association (JCDA). Only after prompting the journal to complain to Kaggle did the company take down the dataset. 

    The dataset page on Kaggle now shows a 404 error but no explanation for why it was removed. 

    “When an enforcement action occurs, the page is made unavailable on Kaggle and the uploading user receives a direct notification detailing the reasoning alongside instructions on how to appeal the decision,” a representative for the company told us by email. They did not address a question about how users of the database are informed of a removal. 

    The removal came months after Barnett tried unsuccessfully to convince Kaggle the widely used collection of stroke images was “scientific nonsense.”

    Kaggle has removed datasets before for breaching their terms of service, but Barnett didn’t have any luck with his earlier attempts to flag the collection.  “I think it’s funny,” he said, “because we approached Kaggle and said, ‘Hey, this dataset is rubbish, scientific nonsense,’ and that didn’t work.” But breaching copyright seems to be “the quickest way to get some of this rubbish taken down,” Barnett said. “I am going to be using this approach now that I’ve seen that it’s successful.”

    Kaggle removes datasets that violate community guidelines, including intellectual property rights, the representative said. “Our approach to platform integrity involves active moderation and reviewing daily content reports from the community to ensure adherence to our guidelines, including the protection of intellectual property rights,” they said.

    Several of the images taken from the JCDA paper appear to be of a minor.  The authors “consented this young girl, and didn’t anticipate that the image was then going to be just shared all over the internet,” Barnett said. People who agree to share their medical images place trust in the process, and do it to inform other clinicians, “not to help somebody who wants to boost their ratings on Kaggle,” he said. 

    Barnett alerted both the journal and the author of the paper about the use of the images, since they cannot be used without written permission from the Canadian Dental Association, according to the journal’s policy. Michelle Bergeron, a publications specialist at the journal, told Barnett in June JCDA was looking into their unauthorized use, according to emails seen by Retraction Watch. By July, the journal had contacted Kaggle directly about the copyright violation, and Kaggle had removed the dataset. 

    Since we last reported on the problematic datasets hosted on Kaggle, several papers using them have been retracted, but more have since been published. The Scientific Reports paper we wrote about in May was retracted on June 29. The authors had used Kaggle images to train a stroke prediction model, but many of them were of people with Bell’s palsy, including those pulled from the JCDA paper. 

    The retraction notice states the dataset underpinning the model “can no longer be verified through its original source” and appeared to contain identifiable faces without information about ethical oversight, nor whether their diagnosis was confirmed. The content of the article is no longer accessible “to protect the privacy of the individuals,” the editors wrote. But more researchers continue to use the dataset, including in at least two papers published this year. 

    Since our reporting, two other papers in Scientific Reports  that had been under investigation for using the flagged datasets have also been retracted. The notices state the works relied on publicly available datasets whose “provenance and validity cannot be confirmed.” One of these, the “Stroke Prediction Dataset,” which includes quantitative health parameters such as BMI and smoking status, remains available online and continues to be used to train clinical prediction models. 

    In early July, authors of another preprint justified using it to train an AI for stroke prediction because it is “widely adopted” in machine learning research, saying it had been used in more than 100 research articles — a figure they cited from Barnett’s work calling out the dataset for its unreliability. 

    Barnett and his team are now planning a broader audit of 100 datasets from Kaggle to assess their quality and whether they are suitable for medical research. The goal is to determine: “is this an enormous problem, or were we just unlucky and came across a few odd ones?” he said. 

    “It never occurred to me that I’d have to ask the question: Is this real?” Barnett said, “But now, I think that’s the question I’m going to be asking for the rest of my career.”

    Remembering Ralph Wijers

    arXiv is sad to announce the recent passing of our colleague and friend, Professor Ralph Wijers. A talented researcher, lecturer, and mentor, Ralph joined arXiv as a moderator and advisor in 2020 and was chair of both arXiv’s Physics Section Editorial Committee (SEC) and its Editorial Advisory Council. 

    Ralph generously lent his talents to arXiv despite his busy schedule and long list of accomplishments. Interested in astronomy from a young age, Ralph studied and taught in universities on both sides of the Atlantic, and was most recently a Professor of Astronomy at the Anton Pannekoek Institute of Astronomy (API) at the University of Amsterdam. From 2011 to 2021, Ralph served as API’s director, during which time API’s staff doubled in size. Ralph contributed to many scientific publications on all types of gamma-ray bursts (GRBs). In total, he published more than 550 scientific articles, supervising more than 30 PhD students and 20 postdocs.

    Ralph joined arXiv in 2020, an important time of growth and transition. Joining first as a moderator of arXiv’s astro-ph.HE category, Ralph soon took over as arXiv’s Physics SEC chair in 2021, where he generously gave his time and expertise to helping shape arXiv’s new governance. In 2023, Ralph became arXiv’s inaugural Editorial Advisory Council chair, essentially functioning as arXiv’s “Editor in Chief,” helping guide arXiv moderation and policies during a rapidly changing time for research communications.

    A true champion of open science and arXiv, Ralph approached everything he did for arXiv with thoughtfulness and zeal, whether it was working with arXiv staff and volunteer moderators on policy issues or accepting the prestigious Jocelyn Bell Burnell Inspiration Medal on behalf of arXiv at the 2025 European Astronomical Society Annual Meeting. Ralph’s vision for arXiv was to support science while creating a community of respect, support, and inclusiveness. All work that promoted this vision was equally important work to Ralph, and treated by him with the same care.

    “I joined arXiv shortly after Ralph,” said Ramin Zabih, Interim CEO of arXiv. “I was fortunate to work with Ralph during the entire four years of my tenure at arXiv. Ralph was remarkably dedicated to arXiv’s mission. I am confident that Ralph’s vision for arXiv as an inclusive scientific community will be carried on by arXiv’s staff. We were all fortunate to have him as a colleague, and he will be deeply missed.”

    We will miss Ralph not only for his research accomplishments and contributions to astronomy and to arXiv (which were many) but also for his enthusiasm and passion for education, popularising science, and sharing the mysteries of the cosmos (and how they might be untangled) with the world at large.

    Ralph didn’t reserve his time teaching to only students who would be dedicating their academic careers to physics. In addition to giving popular public lectures, Ralph also found it worthy to teach students with no background in natural sciences at all, to develop courses for Bachelors and Masters programs, and to work tirelessly to include students from all walks of life and levels of privilege in the wonder of learning and caring about the universe and what it can show us.

    Science, no matter the subject or goal, is at its core a human endeavor. Ralph, even as a great intellect and researcher, knew the human side of science was just as important as the results. He was open with advice and wisdom not only to nascent astronomers or students in his charge, but anyone he encountered – new hires at arXiv were quickly and warmly greeted by Ralph, despite his busy schedule, with an invitation to talk about arXiv, research, or anything at all.

    Ralph’s infectious enthusiasm and earnest curiosity made him adept at breaking down barriers, which made him a natural fit for the many things he put his hand to, but especially open access causes, scientific engagement, and to arXiv, where breaking down barriers to research access is paramount.

    Thank you, Ralph, for your generosity with arXiv and with the scientific community at large – you are already missed by all of us. Our thoughts are especially with Ralph’s family and loved ones at this difficult time.

    Exclusive: News outlet killed story on plagiarism allegations after Cambridge prof hired law firm

    Jason Arday

    In September 2025, Times Higher Education had a bombshell story in the works: A star sociology professor at the University of Cambridge had allegedly plagiarized parts of his Ph.D. thesis and several journal articles. In one of the articles, the borrowed text appeared in quotes from subjects the professor, Jason Arday, claimed to have interviewed himself. 

    But after Arday enlisted Carter-Ruck, one of the United Kingdom’s leading defamation law firms, to send Times Higher Education a letter, the publication spiked the story, according to three sources who spoke to Retraction Watch.

    One of the sources was David Sanders, a biochemist at Purdue University in Indiana, and a sleuth who contributes to the Times Higher Education on topics related to academic misconduct. He reviewed a 63-page dossier of text comparisons prepared by the publication last year and came back with his own findings. “There is no question that there is extensive plagiarism,” Sanders told us.

    The allegations against Arday finally emerged last week following a Substack post from Nathan Cofnas, a philosopher at the University of Ghent in Belgium. Cofnas, who is known for his controversial writings on racial differences, independently identified dozens of passages in Arday’s 2015 dissertation that were taken nearly verbatim from the 2009 dissertation of Paula Zwozdiak-Myers, a reader at Brunel University of London. Cofnas says he learned about the unpublished Times Higher Education story only after he began working on his own piece about Arday.

    Arday is known for his work on topics related to race, inequality and education. His interest was more than scholarly: From humble beginnings as a nonverbal autistic kid in South London, he became, at age 37, the youngest Black person appointed to a professorship at Cambridge.

    That achievement was covered by the BBC, CBS News, and Good Morning America. He is listed as a collaborator on more than $10 million in recent grants from two U.K. funding agencies, U.K. Research and Innovation and the National Institute for Health and Care Research.

    Arday’s achievements have not been limited to academia. He has said he has raised more than $8 million for charity and performed impressive athletic feats, including running 30 marathons in 35 days. His forthcoming memoir, Great and Unfortunate Things, is slated to be published next month by Simon & Schuster.

    Arday did not respond to three requests for comment sent to his Cambridge email address, but during a plenary he gave at the British Sociological Association conference in Edinburgh this April, he spoke vividly about recent attacks on Black academics.

    “You get the email and they say you conducted academic misconduct. And you think to yourself, ‘Well this is incredible. I’m on the back foot here. How do I defend myself against this machine?” he said. “The instruments that are designed to protect academic integrity, designed to protect our wellbeing and our intellectual property, are weaponised.”

    A spokesperson for Cambridge told Retraction Watch that Arday was a “victim of a vile campaign to undermine his credibility” and that he had been cleared of “allegations of plagiarism regarding his thesis and journal publications.”

    Carter-Ruck and Times Higher Education did not respond to emailed requests for comment. 

    In interviews, Arday has said that his path to academic publishing was a rocky one, in part, because he only learned to read and write at the age of 18. “The peer review process was so cruel, it was almost funny, but I treated it as a learning experience and, perversely, began to enjoy it,” he told the BBC in 2023. 

    Within months of the media attention around his appointment at Cambridge, questions began surfacing about the caliber of that work. On May 4, 2023, Dave Harris, an emeritus professor at Plymouth Marjon University in England, sent Arday a lengthy email. He explained that he had been posting summaries evaluating articles related to critical race theory on his personal website, and he wanted to clear up a few things about Arday’s own work, including a 2018 study in the journal Social Sciences.

    In the study, Arday said he conducted focus groups with 32 Black and minority university students in April 2017 to understand the mental health issues they were facing. Although Arday wrote he obtained “informed consent” for the interviews, the article did not include a statement indicating whether his study was approved by an ethics board, as was required by the journal at the time.

    Harris wanted to know if Arday could explain some “puzzling similarities” between sentences in that article and a BMJ Open study from epidemiologist Anjum Memon about mental health services in Black and minority communities. Arday ignored his first email, but when Harris followed up several days later, Arday was blunt. “Instead of spending your time trying to dismantle racism and ableism, you have spent what I assume is hours combing through my work looking for mistakes,” he wrote. Arday told Harris not to contact him again: “Anything further from you will be considered bullying and harassment.”

    Harris soon recruited a colleague, Martyn Hammersley, a sociologist at Open University, to review the Social Sciences article himself, and he, too, was concerned. On May 19, 2023, Hammersley sent an email to Hilary Cremin, the Head of the Faculty of Education at Cambridge, and to Rhys Morgan, the university’s Head of Policy, Governance, and Integrity:

    There is substantial plagiarism from one source, and some from another. It is also the case that a considerable portion of the article follows the structure of the paper by Memon et al: even where their words are not used exactly, these are paraphrased (though also elaborated). It even looks as if a couple of the quotations from informants may have been derived from Memon et al. Both of these articles are cited by Arday, but the quotes from them are unattributed.

    In a response dated June 21, 2023, Cremin wrote that Arday had agreed “to take the article down from the journal online platform in order to address these concerns” but Arday had the department’s “full support.” Morgan, meanwhile, noted that because the alleged plagiarism occurred prior to his arrival at Cambridge, “we do not consider it within our remit to investigate the paper to which you refer.” (Morgan did not respond to our request for comment, and our email to Cremin received an out-of-office response.)

    Six months later, in December 2023, Social Sciences published a correction, adding new citations to make it “clear where ideas were adapted from past research.” A second article, published in Educational Philosophy and Theory, was corrected the following year for text overlap with the Memon paper and other publications.

    But the Social Sciences correction notice glossed over the most significant post-publication changes Arday actually made to the text. He removed claims that he analyzed the transcripts from interview subjects with “an iterative coding scheme using NVivo qualitative data analysis software” and had a second researcher “independently read the scripts” and code the data to “enhance the validity of emerging themes and claims.” Instead, he said, he recorded the interviews, transcribed them verbatim and identified salient themes through “review and reflection.”

    In addition, Arday changed or removed quotes that had previously been attributed to his interview transcripts. Out of 31 quotes in the original manuscript, he trimmed or reworded 12 of them, and cut 6 of them entirely. Some of the original quotes appear to have been patched together from subjects in Memon’s BMJ Open paper. 

    Arday numbered his participants from 1 to 32, but there were actually two 13s: One of them he described as a Black female undergraduate and the other as a Black male undergraduate. The Black male undergraduate, according to Arday, viewed clinicians as being instruments of oppression:

    Let’s face it … with the stroke of a pen you could be sectioned or sent away for some counseling. Or … have administered some ridiculous dose of whatever kind of medicine.

    A similar quote about clinicians appears in Memon et al. attributed to Male D in focus group 1:

    With the stroke of a pen to decide whether you are going to be sectioned or whether you are going to be sent away for some counselling. Or … administered some really, really high doses of whatever kind of medicine.

    The Black female undergraduate in Arday’s study expressed similar distrust toward the healthcare system:

    Within African and West Indian communities … people do not trust psychiatrists in particular.

    This sentiment, too, appears to come from Male D in Memon et al: 

    In the West Indian community … people do not trust psychiatrists particularly.

    In the corrected version of Arday’s paper, the quote from the female 13 has been trimmed of the overlap, while the quote from the male 13 has been cut entirely. None of this is described clearly in the correction notice.

    “Bloody awful work creeps in when standards are relaxed … in ‘special editions,’ often devoted to ‘activist’ issues like racial inequality,” Harris told us. 

    The unpublished article in Times Higher Education was bylined by Jack Grove, who has written extensively about the challenges faced by minorities in U.K. institutions, according to Harris, Hammersley and Sanders. 

    After Grove sent Arday a detailed email in September 2025 offering him an opportunity to respond to the allegations, Times Higher Education received a letter from Carter-Ruck, a law firm known for defending clients ranging from the Church of Scientology to the Russian oligarch Boris Berezovsky. 

    Private Eye, a British satire magazine that once tangled with the firm, refers to them as Carter-Fuck.

    In its letter, the firm wrote that the allegations were false and they would damage Arday’s reputation. The publication subsequently killed the story. 

    At the British Sociologists Association meeting, Arday said that he and his colleagues needed to create “their own playbook to combat” the attacks on Black scholars. “The ultimate tragedy is not the oppression and cruelty by the bad people, but the silence of good people,” he said. “Darkness cannot drive out darkness, only light can do that.”

    Cofnas told us he first began looking into Arday’s case on June 13 of this year, when he was contacted by a faculty member at Cambridge who was disappointed by the university’s wan response to the Arday situation. In 2024, Cambridge’s Emmanuel College cut ties with him just two months after he aired his controversial views on race and the meritocracy on his Substack.

    In his new post, Cofnas alleged that Arday was hired “to advance the cause of racial diversity” and wrote, “by hiring Jason Arday and refusing to investigate credible accusations against him, isn’t Cambridge making a statement that the only way to have black professors is to abandon all standards?”

    Sanders said that what happened at Cambridge is part of a broader phenomenon that has nothing to do with race, which is that journals and universities across the board “do not regard plagiarism by researchers as actionable.” While he abhors “the singling out for investigation of particular members of the academic community because of their ethnicity,” he said that “sometimes the allegations are true, and they need to be addressed fully.”

    In one of his follow-up emails to Cambridge, Hammersley had noted that he hoped there had been “a systematic investigation of Professor Arday’s other publications to check for plagiarism … since these were presumably a major part of the basis on which he was appointed at Cambridge.”

    He told us he believes that Cofnas’s Substack post was just about the worst place for them to emerge. “It’s an unfortunate situation because it could have been dealt with by Cambridge,” he said. “The result of that is that it means that the only people who will take it up are the people on the radical right.”
